St Josephine BakhitaFrom a slave market in Sudan to the door of a convent in Schio

St Josephine Bakhita (c. 1910) · CC0, via Wikimedia Commons

Livedc. 1869–1947, Darfur–Schio
Feast day8 February
RecognisedCanonised 1 October 2000 (John Paul II)
PlaceSchio, Italy

Her story

She was born around 1869 in a village in Darfur, in what is now Sudan, of the Daju people. She was kidnapped as a child by slave traders. She forgot the name her parents had given her. The traders called her Bakhita, Arabic for "fortunate", which was not a kindness. She was sold and sold again in the markets of El Obeid and Khartoum. One owner, a Turkish general, had her scarred: more than a hundred cuts, salt rubbed in so the pattern would stay. She was twelve, or near it.

A change of owners brought her to Callisto Legnani, the Italian vice-consul in Khartoum, who bought her and did not beat her. When the Mahdist war forced him to leave, she asked to go with him. In 1884 or 1885 she landed in Italy. Legnani's friends, the Michieli family of Zianigo, near Venice, took her as nurse to their daughter Alice, called Mimmina. For a while she was back in a house that belonged to someone else, only the sea was different.

In 1888 the Michielis left Alice and Bakhita with the Canossian Sisters in Venice for nine months, and Bakhita heard the Christian story as a story about herself. She asked to be baptised. When the Michielis came back and prepared to take her and the child to Africa, she refused to go. The sisters sheltered her. The Patriarch of Venice and an Italian court were drawn in. Slavery had no legal standing in Italy. In 1889 she was declared free. On 9 January 1890 the patriarch baptised her, confirmed her and gave her Communion: Josephine Margaret Fortunata. Fortunata is Bakhita, said in Italian, this time as a baptismal name and not a slave's.

She entered the Canossians in 1893 and was sent to the convent at Schio, in the province of Vicenza. For nearly fifty years she was cook, sacristan and, above all, the sister at the door. The town called her la madre moretta, the little dark mother. Children were less delicate. She did not seem to mind. During the Second World War the town treated the convent as a safe house, and treated her as the reason. She died there on 8 February 1947.

She said, late in life, that if she met the men who kidnapped and cut her, she would kneel and kiss their hands, because if that had not happened she would not have become a Christian. It is a sentence that can be misused. She was not saying the market was a kindness. She was saying what she had done with it.

Path to sainthood

John Paul II canonised her on 1 October 2000, in the same ceremony as the Chinese martyrs and others. Her optional memorial is 8 February. She is honoured as a patron of Sudan and of victims of human trafficking.

Patronages

Sudan, South Sudan in popular devotion, and victims of slavery and trafficking. Also the diocese of Schio's poor, in practice, for half a century before any decree.

Trivia

  • The photograph from about 1910 shows her in the Canossian habit, hands on a table, a crucifix beside her. It is the face Schio knew.
  • Her body rests in the convent church at Schio, in a glass shrine.
  • "Fortunate" was the kidnappers' name. She kept it. The baptism added Josephine, for the foster father of Jesus, which fitted a woman whose own name had been taken.
