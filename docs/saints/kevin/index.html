Kevin is the saint of a particular valley. Irish speakers called him Cóemgen, later Caoimhín, a name that means fair-born or fair-begotten. English turned that into Kevin. The valley is Glendalough, Gleann Dá Loch, the glen of two lakes, in the Wicklow Mountains. He is remembered as the hermit who would not move his hand while a blackbird nested in it, and as the founder of a monastery that became one of the great religious cities of early Ireland.

What can be said as history. A man named Cóemgen founded a monastery at Glendalough in the sixth century, and the Irish annals place his death in 618. The community lasted for centuries. It had abbots, it was burned and rebuilt, it became the seat of a diocese, and in the twelfth century its best-known son was Laurence O'Toole. The stone churches and the round tower that visitors walk among today are medieval, centuries after Kevin. Nothing standing can be shown to be a building he himself put up.

What is tradition. Almost everything more personal than that comes from Lives written long afterwards. The fullest Latin Life is generally placed in the tenth or eleventh century. Three Irish Lives are later still. James Ussher's birth year of 498, the age of 120, the otter, the doe, the nettles, and the blackbird are hagiography and devotion. They tell what later monks and pilgrims wanted Kevin to mean. They are not a diary.

How to read a Life this late

There is no writing from Kevin's own lifetime. James F. Kenney, in The Sources for the Early History of Ireland (1929), dated the Latin Life to the tenth or eleventh century and noted that the Irish texts are later and not simply translations of it. Charles Plummer printed the Latin Life in 1910 from the Codex Kilkenniensis in Marsh's Library, with shorter copies in the Codex Salmanticensis and in the Bodleian. He printed the Irish Lives in 1922. One of those Irish texts claims a monk named Solomon, a disciple, as its author. Plummer did not think the language could belong to a man who had known Kevin.

Pádraig Ó Riain's Dictionary of Irish Saints is blunt in the summary that Irish writers now repeat: no full critical edition and evaluation of these Lives has settled them, so what we have is tradition. That is the rule of this page. When a sentence comes from a Life, a poem, or a later fair-day, it is marked as tradition. When a wall, a lake, or an opening hour can be checked, the source is named.

The Catholic Encyclopedia of 1913 already treated the birth date as "very obscure" and did not turn the animal stories into history. This page follows that caution, and then stays with the stories, because the stories are how Ireland actually remembered him.

The valley of two lakes

Glendalough is in County Wicklow, in the Wicklow Mountains, south of Dublin and inland from the east coast. The village of Laragh sits at the mouth of the glen. The name is exact. Two lakes lie on the floor of a U-shaped valley that the ice cut. The Office of Public Works, in its schools guide to the site, describes that valley as glacial, cut in granite and mica-schist, with the Glenealo river and the Glendasan river running through it.

Picture the glen with west on your left, the way the schematic below is drawn. The upper lake is the larger sheet of water, higher in the valley, under the steep sides of the mountains. The lower lake is smaller, further east, and more wooded. The Glenealo comes down from the waterfall at the western head of the upper lake, passes through both lakes, and east of the lower lake meets the Glendasan, which has come down from the Wicklow Gap. On the tongue of land at that meeting of the waters is the main group of ruins, the place now called the monastic city.

The ridge to the north of the lakes is Camaderry. The crags to the south are Lugduff. Between them the valley is a long green floor: oak woods, the two lakes, the ruins, and, further up, the scars of later lead mining. It is beautiful, and it is also a mountain place. Paths close after storms. The cliff above the upper lake has taken more rescues than any other spot in the national park. The geography on this page is for understanding and for the walks the park actually waymarks.

A name, and a death we can date

The Catholic Encyclopedia, following the older Irish writers, calls him son of Coemlog and Coemell, names that are themselves forms of the same "fair" word as his own. Spellings wander from manuscript to manuscript: Caomhlugh and Caoimheall are among them. The Lives give him a royal Leinster pedigree and place his people, the Dál Messin Corb, in the east of the province. Later scholars have argued about whether that means the coastal side of Wicklow or the country west of the mountains. The argument matters for which road he walked. It does not change the valley where he stayed.

The birth year 498 is not a record from the sixth century. The Lives say he lived 120 years. Archbishop James Ussher, the same chronologer who dated the Creation to 4004 BC, subtracted 120 from a death in 618 and printed 498. A lifespan of 120 is a hagiographer's way of saying that a holy man grew very old. It is not a birthday. A birth sometime in the sixth century, and a death at the beginning of the seventh, is as tight as the evidence honestly gets.

The death is firmer. The Catholic Encyclopedia gives 3 June 618, and the Irish annals are the reason. The Annals of Ulster enter the rest, the quies, of Cóemgen, abbot of Glenn dá Locha, under 618. Other annal collections do not always agree to the year, so 618 is the best early date rather than a modern certificate. The day, 3 June, is the feast the calendars kept. The Encyclopedia adds that he belonged to the "second order" of Irish saints, the generation of monastic founders after Patrick, and that he was probably never a bishop. Glendalough became a diocese later. That was the monastery's afterlife, not his office.

The life the monks told

Read this section as a story the monastery told about its founder. The outline is shared, more or less, by the Latin Life and the Irish ones. The miracles inside it are the point of a Life. They are not minutes of a meeting.

A child is carried to a priest named Cronan for baptism. Someone, understood to be an angel, breathes on the boy, blesses him, and names him Cóemgen, fair-born. Cronan accepts the name: this child will be beautiful, and the name will say so. It is a baptism legend of a kind Irish Lives love. It explains a name. It does not document a parish register.

The boy is put with three holy elders. Their names are given as forms of Eoghan, Lochan, and Enna, and the place is only "the cell of the monks," somewhere in Cualu, the country between the mountains and the sea south of Dublin. Later writers guessed Kilnamanagh near Tallaght, or other cells with similar names. Liam Price, the Wicklow placename scholar, pulled those guesses apart. The site of the school is not known. One strand of tradition says an Eoghan who was Kevin's uncle taught him the psalms there and that the boy served as cellarer. Hold that as family legend, not as a located school you can visit.

The woman and the nettles. This is the story in the Latin Life, and it belongs to the school days, not to the cliff at Glendalough. A young woman will not leave him alone. He runs into the woods, strips, and rolls in a bed of nettles. When she follows, he dresses, pulls a bunch of the nettles, and strikes her about the face, hands, and feet. She kneels, asks forgiveness, and promises to become a nun. His fellow students arrive and are startled at the scene.

It is a rough little tale, written centuries later, and it is propaganda for the monastic vow as much as it is a memory. John O'Donovan, summarising the Latin Life for the Ordnance Survey in 1839, saw no reason to throw it out as a late invention. He also did not pretend it was gentle. What it is not, is the story of a woman pushed from the cave into the lake. That second tale grew up locally because the cave is a dramatic place for it. It is not in the Latin Life. Thomas Moore put the drowning into a song, "By that Lake, Whose Gloomy Shore," and Daniel Maclise drew it. Some modern walking pages still repeat it as if it were Kevin's act. It is a later legend, and a harsh one. The Latin story ends with repentance, not with a death. Even that earlier story should be read as hagiography about chastity, not as a fact of criminal history.

After this, the Lives say, he wanted the hermit's life and his teachers were slow to let him go. An older monk, also called Kevin, told him to fetch fire. He had nothing to carry it in. The elder said he could carry it in his cloak. He did, and neither cloth nor skin was burned. He asked that the wonder be kept quiet. It was not. The attention drove him out.

He wandered until he found Glendalough, and for a while he lived in the hollow of a tree by the upper lake, on wild fruit and water. Herdsmen noticed that one cow was giving an astonishing amount of milk. They followed her and found her licking the feet of a man in the tree. The Irish prose Life names the cow's owner Dimma, sometimes a stranger from Meath, sometimes the local chief. They carried Kevin out on a litter. He did not want to go, and the tale says the trees lay down to let the litter pass, so he blessed the wood. His old teachers fetched him back. Soon they let him go again, this time to a bishop named Lugid, or Lugaid, who ordained him priest and sent him with a few monks to found a church.

That first foundation is called Cluain Duach. Nobody has identified the townland with confidence. The Lives say the little community did not stay. They moved to Glendalough, to the lower valley where two clear streams meet. That sentence is the nearest the Latin Life comes to a foundation charter, and it fits the tongue of land where the monastic city stands. Kevin settled the monks there, put the place in the care of men he trusted, and went back up the valley, about a mile, to be a hermit again.

Hermit and monastic city

This double life is the real shape of an early Irish monastery, and it is why Kevin is loved as both a solitary and a founder. A monk who wanted a harder rule did not have to leave his community. He withdrew to a diseart, a desert, close enough to belong and far enough to be alone. The Latin Life puts Kevin's desert on a narrow place between the mountain and the lake, with woods and clear streams. That is a very good description of the south shore of the upper lake, where Temple-na-Skellig, the church of the rock, later stood. It is a less good description of the little circular foundation called Kevin's Cell, above the south-east corner of the lake, though that hut is old and belongs to the same hermit country. Scholars still argue the point. The valley itself is not in doubt.

The Lives give him four years in that desert, or seven. He is said to have lived on nettles and sorrel, without fire, some of the time in a hut, some in a bothy of branches on the north shore, some in the cave now called St Kevin's Bed. Wild creatures came to drink from his hands. Angels argued with him. One Lent, an angel told him to leave the cave because the rock above was about to fall. Kevin told the angel to let him finish the fast. On Easter eve the warning came again. He agreed. The tale says the angel took him across the lake dry-shod, and the rock came down behind him. Then the angel led him to the place where he was to rise again, and told him to build a church. The ground was too stony for graves. The angel loosened the stones.

Even the Latin of that scene has been misread. O'Donovan put the church at the east end of the greater lake. The manuscript says the lesser lake. St Mary's, just west of the monastic city, Reefert above the upper lake, and the city itself have all been claimed as the spot. The honest conclusion is the dull one. The story is an etiology. It plants Kevin's authority on a church the writer cared about. It does not identify a foundation trench.

What the history can use is the pattern underneath the marvels. He came with companions, not as a man who never saw another soul. He founded a community at the meeting of the rivers. He kept a hermitage by the upper lake. Disciples gathered until the glen was a monastic town. The Catholic Encyclopedia's phrase is the traditional one: so many followers that Glendalough became a city in the desert. The wattle huts of that town are gone. The reputation is what the annals inherit, in the names of abbots and the record of fires.

Why people loved him

The affection is not an accident of a pretty valley, though the valley helps. Three things keep meeting in the way he is told.

He would not stay famous if he could be hidden, and he would not stay hidden if people needed a father in God. The tree, the return to his teachers, the ordination, the short-lived Cluain Duach, the monastery at the rivers, the mile back upstream to the desert: the plot is a man tugged between solitude and the crowd, who keeps choosing both. Irish monasticism recognised that rhythm. So did later pilgrims, who came to a tomb and also to a cave.

He is the saint who does not shake the bird off. Gentleness to animals is a commonplace of the desert fathers, and Irish writers knew those stories. Kevin's versions are local and specific: a cow, an otter, a doe, a blackbird, beasts drinking from his hand. By the late twelfth century Gerald of Wales could say that pictures of Kevin in Ireland showed a blackbird on the open palm. The iconography had already settled. People wanted a saint whose holiness made room for a nesting bird.

And the place became a tearmann, a sanctuary, in the practical sense as well as the pious one. Churches in early Ireland held people and goods and cattle. That is one reason they were raided, by Vikings and by Irish neighbours both. The annals of Glendalough are often a list of abbots' deaths and of burnings. The burning is not a contradiction of the love. It is what a rich, holy, well-known place suffered, and what devotees rebuilt. For a thousand years after him the glen stayed a destination: a school, a diocese, a pattern day, a walk.

The Archdiocese of Dublin still names him among its patrons. The older and more official face of the diocese is Laurence O'Toole, who had been abbot here before he was archbishop. Kevin is the founder behind that line. His feast is not on the short universal calendar used for the feast table elsewhere on this site. In Ireland, 3 June is his day.

The blackbird

This is the story people mean when they say his name, so it should be told properly, and with its variants left visible.

The classic medieval telling is in Gerald of Wales, the Topographia Hibernica, written in the late twelfth century, a good five centuries after Kevin's death. Gerald has Kevin keep Lent alone in a small hut that keeps off only the sun and the rain. He is reading and praying. As he lifts his hand toward heaven he puts it out through the window. A blackbird lands on the hand, treats it as a nest, and lays her eggs. Kevin, moved and patient, neither closes the hand nor draws it back. He holds it out, without giving up, until the young are hatched. Gerald adds the devotional consequence: in memory of this, the images of Saint Kevin throughout Ireland show a blackbird in the outstretched hand.

The Irish Life tells it inside the Lent he kept every year in a wattled pen, with a grey flagstone for a bed and, the text says, the music of the angels for food. One Lent a blackbird comes from the wood, hops onto the palm he is holding out as he lies on the stone, and he will not shift the hand. She builds, and she hatches the brood. An angel comes and tells him the penance is over, that he should go back to people. Kevin answers, in the sense of the verse the Life quotes, that the pain of his hand under the blackbird is little beside the pain of Christ's hands, side, and feet. The stillness is not a nature anecdote only. It is cross-vigil, crois-fhigill, the prayer with arms out in the shape of the Cross, set next to the Passion on purpose.

A third staging appears in the Office of Public Works leaflet used with school groups at the site. There Kevin is standing in the upper lake, praying with his hands out, and the blackbird nests while he stays in the water. That is how the story is sometimes taught on the ground today. It is a simplification. It is not Gerald's hut, and it is not the flagstone pen.

What to do with the differences. The window, the flagstone, and the lake cannot all be the same afternoon. Nobody who knew Kevin wrote the scene down. A blackbird's nest, from building to fledging, is a matter of weeks, which is why the story feels both lovely and impossible. The people who told it knew that. The monk who shaped the Irish verse was honouring a severity of prayer, not filing a naturalist's note. Gerald was collecting wonders for a book about Ireland, and he was also reporting how Kevin was already pictured.

The devotional sense, shared by the tellings, is steady. A small living creature trusts him. He will not betray the trust to save himself discomfort. He measures that discomfort against what Christ bore. You can love the story, and paint it, and walk the valley because of it, without pretending a witness minuted the eggs.

In 1996 Seamus Heaney retold it in the poem "St Kevin and the Blackbird," published in The Spirit Level. The poem is still in copyright, so it is not quoted here. It is the modern door through which many readers outside Ireland meet the legend. The medieval door is Gerald, and the Irish Life.

Other stories, clearly marked

The otter. The Lives give him an otter as a servant of his solitude. In the telling long attached to Glendalough, the animal brought him salmon. In another, he dropped his psalter in the lake and the otter brought it back unharmed. Both are kin to the desert stories in which a beast serves a hermit. They are not zoology, and they are not two verified incidents. They are the same idea told twice: the water looked after the man who had given himself to the water.

The doe and the Deer Stone. The Irish prose Life tells of a local chief named Colman who had put away his first wife. She was blamed for the deaths of his sons by the second wife. To save the latest baby he gave the child to Kevin. A monastery is an awkward nursery. A white doe came and let herself be milked each day into a hollow stone at the gate. The child grew up Kevin's man. The wife passed into local legend as the Hag of Glendalough. The hollow stone is still pointed out: a bullaun called the Deer Stone, near the river by the monastic city, and the nearby crossing was called the Bridge of the Doe. The stone is real. Hollowed stones are common on Irish church sites, and they collected water. The doe is the story that made this one Kevin's.

The boar is left out on purpose. Some modern retellings add a hunted boar that runs into his oratory, and hunters who find Kevin praying with birds on his shoulders and call off the chase. It is a sanctuary story of a familiar type. It is not told here as if it had a chapter and verse in the Latin Life that this page has been able to pin down.

Devotees after Kevin

The monastery did not freeze in the sixth century. The annals, from at least the later eighth century, record fires and plunder. Some raids are blamed on the Vikings, some on Irish rivals, some on accident. A church enclosure that sheltered people and cattle was a target. Timber roofs and wattle houses burned even when stone walls stood. Manuscripts burned with them. Very little writing that can be proved to come from the Glendalough scriptorium survives. The fame of the school is real in the tradition and thin in the library catalogues. That gap is itself part of the history.

In the twelfth century the reform of the Irish church reached the glen. Stone churches were rebuilt or enlarged. St Saviour's, a mile down the valley, carries Irish Romanesque carving of that century on its chancel arch and east window, and it is often linked with the years of Laurence O'Toole, though the carving should not be treated as his signature. Laurence, Lorcán Ua Tuathail, was abbot of Glendalough and then archbishop of Dublin, the saint who stands at the join between the old monastic city and the diocese. An archaeology guide to the upper lake notes that St Kevin's Bed was later a retreat for him while he was archbishop. The cave's story did not stop with Kevin.

At the Synod of Kells in 1152 Dublin was made an archbishopric. The papal legate would have folded Glendalough into it. Irish opposition kept the two sees apart for a time. In the early thirteenth century, after the Norman arrival in Leinster, the diocese of Glendalough was joined to Dublin. The mountains remained the country of the O'Tooles and the O'Byrnes. Pilgrimage did not stop because a bishop's title moved.

The pattern, the patron's festival, was kept on 3 June. It was a religious round of the churches and also a great open-air gathering. The historian W. E. H. Lecky records the sheriff breaking one up as a riotous assembly in 1715. Nineteenth-century writers, William Wilde among them, described crowds camping in the ruins, drink, and faction fighting, until the parish clergy suppressed the rowdier pattern in the earlier nineteenth century. One custom Wilde and others record is a dawn walk up the Glendasan with sickly children, to dip them in Kevin's Keeve, a pool whose exact place is now lost. The pattern's reputation for helping women in childbirth was tied, in local talk, to a visit to the Bed. That claim is folklore about the cave, not a reason to climb to it.

What the pattern looked like to a painter is still on the wall of a museum. Around the early nineteenth century Joseph Peacock painted The Patron, or The Festival of Saint Kevin at the Seven Churches, Glendalough. It is not a survey. The mountains are a Romantic stage, and the round tower is set in the glen like a monument in a fair. It is, even so, a picture of devotees: tents, horses, carts, a crowd, a holy place turned into a people's day. The seven churches are the traditional count of the valley's churches, a pilgrim's itinerary rather than a census that never changes.

The state's care of the ruins begins in the later nineteenth century, after the Irish Church Act of 1869 vested ancient church sites in the Commissioners of Public Works. Walls were rebuilt, the round tower's cap was restored, and St Saviour's was pieced back from fallen stones. Some of that work is itself now part of what you see. It is repair, not Kevin's masonry.

A timeline

Sixth century

Kevin's lifetime. Birth year unknown. The figure 498 is Ussher's arithmetic from a legendary age of 120. Tradition: schooling in Cualu, ordination by Bishop Lugid, a short stay at Cluain Duach, then the monastery at the meeting of the rivers and a hermitage by the upper lake.

3 June 618

The Annals of Ulster record the death of Cóemgen, abbot of Glenn dá Locha. The day becomes his feast. He is a pre-Congregation saint, recognised by long public veneration, not by a modern canonisation process.

Eighth century and after

The annals name later abbots and record burnings and raids, by Vikings and by Irish neighbours. The timber and wattle of a monastic town was what burned. Stone came and went.

Tenth to twelfth centuries

Most of the stone that still stands belongs to this long medieval rebuilding: the round tower, the cathedral, St Kevin's Church, Reefert, the gateway. Dates of individual walls are argued. None of them is a documented building of Kevin's own hands. The Latin Life is written in this same broad period, centuries after him.

Twelfth century

Laurence O'Toole is abbot, then archbishop of Dublin. St Saviour's carries Romanesque carving of this era. The Bed is remembered as one of Laurence's retreats. In 1152 the Synod of Kells makes Dublin an archbishopric and leaves Glendalough beside it for a time.

Early thirteenth century

The diocese of Glendalough is joined to Dublin. Pilgrimage continues in the mountains.

3 June, for centuries

The pattern. A religious round of the churches, and a fair that could turn riotous. Suppressed in its rowdy form in the earlier nineteenth century. Peacock paints the crowd.

Later nineteenth century

The ruins pass to the Board of Works. Repairs, including the tower's cap and the gathering-up of St Saviour's, shape the site visitors see.

Now

The monastic city is in the care of the Office of Public Works, with a visitor centre. The lakes and the trails are Wicklow Mountains National Park. St Kevin's Way is a waymarked pilgrim path from Hollywood. His feast is still 3 June, and the Archdiocese of Dublin still names him among its patrons.

What stands in the valley

Walk the monastic city and you are walking a medieval town that inherited Kevin's name, not a sixth-century building site. The distinction is worth keeping in your eye. It does not make the place less his. It makes the stones honest.

The gateway. You come in from the road through a stone gatehouse, a rare survival of a monastic entrance. A later iron gate stands beside the approach. The arch is the threshold of the city. It is not a round tower, and it never had one on top of it, whatever older guesses said.

The round tower. About thirty metres high in the usual guidebook measure, with a doorway well above ground level, once reached by a ladder. The conical cap was reconstructed in the nineteenth-century works from the fallen stone. Round towers served as bell-towers, and also as stores and refuges. This one is the landmark of the glen. It is not Kevin's.

The cathedral. The largest church, a nave of early Irish type with a chancel added later, probably in the twelfth century. The east window and chancel arch were rebuilt in the nineteenth century from stones found on the site. Antae, the projections of the side walls past the gables, still show. Part of the building is repair upon repair. Calling any one stone "Kevin's cathedral" is a guess the masonry will not support.

St Kevin's Church, called the Kitchen. The nickname comes from the small round belfry on the gable, which looks like a chimney. It is a stone-roofed church, with a vault and a croft between vault and roof, kin to St Columba's House at Kells. A chancel was added and later mostly taken down. A sacristy remains. The building is medieval. Petrie once wondered whether Kevin had lived in it. The architecture is too late for that. It is the best roofed interior among the churches, and the belfry is the joke that stuck.

St Kevin's Cross, the Priests' House, St Kieran's, St Mary's, Trinity. In the graveyard a plain granite ringed cross, St Kevin's Cross, stands without the crowded biblical panels of a scripture cross such as those at Monasterboice. Its plainness is its character. Do not import another site's carving and call it his. The Priests' House is a small stone building whose first purpose is uncertain. The name comes from priests buried nearby in the eighteenth century. A carved fragment over one opening shows part of a figural scene. It is medieval sculpture, reset, and scholars argue about the reconstruction. St Kieran's is a small church inside the city, linked by tradition to Ciarán of Clonmacnoise. The tradition is a friendship story. The masonry is not a documented sixth-century monument. St Mary's, or Our Lady's, a little west of the city, has an early nave and a very fine lintelled granite doorway. Some have thought it among the oldest churches in the glen, and local tradition has put Kevin's burial there. Trinity Church, toward Laragh, is another early nave-and-chancel, once carrying a round tower that fell in a storm in 1818.

Reefert. Above the south-east of the upper lake, among trees and early grave slabs, Reefert Church is roofless and compact. The name is usually explained as rígh-feart, the burial-place of kings. If the kings were the O'Tooles, that burial custom cannot be older than their move into Wicklow in the later twelfth century. The church itself is medieval, with a chancel arch still standing. It is one of the loveliest ruins in the valley, and it belongs to the hermit side of the glen rather than to the city at the rivers. It is not proved to be Kevin's grave.

St Kevin's Cell. A low circular foundation, the stance of a beehive hut, above the south-east corner of the upper lake, with a view over the water. This is as close as the ground gets to the hermit's house. The hut itself is gone. The National Park's bronze route to it has had its loop closed by storm damage. The park says you can still walk out to the cell by the bronze arrows and return the same way, and it suggests the short detour to Reefert. Check the notice at the Information Office before you trust that sentence. Storms change it.

St Kevin's Bed, and Temple-na-Skellig. These two need a different kind of sentence. The Bed is a small man-made cave in the cliff on the south side of the upper lake, about ten metres above the water. It is too low to stand in. Heritage Ireland says Kevin is said to have lived and prayed there, and that it may in fact be a prehistoric burial place, older than he is. Temple-na-Skellig, the church of the rock, is the most remote church in the glen, on the south shore. Françoise Henry's excavations in the 1950s found people living on the platforms there in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, and possibly in the ninth. That is medieval occupation of a holy ledge. It is not a preserved wattle cell of the 500s.

Do not climb to the Bed, and do not try to reach Temple-na-Skellig. Both are unsafe. In 2000 the Minister for Arts and Heritage told the Dáil that the Bed is highly unsuitable for public access, that the way up is a brittle cliff about ten metres above the lake, and that the spot had needed more rescues than any other place in Wicklow Mountains National Park. Fences and warning signs had not stopped people. The minister was not going to build a route. An Archaeology Ireland guide says the same in fewer words: Temple-na-Skellig and St Kevin's Bed cannot be safely accessed. See the cave from the Miners' Road on the north shore. The photograph below was taken from across the water. It is there so you can recognise the cliff, not so you can aim at it.

St Saviour's stands about a mile downstream, on the south bank, and rewards the walk. The Romanesque carving is the valley's richest sculpture. It is twelfth-century work, the last flowering of that Irish manner before the great continental churches took over. It is not a portrait of Kevin, and it is not a page from the Book of Kells. Glendalough's own art is this: plain crosses, a gatehouse, a stone roof, a ringed cross with almost no figures, and, at St Saviour's, the knot and the beast of the Romanesque.

Stone, and a painting of the crowd

If you are looking for "Irish art of St Kevin," start with what the valley actually has, and with the way he was pictured.

Gerald's report is the iconographic fact: by the 1180s, Irish images of Kevin carried the blackbird on the open hand. That is his attribute, as a tower is Barbara's or a key is Peter's. Later holy cards and windows repeat it. A modern stained-glass window may be under copyright even when the saint is ancient, so this page does not reproduce twentieth-century glass. Peacock's painting, above, is the public picture of the devotees rather than of the hermit: the people in the glen, the tower as their landmark, the pattern as a festival. It belongs here because it is about this place and this cult. A page of the Book of Kells would not. Kells is another monastery.

The small interlaced ornament between sections of this page is a new drawing for the site, a nod to the habit of interlace. It is not a tracing of a Glendalough stone, and it should not be read as one.

Walking there today

Two public bodies share the glen, and their pages are the ones to trust for hours and paths. The Office of Public Works keeps the monastic city and the visitor centre. Wicklow Mountains National Park keeps the lakes, the woods, and the coloured walking trails. Hours and closures below were read from those sites on 6 November 2026. They change. The footer of this page says the same thing the guides say: confirm before you go.

The visitor centre and the monastic city. Heritage Ireland lists the centre as open all year, closed 23–29 December. From mid-March to 14 October the hours are 09:30–18:00, last admission 17:15. From 15 October to mid-March they are 09:30–17:00, last admission 16:15. Admission is €5 adult, €4 group or senior, €3 child or student, €13 family. Paying to park in the visitor centre car park includes one admission. The Eircode they publish is A98 HC80. The centre is step-free. The historic graveyard is very difficult for wheelchairs. Toilets close at 16:15 in the winter season. A visit to the centre and the city is commonly an hour to an hour and a half, and then the valley is still there.

The park trails. There are nine waymarked routes from the National Park Information Office near the upper lake, plus longer roads that pass through: the Wicklow Way, St Kevin's Way, and the Miners' Way. Large maps are posted outside the office and at the OPW centre. The office is open 10:00–17:30 from March to October, and 09:30–16:30 from November to February, and it sometimes shuts without warning because the staff have gone to a rescue. The park itself is open day and night. Groups of more than fifty people need a permit. Ticks are a real, if uncommon, risk off the paths, and midges can be thick on a still day. The park's own note on this is worth reading before a long walk.

The trails most tied to Kevin, as the park described them:

  • Miners' Road (purple). About 5 km and an hour, out and back, mostly a track, with a climb of about 20 metres. This is the walk on the north side of the upper lake. Halfway along you can see St Kevin's Bed across the water, and the ruined miners' village is at the far end. Sport Ireland's separate listing of a Miners Road Walk gives 2.6 km. The two pages are not measuring the same sentence. Follow the purple arrows and the map at the office, and treat the park's own description as the one for this shore. This is the right place to look at the cave.
  • Green Road (green). About 3 km and 45 minutes, easy, on the flat, with a boardwalk and a view up the valley. The park suggests adding the monastic city. It is the kindest introduction to both lakes and the ruins.
  • Poulanass and St Kevin's Cell (bronze). About 1 km. The loop was closed by storm damage when the park's page was read. Access to the cell was still possible by following the bronze arrows and walking back the same way, with Reefert as a detour. Read the sign on the day.
  • Poulanass waterfall (pink) and the Derrybawn trail (orange) have also been altered by storm damage. The pink path beyond the waterfall had collapsed. The orange trail had been rerouted to avoid the fall. Do not invent a way around a closed section.
  • The Spinc routes (white, red, blue) are steep mountain walks with a cliff-edge boardwalk and serious views. Boots and rain gear. They are not required for Kevin's story. If you go, stay on the boards.

St Kevin's Way is the pilgrim path, not a lap of the lake. Sport Ireland grades it strenuous: 34.3 km, about 580 metres of climbing, one day, linear, from Hollywood to Glendalough, with another start at Valleymount. The two starts meet at Ballinagee Bridge, cross the Wicklow Gap, and follow the Glendasan down into the valley. Dogs are not allowed. Waymarks are a yellow arrow on black. Stretches of the old flagged pilgrim road still show. Pilgrim Paths of Ireland describes the same road as about 30 km and moderate, and asks for boots, rain gear, and care on the road sections, with wet ground in places. The length and the grade disagree because the two publishers are not using one measuring tape. It is a mountain day either way. The Wicklow Gap is the high point. Do not start it late, in a clearing wind, in ordinary shoes.

The Hollywood start is why Kevin is associated with west Wicklow at all. The road is a medieval pilgrim approach to his tomb. Whether he himself first crossed the gap from that side is the theory Liam Price argued, and other readers of the Latin Life have not accepted it. Walk the road as a pilgrim road. You do not have to settle the sixth-century itinerary to be on it.

A grid reference, from Sport Ireland, for the upper-lake information office where several short trails begin: T 111 961. The St Kevin's Way start at Hollywood is N 940 055.

Feast, patrons, and where this was read

Path to sainthood

Kevin is a pre-Congregation saint. The Church recognised him by continuous public veneration, long before the modern process of Servant of God, Venerable, Blessed, and Saint. The Catholic Encyclopedia of 1913 presents him that way and does not record a twentieth-century canonisation. His feast is 3 June, kept in Ireland and named in the tradition of the Roman Martyrology. The Archdiocese of Dublin calls him one of its patron saints. Glendalough itself, and the people who walk there, are the living patronage.

A claim sometimes copied on the internet, that Pius X canonised him on 9 December 1903, is not repeated here. The standard early-twentieth-century encyclopedia article, written after that year, does not mention it.

Patronages

He is bound to Glendalough and to the diocese of Dublin. The blackbird is his attribute in art from at least the twelfth century, which is why people call him a patron of blackbirds. That is devotion growing out of Gerald's story and the images, not a separate decree.

Where to go

The monastic city, the visitor centre, Reefert, the cell if the path is open, and the view of the Bed from the Miners' Road. St Kevin's Way from Hollywood or Valleymount across the Wicklow Gap. Not the cliff.
