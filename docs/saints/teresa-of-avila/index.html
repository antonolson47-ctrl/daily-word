Her story

Picture a winter night on a frozen road in Old Castile. A covered cart creaks along, pulled by mules. Inside, wrapped in a coarse brown habit and a white mantle, sits a woman past sixty, with a fever, a bad arm that was once broken and never set properly, and a heart on fire. She is on her way to found another convent. Behind her lie bishops who said no, town councils that slammed doors, a nuncio who called her a "restless gadabout", and twenty years of her own lukewarm prayer. Ahead of her lies another rented house, another bell to hang, another little chapel where Christ will be honoured day and night.

Her name is Teresa de Ahumada, and the world knows her as St Teresa of Ávila, or as she chose to sign herself, Teresa of Jesus. She was not born a saint. She was charming, talkative, fond of fine clothes and good company, and for nearly two decades she gave God only half her heart. Then God took the other half, and she spent the rest of her life giving it away to anyone who would listen. Her story is good news for every believer who has ever felt that their prayer is dry and their progress slow. God was patient with Teresa for twenty years. Then he set her on fire.

Why the Church honours her

  • She renewed Carmel. She led the reform that became the Discalced ("barefoot") Carmelites: small, poor, enclosed communities of prayer. She founded seventeen convents of nuns across Spain, and with St John of the Cross she brought the same reform to the friars. Today Discalced Carmelite nuns and friars pray on every continent.
  • She is one of the great teachers of prayer. Her definition of prayer is still quoted by the Catechism: prayer is "nothing else than a close sharing between friends; it means taking time frequently to be alone with him who we know loves us".
  • She was a mystic who kept her feet on the ground. She knew visions, raptures and the piercing of her heart by an angel, and she still insisted that "the Lord walks among the pots and pans" and that real holiness is shown in love of neighbour.
  • She wrote spiritual classics. The Book of Her Life, The Way of Perfection, The Interior Castle and The Book of the Foundations are read in every language, and she is counted among the great writers of Spain's Golden Age.
  • She is the first woman Doctor of the Church. On 27 September 1970, Pope Paul VI proclaimed her a Doctor, a teacher for the whole Church.

"Forever, forever, forever"

Teresa was born on 28 March 1515 in Ávila, a city of soldiers and saints on a high, windswept plain of Old Castile, some seventy miles north-west of Madrid. Ávila is still ringed by its great medieval walls: some two and a half kilometres of granite, with eighty-odd towers and nine gates, among the best preserved in Europe. It was a city where everyone knew everyone, and every family had a coat of arms.

Her father, Alonso Sánchez de Cepeda, was a serious, honest man who loved books; her mother, Beatriz de Ahumada, was gentle, often ill, and loved the Rosary and tales of chivalry. There were twelve children in the house, counting her father's children from his first marriage. Part of Teresa's childhood was spent at her mother's estate at Gotarrendura, a village a few miles north of the city, whose old dovecote still stands.

Her favourite brother was Rodrigo, four years older. Together they read the lives of the saints, and the little girl did some simple arithmetic: the martyrs went straight to heaven, and heaven lasts forever. "We settled to go together to the country of the Moors," she remembered, "begging our way for the love of God, that we might be there beheaded." The two children slipped out of the house and set off down the road. Tradition says an uncle caught up with them just outside the walls, near the spot where the stone shrine of the Cuatro Postes now stands, and brought them home to a frantic mother.

What stayed with her was not the adventure but a few words. The children were astonished that the joys of heaven and the pains of hell were everlasting, and they would say it to each other over and over: "For ever, ever, ever." Through those words, she wrote, "our Lord was pleased that I should receive an abiding impression of the way of truth when I was yet a child." When martyrdom proved impossible, the two of them built little hermitages of stones in the orchard. They kept falling down.

When Teresa was about twelve, her mother died. "When I began to understand my loss," she wrote, "I went in my affliction to an image of our Lady, and with many tears implored her to be my mother." It was a child's prayer, and Mary took it seriously. "I have by experience found the royal Virgin help me whenever I recommended myself to her."

The teenage years were less heroic. Teresa was pretty, warm and quick-witted, and people loved being around her. She read romances of knights and ladies by candlelight, took great care over her hair and her perfume, and enjoyed the company of her cousins a little too much. Her worried father sent her, at sixteen, to board with the Augustinian nuns of Our Lady of Grace in Ávila. There a holy nun, María de Briceño, began to win her heart back for God. Illness sent her home, and on the way to recover at her sister's house she stayed with an uncle, Pedro, who had her read good books aloud to him. In the Letters of St Jerome she found the courage she was looking for. She would become a nun.

The Incarnation and the long illness

Her father would not hear of it. So early one morning in November 1535, at twenty, Teresa slipped out of the house again, this time with a different brother, and walked to the Carmelite convent of the Incarnation, just outside the city walls to the north. Leaving her father, she wrote, felt like every bone in her body being wrenched apart. She did not love God enough yet to make it easy. She went anyway, and her father soon gave his blessing.

The Incarnation was a big, lively house of more than a hundred and fifty women. It was not wicked, but it was not quiet: nuns came and went, visitors filled the parlours, and the well-born sisters kept servants and private rooms. Teresa made her profession in 1537, delighted with the life.

Then her body broke down. Within a year she was seriously ill, with fainting fits and heart pains no one could explain. Her father took her to a woman healer at Becedas, in the mountains, whose harsh "cures" nearly killed her. But on the way, her uncle Pedro gave her a little book that changed everything: the Third Spiritual Alphabet of the Franciscan Francisco de Osuna, which taught the "prayer of recollection", gathering the soul inward to be with God. She had found her teacher.

In August 1539, back in Ávila, she fell into a coma so deep that she was taken for dead. "They must have regarded me as dead more than once," she wrote, "for I found afterwards drops of wax on my eyelids." "For a day-and-a-half," she wrote, "the grave was open in my monastery, waiting for my body." Her father refused to let them bury her. She woke, and for some three years afterward she was paralysed, "bent together like a coil of ropes", unable to move more than a finger.

In that helplessness she turned to St Joseph. "I took for my patron and lord the glorious St Joseph," she wrote, "and recommended myself earnestly to him... I cannot call to mind that I have ever asked him at any time for anything which he has not granted." By about 1542 she was walking again, and she never stopped telling people to go to St Joseph. More than half her convents would bear his name.

Twenty years on a stormy sea

Here is the part of Teresa's story that the modern believer most needs to hear. Healed, she did not become a saint. For nearly twenty years she lived a divided life. She prayed, and then she chatted in the parlour for hours. She longed for God, and she loved being admired. "I passed nearly twenty years on this stormy sea," she confessed, "falling and rising, but rising to no good purpose." At one point she was so ashamed of herself that she gave up mental prayer altogether for a year or more, thinking that someone so unfaithful had no right to be alone with God. Later she would call that the most dangerous temptation of her life.

Her father died in 1543, and the Dominican friar who attended him, Vicente Barrón, told her firmly to take up prayer again and never let go. She obeyed, and from then on she never abandoned prayer, however badly it went. Her advice to anyone who is struggling comes out of those years: never stop praying. Even a poor prayer, faithfully kept, is a door God can open.

Before the wounded Christ, 1554

The door opened in Lent of 1554. Teresa was thirty-nine. One day she went into the convent oratory and saw a statue that had been borrowed for an upcoming feast. "It was a representation of Christ most grievously wounded," she wrote, "and so devotional, that the very sight of it... moved me, so well did it show forth that which He suffered for us. So keenly did I feel the evil return I had made for those wounds, that I thought my heart was breaking. I threw myself on the ground beside it, my tears flowing plenteously, and implored Him to strengthen me once for all, so that I might never offend Him any more."

This time she did not get up the same woman. She stopped trusting her own strength and put all her trust in God. Around the same time, the Confessions of St Augustine, newly printed in Spanish, came into her hands. When she reached the scene in the garden in Milan, where Augustine hears a child's voice say "take up and read", she wept: "I thought I saw myself there described." The two great converts, a thousand years apart, became friends on the page.

The angel with the golden spear

What followed is one of the most remarkable chapters in the history of Christian prayer. Teresa began to experience deep states of prayer: quiet, union, rapture. She heard words from the Lord in her soul, and she sensed Christ standing beside her. It frightened her. She was afraid of being deceived, and several confessors told her it was the devil. One told her to make a rude gesture at her visions. She obeyed, and asked Christ to forgive her for it.

Help came from the young Jesuits of Ávila, and from saints. St Francis Borgia reassured her, and in 1560 the aged Franciscan St Peter of Alcántara, thin as "the roots of trees", told her plainly that her experiences were from God. Then came the vision that has made her famous. Sometime around that year, she wrote:

I saw an angel close by me, on my left side, in bodily form... He was not large, but small of stature, and most beautiful, his face burning... I saw in his hand a long spear of gold, and at the iron's point there seemed to be a little fire. He appeared to me to be thrusting it at times into my heart, and to pierce my very entrails; when he drew it out, he seemed to draw them out also, and to leave me all on fire with a great love of God. The pain was so great, that it made me moan; and yet so surpassing was the sweetness of this excessive pain, that I could not wish to be rid of it. The soul is satisfied now with nothing less than God. — Life, chapter 29

The Carmelites call this grace the Transverberation, the piercing of her heart, and keep its feast each year on 26 August. Teresa herself is careful to say that "the pain is not bodily, but spiritual". What she describes is love: God wounding a human heart so that it can no longer be satisfied with anything less than himself.

San José: the first little dovecote

Now Teresa had a fire to share. One evening in 1560, in her cell at the Incarnation, she and a few friends talked about how wonderful it would be to live the Carmelite life as the first hermits of Mount Carmel had: few in number, poor, enclosed, given wholly to prayer. Her young cousin María de Ocampo offered her dowry on the spot. The Lord confirmed it in prayer and asked that the house be dedicated to St Joseph.

The city erupted. Ávila already had too many convents living on alms, people said, and who was this nun to start another? The plan was denounced from the pulpit. Teresa's provincial withdrew his permission. She waited, prayed, and quietly carried on, with her sister Juana and brother-in-law buying a small house as if for themselves. Tradition adds that when a wall of the building fell on her little nephew Gonzalo, Teresa took the lifeless child in her arms and prayed, and he was given back to his mother alive.

On 24 August 1562, the feast of St Bartholomew, Mass was said for the first time in the tiny chapel of San José, and four poor young women received the habit. There was a storm in the city council, and Teresa was ordered back to the Incarnation. But the little convent held, and before long she was allowed to join her daughters. She now called herself Teresa of Jesus, she wore rough sandals, and she later said these were the most restful years of her life. She called her convents "little dovecotes of Our Lady", and her nuns were known for joy as much as for penance. Words often credited to her put it plainly: "A sad nun is a bad nun."

On the roads of Spain

In 1567 the head of the whole Carmelite order, Fr Giovanni Battista Rossi (Rubeo in Spain), visited Ávila, saw San José, and was so moved that he gave her written permission to found as many convents as she could. She was fifty-two, often ill, and had no money. She set out anyway.

For the next fifteen years Teresa was always on the road: in covered carts over frozen meseta, on mules across flooded rivers, in filthy inns where she hung a blanket to make a cloister. She founded at Medina del Campo, Malagón, Valladolid, Toledo, Pastrana, Salamanca, Alba de Tormes, Segovia, Beas, Seville, Caravaca, Villanueva de la Jara, Palencia, Soria and Burgos, and her daughters, under her authority, founded Granada. Often she started with a handful of ducats and a borrowed house. "Teresa and this money are indeed nothing," she is remembered as saying, "but God, Teresa and these ducats suffice."

At Medina del Campo in 1567 she met a small, intense young friar, Juan de Santo Matía. He was thinking of leaving to become a Carthusian. Teresa persuaded him to stay and help her bring the reform to the friars instead. She joked that she had found "a friar and a half" for her plan, because the other recruit was tall and this one barely five feet. On 28 November 1568, in a farmhouse at Duruelo with a skull and two crosses for decoration, the first Discalced friars began their life. The young friar took a new name: John of the Cross.

In 1571 she was sent back, against the will of the nuns, as prioress of her old convent, the Incarnation. The sisters were furious. On the day she took charge, Teresa placed a statue of Our Lady in the prioress's stall, with the keys of the convent in her hands, and sat at her feet. Mary would be prioress; Teresa would serve. She won them over by kindness, and brought in John of the Cross as their confessor. On 18 November 1572, while receiving Communion from his hands, she received the grace she called the spiritual marriage, the deepest union with Christ, who told her that from now on she would care for his honour as his true bride.

The road was not only hard; it was dangerous. Tradition tells of a day when her cart overturned while crossing a river and she was thrown into the cold water. When she complained to the Lord, she heard him say, "This is how I treat my friends." "Yes, my Lord," she answered, "and that is why you have so few!" ⚠ It is a beloved story, and it sounds like her, but it is not in her own writings or in the early biographies; the earliest known version is from 1912. The spirit of it is true: she spoke to God the way a friend speaks to a friend, with complete honesty and complete trust.

From 1575 to 1580 came the hardest years. Tension between the reformed (Discalced) and unreformed (Calced) Carmelites turned bitter. A papal nuncio, Felipe Sega, called her "a restless, disobedient gadabout of a woman". She was ordered to stop founding and confine herself to a convent. John of the Cross was seized in December 1577, locked in a tiny cell in Toledo for nine months, and escaped only by night down a rope of knotted blankets. Teresa wrote letters to everyone, including King Philip II, and she prayed. In 1580 Pope Gregory XIII made the Discalced Carmelites a separate province, and at last there was peace.

Through it all she remained wonderfully human. She loved a good joke, a good letter and a good sermon. She told her nuns that "the Lord walks among the pots and pans", and that obedience in the kitchen was worth more than visions. She was a superb businesswoman, a shrewd judge of character and a devoted friend.

The books: a castle made of diamond

Teresa never meant to be an author. She wrote because her confessors told her to, often at night after a full day, in a rapid hand without crossing anything out. She called herself a "poor woman" without learning. Her books are now treasures of the whole Church.

  • The Book of Her Life (final version 1565): her story, written for her confessors, a confession in the old sense, praising God's mercy. It contains her famous image of prayer as watering a garden: by hauling buckets from a well, by a water-wheel, by a stream, and finally by rain from heaven, when God does all the work.
  • The Way of Perfection (c. 1566): written for her nuns at San José, a practical guide to prayer and community life, built around a beautiful commentary on the Our Father.
  • The Interior Castle (1577): her masterpiece, written in about six months during the persecution. She saw the soul "as a castle made of a single diamond or of very clear crystal, in which there are many rooms", seven dwelling places leading inward to the centre where the King lives. The door into the castle is prayer.
  • The Book of the Foundations: the story of her convents, full of adventure, humour and sharp portraits of people.
  • Poems and letters: more than 450 of her letters survive, lively, practical and loving. Her poems include Vivo sin vivir en mí, "I live without living in myself".

Found after her death, tradition says, as a bookmark in her breviary, was a little prayer that has become the best-loved thing she ever wrote:

Nada te turbe, nada te espante;
todo se pasa, Dios no se muda.
La paciencia todo lo alcanza;
quien a Dios tiene nada le falta:
sólo Dios basta.

Let nothing disturb you, let nothing frighten you; all things pass, God does not change. Patience obtains everything; whoever has God lacks nothing: God alone suffices.

"A daughter of the Church"

In the autumn of 1582, after founding Burgos in the snow and floods of spring, Teresa set out for home. She was sixty-seven, exhausted and ill. On the way she was asked to go instead to Alba de Tormes, near Salamanca, where the Duchess of Alba was eager to see her. She arrived at her convent of the Annunciation on 20 September and went straight to bed. "God help me," she said, "how tired I am."

She received the last sacraments with great joy. When the Lord was brought to her, the nuns remembered, she raised herself in bed and cried out, "My Lord and my Spouse, the longed-for hour has come. It is time for us to see one another!" Again and again she thanked God, saying: "After all, Lord, I am a daughter of the Church." She died on the evening of 4 October 1582, in the arms of her faithful companion Blessed Anne of St Bartholomew.

That very night Spain switched to the new Gregorian calendar, and ten days were skipped: the day after 4 October 1582 was 15 October. That is why her feast falls on the 15th.

Her body was buried at Alba. When the coffin was opened nine months later, the sisters found it incorrupt and giving off a sweet fragrance, and it has been honoured that way ever since. In 1585 it was secretly taken to Ávila; after a great quarrel, the Pope ordered it returned to Alba in 1586, where it rests today in a silver urn above the high altar, the gift of King Ferdinand VI and Queen Barbara of Braganza. Her heart and her left arm are kept in reliquaries nearby. By a long and loving tradition, the heart bears the mark of the angel's spear. In August 2024 the tomb was opened for scientific study, and the Carmelite postulator reported that her body was in the same state as when it was last seen, in 1914.

Her places and churches

Ávila is still Teresa's city. Every corner of the old town has a story, and on her feast in October the whole city celebrates. Here are the places most connected with her, in the order of her life.

Gotarrendura

Her mother's family estate, about twenty kilometres north of Ávila. Teresa spent part of her childhood here, and some local traditions even claim she was born here. Of the manor house little is left, but the brick and adobe dovecote of St Teresa still stands and has been restored.

The Convento de Santa Teresa, Ávila: her birthplace

Built by the Discalced Carmelite friars in the 1630s on the site of the house where she was born, inside the walls near the Puerta de la Santa (the Saint's Gate). Its Baroque church faces a broad square. A chapel richly decorated in gold marks the place of her birth room, and the convent keeps a small room of relics and a museum of her life. Fittingly, a carved Christ at the column by Gregorio Fernández is honoured here.

The Monastery of the Incarnation, Ávila

Just outside the walls to the north. Here she lived for nearly twenty-seven years as a young nun, fell gravely ill, struggled in prayer, was converted before the wounded Christ, received the grace of the Transverberation, and later returned as prioress. Visitors can see her cell, the parlour where she spoke with John of the Cross, and a museum that keeps a tiny pen-and-ink drawing of Christ crucified, seen from above, made by John of the Cross; it later inspired Salvador Dalí. Tradition says that on the convent stairs Teresa once met a beautiful child who asked her name. "Teresa of Jesus," she said. "And I," he answered, "am Jesus of Teresa."

The Convento de San José ("de las Madres"), Ávila

East of the walls, the first foundation of her reform, opened on 24 August 1562. The original small chapel has been preserved, and the larger church was built in the early seventeenth century. Her friend and protector, Bishop Álvaro de Mendoza, is buried here, and the convent has a small museum of Teresa's memories.

The Cuatro Postes

The little shrine on the Salamanca road, just west of the city, where by tradition the young runaways were caught (pictured above). It offers the most famous view of the walled city.

Salamanca

The university city where Teresa founded in 1570. Her convent later moved, but the house where the first community lived, known as the Casa de Santa Teresa, still stands. By long tradition it was here, at Easter 1571, that she was carried into ecstasy by a novice singing a little song about longing to see Jesus.

Alba de Tormes: the Monastery and Basilica of the Annunciation

A small town on the river Tormes, about twenty kilometres south-east of Salamanca. Teresa founded the Convent of the Annunciation here in 1571, and died here in 1582. Her incorrupt body rests above the high altar of its church, and the reliquaries of her heart and arm are kept in a chamber beside it. Next door the Carmelite museum, CARMUS, displays treasures of her life, and the cell where she died can be seen. Pilgrims also see the great unfinished basilica that was begun in her honour at the end of the nineteenth century.

Teresa in art

Few saints have inspired so many great artists. Some painted her face as it was; others tried to paint the fire in her heart.

Fray Juan de la Miseria: the only portrait from life (1576)

In 1576, at the convent in Seville, Teresa was asked to sit for a portrait by a lay brother, Fray Juan de la Miseria. It is the only picture of her painted from life: a broad, kind, sixty-one-year-old face, with the dove of the Holy Spirit above. Tradition records her verdict with a smile: "God forgive you, Fray Juan, for painting me ugly and bleary-eyed!" The portrait is still kept by the Discalced Carmelite nuns of Seville, at the Convento de las Teresas.

Bernini: The Ecstasy of St Teresa (1647–1652)

The most famous image of her is in Rome, in the Cornaro Chapel of the church of Santa Maria della Vittoria. Commissioned by Cardinal Federico Cornaro, Gian Lorenzo Bernini carved in white marble the very moment of the Transverberation from her Life: the smiling angel with the golden dart, Teresa swept up on a cloud, her habit rippling as if on fire, golden rays of light streaming down from a hidden window above. On the side walls, members of the Cornaro family lean out of theatre boxes to watch, as we do.

Rubens: St Teresa Interceding for Souls in Purgatory and The Vision of the Dove

Peter Paul Rubens painted Teresa more than once. His great altarpiece of Teresa pleading with Christ for the souls in Purgatory was painted in the 1630s for the Discalced Carmelites of Antwerp, and a version from his workshop is the picture at the top of this page. A smaller panel of about 1614, the year of her beatification, shows her vision of the Holy Spirit as a dove.

Gregorio Fernández: St Teresa (about 1625)

The great Castilian sculptor Gregorio Fernández carved this life-size figure for a Carmelite convent in Valladolid, just a few years after her canonisation. Teresa stands with her book open in one hand and her pen raised in the other, as if pausing in mid-thought: the Doctor as writer. It is now in the National Museum of Sculpture in Valladolid.

Ribera and Zurbarán

Two masters of the Spanish Golden Age painted her as a writer touched by heaven. Jusepe de Ribera's St Teresa of 1630 (Museum of Fine Arts, Seville) shows her pausing over her book as she looks up in wonder. Francisco de Zurbarán's canvas of about 1650, in the main sacristy of Seville Cathedral, shows her at her desk, pen in hand, as the dove of the Holy Spirit comes down to inspire her.

Path to sainthood

Beatified by Pope Paul V on 24 April 1614. Canonised on 12 March 1622 by Pope Gregory XV, on the same day as Ignatius of Loyola, Francis Xavier, Isidore the Farmer and Philip Neri. On 27 September 1970, Pope Paul VI proclaimed her the first woman Doctor of the Church; a week later he gave the same title to St Catherine of Siena. Her feast is 15 October.

Patronages

Spain (proclaimed its patron beside St James by the Cortes in 1617, and again in 1812), writers, people in religious life, the sick, people ridiculed for their piety, and headache sufferers.

Trivia

  • She died on the night Catholic countries switched to the Gregorian calendar: the day after 4 October 1582 was 15 October, which is why her feast is the 15th.
  • Her family on her father's side were conversos, descended from Jews of Toledo who had become Christians. Her grandfather Juan Sánchez had been reconciled by the Inquisition in 1485.
  • St Edith Stein, a Jewish philosopher, read Teresa's Life in one night in 1921 and closed the book saying, "This is the truth." She became a Catholic, and later a Discalced Carmelite.
  • "Christ has no body now but yours", often quoted as hers, is not found in her writings. Her own words are better known in Spanish: sólo Dios basta, God alone suffices.
  • Her works were first printed in 1588 at Salamanca, edited by the poet and theologian Fray Luis de León.
