Her story

A little girl of four is offered a basket of dolls' clothes and told to choose what she likes. Her sister Céline takes a ball of wool. Thérèse puts out her hand and says, "I choose everything," and carries off the whole basket. Years later, as a young nun, she would say the same words to God: "My God, I choose everything… I choose all that Thou willest."

That child was Thérèse Martin, the youngest of five daughters of two saints, Louis and Zélie Martin of Alençon in Normandy. She lost her mother at four, was healed by the smile of the Virgin Mary at ten, and on Christmas night when she was nearly fourteen received a grace that turned her from a sensitive, tearful girl into a strong and joyful soul. At fourteen she went to Rome and asked Pope Leo XIII himself to let her enter Carmel, and at fifteen she did, joining two of her sisters behind the grille of the Carmel of Lisieux.

She lived there only nine years. She did nothing the world would call great. But she discovered what she called her "little way" of trust and love, a way to holiness open to everyone, and she wrote it down at the request of her sisters. She died of tuberculosis on 30 September 1897, aged twenty-four, with the words "My God, I love Thee!" Within a few years her Story of a Soul had gone round the world, and a "shower of roses" of graces followed. Pius XI canonised her in 1925 and named her patroness of the missions; St John Paul II proclaimed her a Doctor of the Church in 1997. Her basilica at Lisieux is the second place of pilgrimage in France after Lourdes, and millions call her simply the Little Flower.

Why the Church honours her

  • A heart given wholly to God. From the age of three, she said, she refused God nothing. She entered Carmel at fifteen and offered her whole life in prayer for souls, especially for priests.
  • The Little Way. She showed that holiness does not need great deeds, only great love: little sacrifices, a smile, a kind word, done for Jesus, and a child's trust in the arms of the Father. St John Paul II called her teaching "the science of divine love".
  • Confidence in God's mercy. "It is confidence and nothing but confidence that must lead us to Love," she wrote. Pope Francis took these words as the title of his exhortation on her.
  • Love for the missions. Though she never left her convent, she prayed and suffered for missionaries and adopted two of them as spiritual brothers. The Church made her patroness of the missions alongside St Francis Xavier.
  • Joy in suffering. She bore her last illness and a long night of faith with patience and humour, and offered every pain for others.
  • A teacher of the whole Church. The writings of this young woman without theological training were found so wise that the Church named her a Doctor.

Louis and Zélie: a holy home

Thérèse's story begins with her parents, whom the Church now honours as saints in their own right. Louis Martin was a watchmaker and jeweller in Alençon. As a young man he had climbed to the monastery of the Great St Bernard in the Alps to ask to become a monk, but he was turned away because he did not know Latin. Zélie Guérin had hoped to become a Sister of Charity and had also been refused. She became a maker of the famous point d'Alençon lace and built a flourishing business.

On 13 July 1858 they were married at midnight in the church of Notre-Dame d'Alençon. They had nine children. Four died young: two little boys, whom they had hoped would become priests and missionaries, and two little girls. The five daughters who lived all became nuns.

Their home was full of prayer and full of laughter. The family went to early Mass, kept Sundays holy, fasted in Lent and cared for the poor who came to the door. On the day they were canonised, Pope Francis said that they "practised Christian service in the family, creating day by day an environment of faith and love which nurtured the vocations of their daughters, among whom was Saint Therese of the Child Jesus."

The little queen of Alençon

Thérèse was born on 2 January 1873 at the family's house on rue Saint-Blaise, the last of the nine children. Two days later she was baptised in Notre-Dame d'Alençon with the names Marie-Françoise-Thérèse; her eldest sister Marie, aged twelve, was her godmother. Her baptismal gown is still kept in the church.

The baby was frail, and when she was a few weeks old her life was feared for. Zélie sent her to the country to be nursed by Rose Taillé, a farmer's wife at Semallé, a few miles outside town, who had already nursed two of the Martin children. There the little girl grew strong in the fresh air of the bocage, and came home in April 1874 a merry, rosy child.

She was a bright, determined and very affectionate child. "You can hardly imagine how much I loved my Father and Mother," she wrote, "and, being very demonstrative, I showed my love in a thousand little ways." Louis adored her. He called her his "little Queen", and sometimes, grandly, "Queen of France and Navarre". When she was four, her sister Léonie offered her and Céline a basket of dolls' things, and Thérèse chose everything; she later saw in it "a forecast, so to speak, of my whole life."

Five sisters

The Martin girls were close all their lives, and four of them would live together in the same Carmel.

"Come and kiss your dear Mother"

Zélie had been ill with cancer for years, and in the summer of 1877 it grew worse. She received the last sacraments with her family around her and died on 28 August 1877. Thérèse remembered her father lifting her up: "Come and kiss your dear Mother for the last time." Her funeral was held in Notre-Dame d'Alençon.

After the funeral the five girls stood together, and the maid said, "Poor little things, you have no mother now!" Céline threw herself into Marie's arms: "Well, you will be Mother now!" Thérèse ran to Pauline: "Well, as for me, it is Pauline who will be my Mother!"

That November Louis sold the lace business and took his daughters to Lisieux, to be near Zélie's brother Isidore Guérin, a pharmacist, his wife and their daughters Jeanne and Marie. Isidore found them a comfortable house on a slope above the town, in its own garden: Les Buissonnets.

At Les Buissonnets her father was the centre of her world. She went on walks with him through the town, visiting a church each afternoon. He took her fishing, and she sat on the grass, "and, without knowing what meditation meant, my soul was absorbed in prayer." At night he showed her the stars, and she was delighted to find a T in the sky: her name, she said, was written in heaven.

At eight and a half she began school as a day pupil with the Benedictine nuns of Notre-Dame-du-Pré. She was a clever pupil, always near the top of her class, but she was shy and sensitive, and she loved best to come home.

The smile of the Virgin

In October 1882 her "second Mother", Pauline, entered the Carmel of Lisieux. Thérèse was nine, and the loss struck her to the heart. "Pauline is lost to me!" she cried. The prioress, Mother Marie de Gonzague, comforted her and promised that one day she would be "my little daughter Thérèse of the Child Jesus".

That winter Thérèse fell ill with a strange sickness: headaches, trembling, fits of terror, and long hours in which she seemed not to know her own family. No remedy helped. Louis sent money to Paris for a novena of Masses at the shrine of Our Lady of Victories "to obtain the cure of his poor little Queen".

On Sunday 13 May 1883, the feast of Pentecost, Marie, Léonie and Céline knelt at the foot of her bed and prayed before the statue of Our Lady that stood in her room. Thérèse turned to the statue too, and begged her heavenly Mother with all her heart to have pity on her.

"Suddenly the statue seemed to come to life and grow beautiful… what touched me to the very depths of my soul was her gracious smile." All her pain vanished. Two great tears ran down her cheeks, tears of pure joy. "Our Blessed Lady has come to me, she has smiled at me. How happy I am."

From that day she was well. A year later, on 8 May 1884, she made her First Communion at the abbey. She had prepared for it with little sacrifices, which Pauline taught her to count as flowers for Jesus. Jesus' first embrace was, she wrote, "indeed an embrace of love. I felt that I was loved, and I said: 'I love Thee, and I give myself to Thee for ever.'" A few weeks later she was confirmed, and received "the gift of fortitude in suffering".

The grace of Christmas night, 1886

Even after her healing, Thérèse cried easily and was hurt by the smallest thing. In October 1886 Marie, too, entered the Carmel, and Thérèse felt more alone than ever. She knew she had to grow up, but she could not do it by herself.

On Christmas Eve 1886 the family went to Midnight Mass at the cathedral of St Pierre in Lisieux. When they came home, Thérèse looked forward, as French children did, to finding presents in her shoes by the fireplace. But her father, tired, said within her hearing: "Really all this is too babyish for a big girl like Thérèse, and I hope it is the last year." Céline, seeing her eyes fill with tears, whispered, "Don't go downstairs just yet."

But Thérèse was no longer the same. She dried her eyes, ran down, and opened her presents "looking as happy as a queen." Jesus had changed her heart:

"On that blessed night the sweet Infant Jesus, scarce an hour old, filled the darkness of my soul with floods of light. By becoming weak and little, for love of me, He made me strong and brave." Afterwards she said: "I felt charity enter into my soul, and the need to forget myself and to please others; since then I've been happy!"

"My first sinner"

After that night Thérèse grew quickly in grace. She read the Imitation of Christ until she knew it almost by heart, and she and Céline spent long evenings talking together of heaven.

One Sunday, closing her missal after Mass, she saw a holy picture of the Crucified, with the Precious Blood flowing from one hand and no one to gather it up. She resolved to stand at the foot of the Cross and gather that Blood for souls. Jesus' cry on the Cross, "I thirst!", rang in her heart, and she longed to quench His thirst for souls.

In the summer of 1887 all France was talking of Henri Pranzini, condemned to death for the murder of two women and a child. He showed no sorrow. Thérèse prayed and offered sacrifices for him with all her heart, asking only for a sign that he had repented. The day after his execution she opened the newspaper La Croix: on the scaffold, at the last moment, Pranzini had turned, seized the crucifix the priest held out, and kissed the sacred wounds three times. Thérèse called him "my first sinner", and from then on her longing to save souls grew every day.

The little white flower

By now Thérèse knew that God was calling her to Carmel, and she wanted to enter at fifteen. On the feast of Pentecost 1887 she found her father sitting in the garden of Les Buissonnets and told him, in tears, of her vocation. Louis wept with her, and gave his consent. Then he went over to a low wall, picked a little white flower, and gave it to her, explaining how carefully God had brought it into being and preserved it. He had "pulled it up by the roots without breaking them… destined to live on, but in other and more fertile soil."

Thérèse saw in the little flower the story of her own life. She fastened it to a picture of Our Lady of Victories, and she would call her life story "the springtime of a little white Flower".

The Carmel was ready to receive her, but its ecclesiastical superior said she must wait until she was twenty-one, and her uncle Isidore, who at first thought her far too young, had only just given his consent. Thérèse went with her father to Bayeux to plead with the Bishop, Mgr Hugonin. She put up her hair to look older. The Bishop was kind but said he must consult. There was one hope left: the Pope.

"Holy Father, let me enter Carmel at fifteen"

In November 1887 the diocese of Bayeux sent a pilgrimage to Rome for the golden jubilee of Pope Leo XIII's priesthood, and Louis took Thérèse and Céline. They left Lisieux at three in the morning on 4 November. In Paris they prayed at Our Lady of Victories, where Thérèse found full peace about the smile of the Virgin, and they consecrated themselves to the Sacred Heart at Montmartre before taking the train through Switzerland to Italy.

They saw the cathedral of Milan, the canals of Venice, the relic of St Anthony at Padua, the body of St Catherine at Bologna and the Holy House at Loreto. In Rome, at the Colosseum, Thérèse and Céline slipped past the barrier and climbed down into the arena so that she could kiss the ground where the martyrs had shed their blood. In the catacombs the two sisters lay down for a moment in the old tomb of St Cecilia, and at the church of St Agnes Thérèse tried to bring back a relic for Pauline, now Sister Agnes.

On Sunday 20 November 1887 the pilgrims attended the Pope's Mass and were received in audience. Each knelt before the Holy Father in turn, and the Vicar General, Father Révérony, announced that no one was to speak to him. Thérèse looked at Céline, who said: "Speak!" So, kneeling before the old Pope, she raised her eyes full of tears:

"Holy Father, in honour of your jubilee, will you allow me to enter the Carmel when I am fifteen?"

The Pope turned to Father Révérony, who explained that the matter was in the hands of the superiors. "Well, my child," said Leo XIII, "do whatever the Superiors decide." Thérèse clasped her hands on his knees: "Holy Father, if only you say 'yes,' everyone else would agree." He looked at her and said, clearly: "Well, well! You will enter if it is God's Will." The Noble Guard had to come forward and lead her away.

She left the audience in tears, but at peace, for she had done all she could. Before the journey she had offered herself to the Child Jesus as His little plaything, "a little ball of no value, that could be thrown on the ground, kicked about, pierced, left in a corner, or pressed to His Heart just as it might please Him." Now, she thought, He had pierced His little ball. The pilgrims went on to Naples and Pompeii, to Assisi and Florence, Pisa and Genoa, and home along the Mediterranean shore. On 28 December the Bishop of Bayeux gave his consent.

Into the Carmel of Lisieux

On 9 April 1888, at fifteen, Thérèse said goodbye to her family, knelt for her father's blessing, and passed through the enclosure door of the Carmel of Lisieux. Pauline and Marie were waiting for her. "At last my desires were realised," she wrote, and her soul was filled with "so deep and sweet a peace" that "has never left me even amid the greatest trials."

The Carmelite life was poor and hard: rising before dawn, hours of silent prayer and the Divine Office, work in silence, plain food without meat, and no fire in winter except in one room. Thérèse often found no sweetness in prayer, and sometimes fell asleep at meditation. She did not let it trouble her: "little children are equally dear to their parents whether they are asleep or awake." She worked in the laundry and the refectory, and later as sacristan, preparing the altar for Mass with special love.

On 10 January 1889 she received the habit. Since childhood she had loved snow, and she had longed for snow on her clothing day, but the weather was as mild as spring. Her father met her at the enclosure door in tears: "Ah! Here is my little Queen!" When she came back into the cloister after the ceremony, the garden was white with snow.

On 8 September 1890, the feast of the Nativity of Our Lady, she made her profession. "I came to save souls," she had said at her examination, "and especially to pray for priests." Over her heart she carried a little prayer asking Jesus for martyrdom "of soul or body", and that she might be "trodden under foot, forgotten, as a little grain of sand". That evening she laid her crown of roses at Our Lady's feet. On 24 September she received the black veil.

Papa's great trial

The joy of her clothing day was her father's "last feast on earth". In 1888 Louis had begun to suffer from a disease of the brain. He grew confused and wandered from home, and in February 1889 he had to be taken to the hospital of the Bon Sauveur at Caen, where he stayed three years. For a father so loved and so loving it was a deep humiliation, and his daughters felt it with all their hearts. Thérèse added to her religious name "of the Holy Face", thinking of the disfigured face of Christ in His Passion, and she saw her father's sufferings hidden in it.

In 1892 Louis came home to Lisieux, where Céline and Léonie cared for him. He was brought once more to the parlour of the Carmel. As they said goodbye, he raised his eyes, and pointing upwards said in a voice full of tears: "In Heaven!" He died peacefully on 29 July 1894. The Church now honours him as St Louis Martin.

Céline's camera

In February 1893 Pauline was elected prioress, and as Mother Agnes of Jesus she asked her little sister to help guide the novices. Thérèse, barely twenty, became their teacher and friend. She was gentle but frank, and taught them with pictures from everyday life. She called herself "the little brush that Jesus has chosen to paint His likeness in the souls" entrusted to her, and she told them how, as a child, she had loved a kaleidoscope, whose three mirrors turn scraps of coloured paper into beautiful patterns, a picture of the Holy Trinity.

On 14 September 1894, six weeks after her father's death, Céline entered the Carmel too. She brought with her a camera, a rare thing in a convent, and was allowed to use it. Because of Céline we have dozens of photographs of Thérèse.

Playing St Joan of Arc

Thérèse loved St Joan of Arc from childhood. In the Carmel she wrote poems and little plays for the community's feast days, and in January 1894 and January 1895 she wrote and acted in two plays about the Maid of Orléans. Céline photographed her in costume.

An offering to Merciful Love

On Trinity Sunday, 9 June 1895, during Mass, Thérèse was filled with the desire to give herself to God's merciful love, which so many souls refused. With Mother Agnes's permission, on 11 June she knelt with Céline before the statue of the Virgin of the Smile and read her Act of Oblation to Merciful Love. It ends:

"When comes the evening of life, I shall stand before Thee with empty hands, because I do not ask Thee, my God, to take account of my works. All our works of justice are blemished in Thine Eyes. I wish therefore to be robed with Thine own Justice, and to receive from Thy Love the everlasting gift of Thyself."

A few days later, as she began the Way of the Cross in the choir, she "felt suddenly wounded by a dart of fire so ardent" that she thought she would die of love.

That same year Mother Agnes, hearing her tell stories of their childhood, ordered her to write down her memories. In the evenings, in the little free time she had, Thérèse filled a school exercise book. She began by kneeling before the statue of Our Lady, and wrote it as a song of thanksgiving: "I begin therefore to sing what must be my eternal song: 'the Mercies of the Lord.'"

The Little Way

Thérèse longed to be a saint. But when she compared herself with the great saints, she felt like a grain of sand beside a mountain whose peak is lost in the clouds. Instead of being discouraged, she looked for another way:

"I will seek out a means of getting to Heaven by a little way—very short and very straight, a little way that is wholly new. We live in an age of inventions; nowadays the rich need not trouble to climb the stairs, they have lifts instead. Well, I mean to try and find a lift by which I may be raised unto God, for I am too tiny to climb the steep stairway of perfection."

She found her lift in the words of Isaiah: "As one whom the mother caresseth, so will I comfort you." "Thine Arms, then, O Jesus, are the lift which must raise me up even unto Heaven. To get there I need not grow; on the contrary, I must remain little, I must become still less."

This was her Little Way: the way of spiritual childhood, of trust and self-surrender. Her part was love, shown in the smallest things: a smile for a sister who tried her patience, help given unasked, a complaint swallowed for Jesus. "The only way I have of proving my love is to strew flowers before Thee—that is to say, I will let no tiny sacrifice pass, no look, no word. I wish to profit by the smallest actions, and to do them for Love."

There was one sister in the community who "annoyed me in all that she did". Thérèse set herself "to do for this sister all I should do for the one I loved most," prayed for her every time they met, and greeted her with her sweetest smile. One day the sister said to her with a beaming face: "My dear Soeur Thérèse, tell me what attraction you find in me, for whenever we meet, you greet me with such a sweet smile." What attracted her, Thérèse wrote, "was Jesus hidden in the depths of her soul."

"My vocation is love"

In September 1896, during her retreat, Thérèse wrote to her sister Marie the pages that are the heart of Story of a Soul. She felt in herself every vocation: to be a priest, an apostle, a missionary, a martyr, a Doctor of the Church. How could one little soul fulfil them all? She found the answer in St Paul's hymn to charity: love contains all vocations.

"At last I have found my vocation. My vocation is love! Yes, I have found my place in the bosom of the Church, and this place, O my God, Thou hast Thyself given to me: in the heart of the Church, my Mother, I will be LOVE!… Thus I shall be all things: thus will my dream be realised."

Two missionary brothers

Thérèse had always longed to be a missionary. One washing day in October 1895 Mother Agnes called her aside and read her a letter from a young seminarian, Maurice Bellière, who asked the Carmel for a sister to pray for him and for the souls he hoped to save; he later went to Africa with the White Fathers. In 1896 Mother Marie de Gonzague gave her a second brother, Father Adolphe Roulland of the Paris Foreign Missions, who was leaving for China. Thérèse wrote them letters full of tenderness and fire, offering them her prayers and sufferings, and promising to help them still more from heaven.

Her special patron among the missionaries was the young martyr Théophane Vénard of the same society, beheaded in Tonkin in 1861. "Théophane Vénard is a little saint," she said; "his life was not marked by anything extraordinary. He had an ardent devotion to Our Immaculate Mother and a tender love of his own family." The Carmel of Hanoi in Indochina asked for her, and she made a novena to him for her cure so that she might go. On 6 September 1897, already dying, she was given the relic of him she had long wished for, and she kissed it again and again. For her farewell she took his words: "I, little flower of one day, am the first to be gathered!" He was canonised in 1988.

The night of faith

In the night between Holy Thursday and Good Friday, 3 April 1896, Thérèse coughed up blood. At five in the morning she went to the window and found her handkerchief soaked with blood, and she was filled with joy: "on this anniversary of His Death, my Beloved had allowed me to hear His first call." She told the prioress, and kept the full Lenten fast and all her work: "the hope of soon entering Heaven transported me with joy."

Then, at Easter, a thick darkness fell on her soul. The thought of heaven, which had been her joy since childhood, seemed veiled, and she felt the temptations of those who do not believe. She called it her trial of faith. She did not stop believing. She went on making acts of faith and love, and she offered her darkness for those who had no faith:

"For love of Thee she will sit at that table of bitterness where these poor sinners take their food, and she will not stir from it until Thou givest the sign."

In June 1897 she began the last part of her story for Mother Marie de Gonzague, prioress once more, writing in the garden when she could no longer walk, and in pencil when she could no longer hold a pen. Her sisters wondered at her cheerfulness. She went on joking to the end.

"A shower of roses"

At the beginning of July 1897 she was moved to the infirmary of the Carmel. Her sisters sat by her bed, and Mother Agnes wrote down her words. Her sufferings were terrible, but her words were full of peace and longing:

"I feel that my mission is soon to begin—my mission to make others love God as I love Him… to teach souls my little way… I will spend my heaven in doing good upon earth."

"After my death I will let fall a shower of roses."

When a sister spoke to her of the happiness of heaven, she answered: "It is not that which attracts me." "And what attracts you?" "Oh! it is Love! To love, to be beloved, and to return to earth to win love for our Love!" Heaven would not be rest for her: "No, there can be no rest for me until the end of the world."

"My God, I love Thee"

On Thursday 30 September 1897, after a long agony, the community gathered in the infirmary in the evening. Thérèse looked at her crucifix. "Oh! … I love Him! … My God, I … love … Thee!" These were her last words.

Then, her sisters recorded, "suddenly she raised herself, as though called by a mysterious voice; and opening her eyes, which shone with unutterable happiness and peace, fixed her gaze a little above the statue of Our Lady. Thus she remained for about the space of a Credo," and her soul was borne away to heaven. She was twenty-four years and nine months old.

Hundreds of medals and rosaries were brought to touch her as she lay in the chapel. On 4 October, after a funeral attended by many priests, she was buried in the Carmelites' plot in the cemetery of Lisieux.

Story of a Soul goes round the world

A year after her death, Mother Agnes gathered Thérèse's three manuscripts into one book, Histoire d'une âme, "Story of a Soul". It was printed in 1898 and sent, as was the custom, to the other Carmels in place of the usual notice of a sister's death. Nuns lent it to priests and friends, and the friends asked for more. Soon it was being reprinted again and again, translated into many languages, and read by people of every kind: priests and children, soldiers and sailors, the sick in hospitals and missionaries at the ends of the earth.

Letters began to arrive at the Carmel from all over the world, telling of graces received through her prayers: healings, conversions, help in every kind of trouble, and often the scent of roses. Her shower of roses had begun.

Saint, patroness and Doctor

The Church moved with unusual speed. Her body was exhumed in 1910 as her cause began, and on 10 June 1914 St Pius X introduced it in Rome. Benedict XV set aside the rule that fifty years must pass after death, and on 14 August 1921 declared her heroic virtues, giving an address on the way of spiritual childhood. In March 1923 her remains were carried from the cemetery to the Carmel chapel, and on 29 April 1923 Pius XI beatified her.

Pius XI called her "the star of his pontificate". On 17 May 1925 he canonised her in St Peter's Basilica. About sixty thousand people filled the basilica and half a million more stood outside, and that night the dome of St Peter's was lit with torches for the first time since 1870.

On 14 December 1927, at the request of many missionary bishops, Pius XI proclaimed the little nun who never left her convent patroness of the missions, together with St Francis Xavier, the great apostle of the Indies. In 1944 Pius XII named her secondary patroness of France, with St Joan of Arc.

St John Paul II came to Lisieux as a pilgrim on 2 June 1980. On 19 October 1997, the centenary year of her death, he proclaimed her a Doctor of the Church in his apostolic letter Divini Amoris Scientia, "The Science of Divine Love". She "is not only the youngest Doctor of the Church," he wrote, "but is also the closest to us in time." She is one of four women Doctors, with St Teresa of Ávila, St Catherine of Siena and St Hildegard of Bingen.

On 19 October 2008 her parents were beatified at Lisieux, and on 18 October 2015 Pope Francis canonised them in St Peter's Square. In 2023, for the 150th anniversary of her birth, he gave the Church a whole apostolic exhortation on her message, C'est la confiance: "It is confidence and nothing but confidence that must lead us to Love."

The pilgrim saint

Thérèse had longed to be a missionary in every land. Since 1994 her relics have travelled the world in a reliquary, carried from country to country and continent to continent. When they came to Ireland in 2001, crowds came to pray at every stop. In 2009 they toured England and Wales, stopping overnight in York Minster on her feast day, 1 October, and about a quarter of a million people came to venerate them. In 2008 the astronaut Ron Garan even carried a relic of St Thérèse into space aboard the shuttle Discovery, a gift of the Carmelites of New Caney, Texas, in honour of her wish to preach the Gospel everywhere at once.

Wherever her relics go, people bring roses. And the letters still come to Lisieux.

Her places and churches

Alençon: her birthplace

The Martin house on rue Saint-Blaise is now a shrine, with the room where Thérèse was born and a chapel built beside it in her honour. Since the canonisation of her parents, Alençon has become a place of pilgrimage for families.

Alençon: the basilica of Notre-Dame

The Gothic church of Notre-Dame d'Alençon, with its famous lace-like porch, is where Louis and Zélie were married, where Thérèse was baptised and where Zélie's funeral was held. Her baptismal gown is shown there, and a modern stained-glass window shows her baptism. Benedict XVI made it a minor basilica in 2009, after her parents' beatification.

Lisieux: Les Buissonnets

The family home is kept as it was in her girlhood, with her room, the dining room, her toys and her First Communion dress. The Church bought the house in 1922, and in 1931 a white marble statue was set up in the garden showing Thérèse asking her father for leave to enter Carmel.

Lisieux: the Carmel

The Carmelite nuns still live and pray in the Carmel where Thérèse lived. In the chapel, open to all, her relics rest in a shrine beneath the statue of the Virgin of the Smile.

Lisieux: the Basilica of St Thérèse

Built on a hill above the town between 1929 and 1954, in a style inspired by the Sacré-Cœur in Paris, the basilica is one of the largest churches of the twentieth century and welcomes some 600,000 visitors a year. It was blessed by Cardinal Pacelli, the future Pius XII, on 11 July 1937, and consecrated on 11 July 1954. A reliquary given by Pius XI holds two bones of her right arm. The basilica came through the bombing of Lisieux in June 1944 with little damage.

Lisieux: the cathedral of St Pierre

Lisieux's Gothic cathedral was the Martin family's parish church. Here Thérèse heard Midnight Mass on Christmas night 1886, the night of her conversion.

Paris: Our Lady of Victories

Louis had Masses said at this shrine for her healing in 1883, and Thérèse prayed here on her way to Rome in 1887.

Rome: the Vatican

At the audience in the Vatican on 20 November 1887 she asked Leo XIII for leave to enter Carmel. In St Peter's she was canonised in 1925; in the square before it she was proclaimed a Doctor of the Church in 1997, and her parents were canonised in 2015.

Thérèse in art

Céline's photographs

The truest image of Thérèse is in the photographs taken by her sister Céline in the Carmel. They show a young woman with a round face, clear eyes and a quiet smile: in the garden, at work, in costume as Joan of Arc, with her sisters, and in her last illness. After her death Céline also drew and painted her many times from memory.

In her own hand

Thérèse painted holy pictures and decorated vestments and altar linen for the chapel. Her picture for Father Roulland, shown above, gathers into one small frame the dates of the graces of their two lives.

The Virgin of the Smile

The statue that smiled on her in 1883 went with the family from Alençon to Lisieux and came into the Carmel. It now stands above her shrine.

Early illustrations

The first French editions of Story of a Soul were illustrated with photographs of her homes and convent and with drawings such as the portraits by Charles Jouvenot shown above. In statues and holy pictures she is shown in the brown habit and white mantle of Carmel, holding a crucifix covered with roses, the roses she promised to let fall on the world.

Path to sainthood

Thérèse died known to few outside her convent. Story of a Soul and the graces reported by its readers made her known all over the world. Her body was exhumed in 1910 as the diocesan inquiry began. St Pius X introduced her cause in Rome on 10 June 1914. Benedict XV waived the fifty-year rule and declared her heroic virtues on 14 August 1921. Pius XI beatified her on 29 April 1923 and canonised her on 17 May 1925, less than twenty-eight years after her death. Her feast was kept on 3 October from 1927 and moved to 1 October in 1969. Pius XI named her patroness of the missions with St Francis Xavier on 14 December 1927; Pius XII made her secondary patroness of France with St Joan of Arc in 1944; and St John Paul II proclaimed her a Doctor of the Church on 19 October 1997.

Patronages

Missions and missionaries (with St Francis Xavier); France (with St Joan of Arc); florists and gardeners; pilots and aviators; the sick, especially those with tuberculosis; orphans; and Russia. She is invoked above all for trust in God and for priests, and by everyone who asks for one of her roses.

Trivia

  • Her name in religion was Sister Thérèse of the Child Jesus and of the Holy Face. In France she is often called "la petite Thérèse", little Thérèse, to tell her apart from the great St Teresa of Ávila.
  • She borrowed the name "little flower" from the martyr St Théophane Vénard, and on the manuscript of her life she wrote the title "The Story of the Springtime of a little white Flower".
  • Mother Teresa of Calcutta took her religious name from St Thérèse of Lisieux: "I chose Therese as my namesake because she did ordinary things with extraordinary love." See St Teresa of Calcutta.
  • The singer Édith Piaf, as a seven-year-old in 1922, is said by her biographers to have been cured of blindness after a pilgrimage to Thérèse's grave.
  • On the pilgrimage of 1887 she venerated the relics of St Anthony of Padua, lay down in the empty tomb of St Cecilia in the catacombs, visited the church of St Agnes and walked in the footsteps of St Francis of Assisi.
  • She loved the writings of St John of the Cross, and his words are woven through her own.
  • Her heroine was St Joan of Arc, with whom she shares the title of patroness of France. She is one of four women Doctors of the Church with St Teresa of Ávila, St Catherine of Siena and St Hildegard, and patroness of the missions with St Francis Xavier.
