St GenevieveThe woman who would not let Paris run from Attila

Saint Genevieve (1821), Élisabeth Vigée Le Brun · public domain, via Wikimedia Commons

Livedc. 420–c. 502, Nanterre–Paris
Feast day3 January (Paris; not universal)
RecognisedPre-Congregation
PlaceParis, France

Her story

Almost everything early about Genevieve comes from an anonymous Life written about twenty years after she died, around 520. That is close, for a saint of her century, and it is still a saint's Life, with wonders in it.

She was born at Nanterre, near Paris, about 420. As a child she was presented to Germanus of Auxerre, passing through on his way to Britain to deal with the Pelagians. The Life says he singled her out and she asked to be consecrated a virgin. She lived in Paris from then on, eating little, praying at night, and annoying people. Twice, the Life says, the city was ready to drown her or drive her out as a false prophet, and Germanus's intervention, once in person and once by a letter, stopped it.

In 451 Attila and the Huns came west. The cities of northern Gaul were emptying. Paris was for flight. Genevieve told the wives of the city to stay, to fast, and to pray, and said Attila would not touch Paris. He did not. He turned for Orléans, and that summer he was stopped at the Catalaunian Plains and withdrew back across the Rhine. The Life, and Paris ever after, gave the city's escape to her prayers. A military historian can add that Attila had reasons to bypass a town on the way to richer targets. The city kept her version, and it is the version the early Life already tells.

Later, when Paris was blockaded and hungry, she took boats up the Seine through the fighting and brought grain back from Arcis-sur-Aube. Childeric, the Frankish king, is said to have respected her even when he was besieging her city, and to have released prisoners because she asked. Clovis's victory and his baptism belong to the next chapter of Paris. She did not baptise him; Remigius did. She was already the person the city trusted with its dead and its bread. She died about 502 (some put it as late as 512) and was buried in a church on the hill that became the abbey of Sainte-Geneviève.

The Revolution turned the abbey church into the Panthéon and scattered or destroyed most of her relics. What survived came to rest in the church of Saint-Étienne-du-Mont, next door, where her shrine is now. Paris still keeps 3 January.

Path to sainthood

Pre-Congregation. Patron of Paris from the early Middle Ages. Her feast is not on the universal General Roman Calendar.

Patronages

Paris, fever, drought, and women in public danger. Also, in the old lists, disasters of war.

Trivia

  • Vigée Le Brun's late painting makes her a shepherdess with a distaff, a halo and a rainbow over a village. The candle is her older attribute: the devils of her Life kept blowing it out, and she relit it without a flint.
  • Her church became the burial place of French kings' rivals: the Panthéon holds Voltaire, Rousseau and Victor Hugo, on the site of her abbey.
  • The early Life is the source to read. Later medieval lives add dragons and candle-miracles. The Attila episode and the grain boats are already in the sixth-century text.
