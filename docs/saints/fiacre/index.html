St FiacreThe Irish hermit whose spade cleared a forest — patron of gardeners and taxi drivers

Le Faouët (56) Chapelle Saint-Fiacre Vitrail de Saint-Fiacre 04 · GO69 · CC BY-SA 3.0, via Wikimedia Commons (stained glass, Chapelle Saint-Fiacre, Le Faouët)

Livedd. c. 670, near Meaux (France) (traditional)
Feast day30 August (18 August in Ireland in some calendars)
RecognisedPre-Congregation (local cult)

The story

Fiacre left Ireland in the great age of Irish monastic exile and settled near Breuil, near Meaux, where Bishop Faro gave him land for a hermitage and hospice. Pilgrims and the sick came; vegetables and herbs paid for hospitality.

The full story

A garden in a day

Legend says Faro offered as much land as Fiacre could turn in a day; the saint walked dragging his spade (or staff) and a furrow — and cleared plot — opened behind him. A suspicious woman accused him of witchcraft; Fiacre, in the misogynist moral of the tale, thereafter barred women from the enclosure, a custom long kept at his chapel.

From hermit to cabbies

His Paris shrine stood by the Hôtel de Saint-Fiacre, where in the 1640s horse-drawn hire carriages first rented out — so Parisian cabs became fiacres, and the hermit became patron of taxi drivers. Gardeners had claimed him centuries earlier for the spade and the hospice garden.

Legend. An Irish hermit at Breuil with an early cult is plausible history; the miraculous furrow and the ban on women are medieval storytelling. The taxi connection is documented Parisian history of the 17th century.

Path to sainthood

Pre-Congregation local cult in France and Ireland; relics long venerated at Meaux Cathedral (destroyed in the Revolution).

Patronages

Gardeners, taxi drivers, haemorrhoid sufferers (blunt popular patronage from the 'fig' disease nickname).

Trivia

  • The French word fiacre for a cab preserves a saint's name in transport history — like 'hansom' preserving a person, but holier.
  • His spade makes him one of the easiest saints to spot in church windows.
