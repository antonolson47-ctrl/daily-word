St SunnivaThe Irish princess whose cave-church sealed itself over her on Selja

Selje kloster Abbey Selja · Svein-Magne Tunli - tunliweb.no · CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons (Selja Abbey ruins, where her cave church stands)

Lived10th century (traditional), Ireland – Selja (Norway)
Feast day8 July
RecognisedPre-Congregation; patron of Bergen and western Norway

The story

Norway's first female saint in tradition was an Irish Christian princess who chose the sea over a forced pagan marriage: Sunniva and her companions put out in ships without sails (the medieval symbol of leaving the outcome to God) and were carried to the island of Selja off Norway's west coast.

The full story

The cave on Selja

They lived in caves, eating fish, accused by mainland neighbours of stealing livestock. When Earl Haakon sent men against them, the Christians prayed — and, legend says, the cave mouth collapsed, burying and sealing them. When Olav Tryggvason's men later found fragrant, incorrupt bones on Selja (c. 996, in the saga telling), a church rose over the cave.

Patron of the west coast

Her shrine moved to Bergen in 1170 and made her patron of the diocese and the coast; Selja's ruined Albanus monastery and cave church remain one of Norway's most atmospheric pilgrim places, reached by boat as she was.

Legend. Sunniva's story survives in the Latin Acta Sanctorum in Selio and saga tradition, written down centuries after the 900s. Irish-Norwegian Christian contact is historical; her biography is tradition. Bergen's medieval cult and Selja's ruins are fact.

Path to sainthood

Pre-Congregation; recognised by the early Norwegian Church; shrine at Bergen from 1170.

Patronages

Diocese of Bergen and the western Norwegian coast, sailors (regional).

Trivia

  • Selja is considered the cradle of the Norwegian Church alongside Stiklestad and Nidaros.
  • Pilgrim boats still land visitors under the cave-church on Selja each summer.
