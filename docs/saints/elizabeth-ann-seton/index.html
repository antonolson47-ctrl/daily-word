St Elizabeth Ann SetonNew York wife and mother, foundress at Emmitsburg

Mother Seton, lithograph by J. Penniman of Baltimore, published by G. Will, deposited 1835 · public domain, Library of Congress, via Wikimedia Commons

Lived1774–1821, New York and Maryland
Feast day4 January (United States)
Canonised14 September 1975, Paul VI

Her story

Elizabeth Ann Bayley was born in New York on 28 August 1774, the year of the first Continental Congress. Her father, Richard Bayley, was a physician who later served as health officer of the port. Her mother died when Elizabeth was small. She grew up in the Episcopal Church and was married at Trinity Church, on 25 January 1794, to William Magee Seton, a merchant. They had five children: Anna Maria, William, Richard, Catherine, and Rebecca.

The business failed. William sickened with tuberculosis. In 1803 the family sailed for Italy, hoping the climate and the kindness of the Filicchi brothers, business friends in Livorno, would save him. William died there on 27 December 1803. In the Filicchi household Elizabeth, still an Episcopalian, met the Catholic Mass, the reserved sacrament, and a way of speaking about the Real Presence that she could not shake. She came home a widow of twenty-nine, and on 14 March 1805 she was received into the Catholic Church at St Peter's on Barclay Street. New York society did not applaud. Some of her family stepped back. She kept her children.

Bishop John Carroll invited her to Baltimore. In 1809 she moved to Emmitsburg, in the valley she called St Joseph's, and began the Sisters of Charity of St Joseph's. The rule was adapted from St Vincent de Paul, with Elizabeth's own American circumstances written into it: a widow with children still in the house, a school that took poor girls for nothing and others for a fee, and a community that was not yet tied to the French Daughters of Charity. That tie came later, in 1850, for part of the family of communities. She is the foundress. She is not the author of every later constitution.

She is widely honoured as a founder of the Catholic parish-school tradition in the United States. Small Catholic schools had existed earlier, especially in Maryland. What began at Emmitsburg was a community whose work was the school, the sick, and the poor, and that pattern spread. She died of tuberculosis at Emmitsburg on 4 January 1821. She was forty-six. A lithograph deposited at the Library of Congress in 1835 already calls her Mother Seton and quotes Proverbs: her children rose up and called her blessed. The Filicchi letters and the community's early records carry the dates of her birth, marriage, reception, foundation, and death.

Path to sainthood

The cause moved through the modern process. Beatified by John XXIII in 1963. Canonised by Paul VI on 14 September 1975, during the Holy Year. She was the first person born in the territory of the United States to be canonised. Her feast on 4 January is on the calendar of the United States. It is not a universal memorial in every country. Her tomb is at the National Shrine of Saint Elizabeth Ann Seton in Emmitsburg, Maryland.

Patronages

Catholic schools, widows, people whose faith has cost them a social world, and the diocese of her shrine. Seafarers have claimed her too, because of the crossing to Italy and the losses of that voyage.

Trivia

  • The 1835 Penniman print, above, is a devotional likeness made fourteen years after her death, not a photograph. It already dresses her in the cap and cape of the community.
  • She translated and adapted European spiritual books for her sisters while running a school and nursing the sick.
  • Two of her daughters, Anna Maria and Rebecca, died of tuberculosis before she did. The community she founded also became her children's household.
