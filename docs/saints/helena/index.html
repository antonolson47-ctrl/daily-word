Her story

Helena (Flavia Julia Helena) was the mother of the emperor St Constantine. Ancient writers agree she was of humble birth. St Ambrose, preaching decades after her death, called her a stabularia, a stable-girl or innkeeper. Constantine rose, and she rose with him: he gave her the title Augusta, and coins were struck in her name. Around 326–328, already an old woman, she travelled to the Holy Land.

What she certainly did there was build. She founded churches at Bethlehem, over the cave of the Nativity, and on the Mount of Olives. Constantine himself was building the great basilica over the tomb of Christ in Jerusalem, the church that became the Holy Sepulchre. Helena's journey belongs to that building campaign. She died about 330, and a huge porphyry sarcophagus connected with her is in the Vatican Museums.

The finding of the True Cross is the story the Church has told since the end of the fourth century, and it is told more fully below. Rome's basilica of Santa Croce in Gerusalemme still keeps relics said to be from that finding, with earth she is said to have brought from Jerusalem.

Churches in the Holy Land

Helena's pilgrimage left two great churches standing in the land of the Gospel, besides the Holy Sepulchre that her son was raising in Jerusalem. One marks the place of the Lord's birth; the other, the Mount of Olives, where he taught and from which he ascended. Both can be visited today, and both have their own pages in our Holy Land pilgrimage guides.

The Church of the Nativity, Bethlehem

Over the cave where Christians have honoured the birth of Christ since the second century, Helena and Constantine raised a basilica. St Justin Martyr, writing around 150, already spoke of a cave near Bethlehem; Origen, about 248, says the cave was shown to visitors. The first church was dedicated in 339. When that Constantinian basilica was lost, Justinian rebuilt it in the sixth century, and it is largely his church that pilgrims still enter.

Everyone comes in by the Door of Humility, a low entrance cut down, tradition says, so that no one should ride in on horseback. You have to bow to pass through. Inside, the long nave of Justinian's columns leads toward the grotto. On the walls, gold mosaics of the Crusader centuries — a procession of angels between the windows, scenes and inscriptions below — were carefully restored in 2013–2019. In 2012 the church and the pilgrimage route became Palestine's first UNESCO World Heritage site.

Down the steps, in the Grotto of the Nativity, a fourteen-point silver star is set in the marble floor. Around it the Latin still reads Hic de Virgine Maria Jesus Christus natus est: "Here Jesus Christ was born of the Virgin Mary." Lamps burn over the place, and pilgrims kneel to touch the star.

The basilica is shared, under the old Status Quo, by the Greek Orthodox, who keep the main church, the Catholics, whose church of St Catherine stands next door (the midnight Mass of Christmas is broadcast from there), and the Armenian Apostolic Church. St Jerome's cave, where he translated the Scriptures into Latin, lies beneath St Catherine's. The whole sanctuary, and how to visit it, is on the pilgrimage page for Bethlehem.

The Eleona and the Pater Noster, Mount of Olives

On the Mount of Olives Helena founded a second basilica, called the Eleona, from the Greek for an olive grove. She built it over a cave where tradition says Jesus taught the disciples — the teaching that includes the Lord's Prayer. The Eleona did not survive the centuries intact. It was destroyed, most likely in the Persian invasion of 614 or in the troubles that followed, and later churches on the same ground were lost in their turn.

What stands there now is the Church of the Pater Noster, a Carmelite sanctuary of the nineteenth century, with a cloister raised by the Princess de la Tour d'Auvergne on the ruins of the older holy place. The new church itself was begun and left unfinished; you can still see the stepped walls in the courtyard. All around the cloister, the Lord's Prayer is set on ceramic tiles in more than a hundred languages, a quiet chorus of "Our Father" in the tongues of the world. The cave of the Eleona remains, down a few steps, and pilgrims still go in to pray where tradition holds that the Lord taught his own how to pray.

A little farther up the mount, near the summit, is the Chapel of the Ascension, a small octagonal shrine in a courtyard, on the site of a fourth-century commemorative church. Tradition holds that Jesus ascended from there. The walk down the mount, from the Pater Noster and Dominus Flevit toward Gethsemane, is still the way of Palm Sunday, and is described on the pilgrimage page for Jerusalem.

The finding of the True Cross

The Church has told this story since the end of the fourth century, with love and with wonder, and it has never tired of telling it.

Around 326–328 Helena came to Jerusalem as a pilgrim, already an old woman, in the years when Constantine's builders were clearing a pagan temple that had stood over Golgotha and the tomb. Beneath that ground, tradition holds, she sought the Cross of the Lord. Three crosses were found. The question was how to know which was Christ's.

St Ambrose, preaching the funeral oration for the emperor Theodosius in 395 (De obitu Theodosii, 40–48), says she knew the Cross by the titulus, the title board that had been nailed above the Lord's head. Rufinus, writing about 402, tells the story as the Church has most often remembered it: Bishop Macarius of Jerusalem proposed a test, and a dying woman was healed when the third cross was laid upon her. Socrates and Sozomen, in the next generation, tell the same miracle of healing, and speak also of the nails. The Cross was recognised not by argument but by a work of mercy. Part of it, tradition says, was left in Jerusalem; part was sent to Constantinople; part Helena brought home to Rome.

In the Church of the Holy Sepulchre the memory of that finding is still kept in the crypt. Down a flight of steps is the Chapel of St Helena, an Armenian sanctuary of hanging lamps and worn stone, on the way to the place itself. Farther down, in a cistern-like chapel, is the Chapel of the Finding of the Cross, where pilgrims light candles in the dark.

The Church keeps the memory of the Cross in her calendar. The Exaltation of the Holy Cross, on 14 September, is a feast of the universal Church; it remembers the dedication of the Holy Sepulchre in 335, and later the recovery of the Cross by the emperor Heraclius. It is listed among the feast days of the year. The Invention of the Holy Cross, on 3 May, was for many centuries the Western feast of the finding itself. It was removed from the General Roman Calendar in 1960, but it is still remembered in some places, and in the older books of the Church.

Other relics

The Cross was not the only holy thing tradition credits to Helena's pilgrimage. Beside the wood itself, the Church has long honoured a cluster of relics of the Passion, and a few others that later ages placed in her care.

The Titulus Crucis, the title board of the Cross, is kept at Santa Croce in Gerusalemme. Tradition holds it is the board Ambrose said helped Helena know the Cross; a portion of a wooden title, with the charge against Jesus in Hebrew, Greek and Latin, was found walled up in the church in 1492. Nails of the Crucifixion are also among the relics of Santa Croce. One nail, a further tradition says, was set into the Iron Crown of Lombardy, the ancient crown kept in the Cathedral of Monza. Thorns from the Crown of Thorns, and a large fragment of the cross of the Good Thief, are shown with them in the Chapel of the Relics.

In Rome, across the square from St John Lateran, stand the Scala Sancta, the Holy Stairs. Pilgrims climb the twenty-eight marble steps on their knees. Tradition holds that these are the stairs of Pontius Pilate's praetorium in Jerusalem, which Jesus climbed on the day of his Passion, and that Helena brought them to Rome.

A later tradition also honours her in the story of the relics of the Magi. It says that Helena found them and took them to Constantinople; they were later at Milan, and in 1164 they were brought to Cologne, where the Shrine of the Three Kings still stands in the cathedral.

Santa Croce in Gerusalemme, Rome

The name is not a mistake. This church was meant to be a piece of Jerusalem in Rome. It was built in Helena's Sessorian Palace, an imperial residence against the Aurelian Walls, and consecrated around 325. Soil from Calvary was laid, so the story goes, under the floor of the chapel that bears her name, so that those who could not go to Jerusalem might still pray on that earth. The relics she brought from the Holy Land were kept here, and they are kept here still.

The church has been rebuilt more than once. Lucius II rebuilt it in 1144, and the Romanesque bell tower of that century still stands beside the later façade. In the 1740s, Benedict XIV gave it its present curving Baroque front and oval atrium, the work of Domenico Gregorini and Pietro Passalacqua. The visitor who stands in the piazza sees that later face; the older palace-church is within.

Inside, the apse holds the fresco of the Legend of the True Cross, painted about 1492 and attributed to Antoniazzo Romano and his workshop. Helena finds the Cross; Heraclius brings it back to Jerusalem; Christ is seated in glory in a blue mandorla above. Below the apse is the Chapel of St Helena, whose vault mosaic — designed around 1484 and attributed to the circle of Melozzo da Forlì, and later restored — shows Christ and the Evangelists in gold. Under that floor, tradition holds, is the earth of Calvary.

The Chapel of the Relics, reached by a corridor of the Stations of the Cross, holds the Titulus, a nail, thorns, and fragments of the Cross, with a fragment of the Good Thief's cross. (As of 2026 the chapel has been closed for urgent building works; the basilica is open. The pilgrimage page for Santa Croce has the current notice.)

Santa Croce is a stational church of Rome. On Laetare Sunday, the Fourth Sunday of Lent, and on Good Friday, the Church in Rome has long come here to keep the liturgy of the Cross, close to the relics and to the earth of Calvary. For the walk of the seven churches, and for hours and practical notes, see the pilgrimage page for Santa Croce in Gerusalemme.

Path to sainthood

Pre-Congregation saint, honoured in East and West from the fourth century. Her feast is 18 August in the Roman Martyrology. She is not on the universal General Roman Calendar. In the East she is remembered with Constantine on 21 May. The finding itself is kept, as above, on the Exaltation of the Holy Cross (14 September), and in older use on 3 May.

Patronages

New discoveries, converts, archaeologists, difficult marriages. Invoked by people looking for what has been lost.

Trivia

  • The town of Drepanum in Bithynia, a likely birthplace, was renamed Helenopolis in her honour.
  • Medieval England also gave her a British father, "Old King Cole" of Colchester, a story of its own.
  • Painters almost always put a large cross in her arms, as Cima da Conegliano does. The cross is the attribute; the crown is the Augusta.
