The Holy InnocentsThe children of Bethlehem, kept as a feast, not as named saints

The Massacre of the Innocents, Peter Paul Rubens, c. 1611–12 · public domain, Art Gallery of Ontario, via Wikimedia Commons

Feast day28 December
RecognisedFeast of the Christmas octave

The story the Gospel tells

Matthew is the only evangelist who tells it. After the magi left Bethlehem by another road, an angel warned Joseph to take the child and his mother to Egypt. Herod, seeing that the magi had not come back, "was exceeding wroth, and sent forth, and slew all the children that were in Bethlehem, and in all the coasts thereof, from two years old and under, according to the time which he had diligently enquired of the wise men" (Matthew 2:16). Matthew hears in the crying the line of Jeremiah: "Rachel weeping for her children, and would not be comforted, because they are not" (Matthew 2:17–18; Jeremiah 31:15).

The Gospel gives no names. It gives no number. It gives a place, Bethlehem and the district around it, an age, two years and under, and a king, Herod the Great, who is already known from other sources as a man who killed his own sons when he thought they threatened him. Historians argue about how large a killing in a small town would have been, and about why Josephus, who lists Herod's cruelties, does not mention this one. Those arguments belong in a commentary. They do not create a list of children. This page will not invent one.

Rubens's painting, above, is the scene as a Flemish painter staged it around 1611: soldiers, mothers, and the dead. It is a work of art about Matthew 2. It is not a portrait of any child who can be named, and it is not a count.

What the text supports. A feast of the Church built on one paragraph of Matthew. Herod the Great died in 4 BC, so if the episode is placed in his reign it belongs to the last years of his life. The flight into Egypt and the return to Nazareth are the Gospel's own sequel (Matthew 2:13–15, 19–23).

Numbers that are not in the Gospel. Byzantine tradition later spoke of 14,000 children. Some medieval Western writers said 144,000, borrowing the number from Revelation 7, which is about a multitude of the redeemed and not about Bethlehem. Both figures are piety doing arithmetic. They are not history, and they are not how this page counts.

How the Church keeps the day

In the West the feast is 28 December, the third day of Christmas, after Stephen on the 26th and John on the 27th. It is among the oldest feasts of the Christmas season. The East often keeps the holy infants on 29 December.

The theological reading, old and often repeated, is that they died because of Christ and so are martyrs, "baptized in blood", even though they were infants and could not confess the faith in words. That is the Church's reading of Matthew. It is not a second historical source.

Older Roman books dressed the day in violet, the colour of mourning, and left out the Gloria and the Alleluia, unless the feast fell on a Sunday. The current Roman calendar keeps Holy Innocents as a feast in red, the colour of martyrs. Both customs are trying to say the same hard thing: this is Christmas, and this is a day of killed children.

The Coventry Carol, from the Coventry mystery plays of the sixteenth century, is the English lullaby of the feast, the mothers singing over the children Herod has ordered dead. The song is named here and not quoted at length. Its refrain, the old "lully, lullay", is the sound of the day in English devotion.

Path to sainthood

There is no canonisation of individuals, because there are no individuals the Church can name. The feast itself is the recognition: a collective memory of the children of Bethlehem, kept every year inside the octave of Christmas.

Patronages

Choirboys and foundlings have been put under their name. More honestly, the day is when the Church prays for children who are killed, and for their parents. It is not a charm.

Places

Bethlehem, and the Church of the Nativity. Various churches have claimed relics of "the innocents". No relic can be tied to a named child. This page does not list fictional biographies for them.

Trivia

  • The three feasts after Christmas were read as three kinds of martyrdom: Stephen by will and in deed, John by will and not in deed (he suffered and was not killed), the innocents in deed and not by will. That is a medieval sermon scheme, useful, and not a verse of Matthew.
  • "Childermas" is the old English name of the day.
  • Rachel, in Matthew's quotation, is the matriarch buried on the road near Bethlehem (Genesis 35:19). The Gospel puts her grief on this story on purpose.
