His story

John and his brother James, fishermen and sons of Zebedee, left their nets to follow Jesus (Mark 1:19–20); Jesus nicknamed them Boanerges, "sons of thunder". With Peter and James he witnessed the Transfiguration and the agony in Gethsemane. Christian tradition identifies him with the "disciple whom Jesus loved", to whom Jesus entrusted his mother from the cross (John 19:26–27). Tradition also attributes to him the fourth Gospel, three letters and Revelation (written on the island of Patmos); modern scholars debate these attributions. According to Irenaeus, he lived to old age at Ephesus.

The voice of his Gospel

Near the end of the fourth Gospel the writer tells us why the book was written at all. It is not a scholar's preface. It is a purpose, and a hope:

KJV · John 20:31
But these are written, that ye might believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God; and that believing ye might have life through his name.
Greek · SBLGNT · John 20:31
Ταῦτα δὲ γέγραπται ἵνα πιστεύητε ὅτι Ἰησοῦς ἐστιν ὁ Χριστὸς ὁ υἱὸς τοῦ θεοῦ, καὶ ἵνα πιστεύοντες ζωὴν ἔχητε ἐν τῷ ὀνόματι αὐτοῦ.
Tauta de gegraptai hina pisteuēte hoti Iēsous estin ho Christos ho huios tou theou, kai hina pisteuontes zōēn echēte en tō onomati autou.
Latin · Vulgate · John 20:31
Haec autem scripta sunt ut credatis quia Iesus est Christus Filius Dei: et ut credentes vitam habeatis in nomine eius.

These things were written so that we might believe, and believing, have life. That is the note that sounds through everything given to us in John's name: the Gospel, the letters, and the vision on Patmos. The Church has long heard in them one voice, the voice of the beloved disciple, though, as the short account above already notes, modern scholars debate these attributions. What follows stays with the Scriptures themselves, and marks clearly where later tradition takes up the story.

The Gospel does not only argue. It remembers love. At the Last Supper, one disciple is named not by his father's house but by the Lord's affection:

KJV · John 13:23
Now there was leaning on Jesus' bosom one of his disciples, whom Jesus loved.

From the cross that same disciple is given a charge that has consoled the Church ever since. Jesus looks at his mother, and at the friend who has not fled:

KJV · John 19:26–27
When Jesus therefore saw his mother, and the disciple standing by, whom he loved, he saith unto his mother, Woman, behold thy son! Then saith he to the disciple, Behold thy mother! And from that hour that disciple took her unto his own home.

The letters carry the same heart into the life of the churches. "God is love" is not a motto hung on a wall. It is the claim on which Christian life stands:

KJV · 1 John 4:7–8
Beloved, let us love one another: for love is of God; and every one that loveth is born of God, and knoweth God. He that loveth not knoweth not God; for God is love.
Greek · SBLGNT · 1 John 4:8
ὁ μὴ ἀγαπῶν οὐκ ἔγνω τὸν θεόν, ὅτι ὁ θεὸς ἀγάπη ἐστίν.
ho mē agapōn ouk egnō ton theon, hoti ho theos agapē estin.
Latin · Vulgate · 1 John 4:8
Qui non diligit, non novit Deum: quoniam Deus caritas est.
KJV · 1 John 4:16
And we have known and believed the love that God hath to us. God is love; and he that dwelleth in love dwelleth in God, and God in him.

At the far end of the canon the same name is heard from an island in the Aegean. John writes as a brother, not as a prince:

KJV · Revelation 1:9–10
I John, who also am your brother, and companion in tribulation, and in the kingdom and patience of Jesus Christ, was in the isle that is called Patmos, for the word of God, and for the testimony of Jesus Christ. I was in the Spirit on the Lord's day, and heard behind me a great voice, as of a trumpet,

The vision does not end in terror. It ends in a promise, and in a prayer:

KJV · Revelation 21:3–5
And I heard a great voice out of heaven saying, Behold, the tabernacle of God is with men, and he will dwell with them, and they shall be his people, and God himself shall be with them, and be their God. And God shall wipe away all tears from their eyes; and there shall be no more death, neither sorrow, nor crying, neither shall there be any more pain: for the former things are passed away. And he that sat upon the throne said, Behold, I make all things new. And he said unto me, Write: for these words are true and faithful.
KJV · Revelation 22:20
He which testifieth these things saith, Surely I come quickly. Amen. Even so, come, Lord Jesus.

His travels

John's life, as Scripture lets us see it, begins on the shore of Galilee. He and his brother James were fishermen, sons of Zebedee, working with their father in the boat when Jesus called them (Mark 1:19–20). They left the nets. Jesus named the two of them Boanerges, "sons of thunder."

With Peter and James he was taken into the Lord's inner company. He was on the mountain of the Transfiguration, when Moses and Elijah appeared and the Father's voice was heard. He was in the garden of Gethsemane, asked to watch and pray in the hour of agony. The fourth Gospel, if we receive the old identification of the beloved disciple, places him at the foot of the cross, and then at the empty tomb. On the morning of the resurrection he ran with Peter; he reached the tomb first, stooped, saw the linen clothes lying, and went in after Peter: "and he saw, and believed" (John 20:8). Later, on the shore of Galilee, the risen Lord came to the fishermen again, and the beloved disciple was the one who knew him (John 21).

After Pentecost, Scripture still finds him in Jerusalem, now at Peter's side in the life of the young Church. Together they go up to the Temple and heal a lame man at the Beautiful Gate (Acts 3–4). Together they are sent to Samaria, that those who had received the word might receive the Holy Spirit (Acts 8:14). Years later Paul can name him among the pillars of the Church in Jerusalem:

KJV · Galatians 2:9
And when James, Cephas, and John, who seemed to be pillars, perceived the grace that was given unto me, they gave to me and Barnabas the right hands of fellowship; that we should go unto the heathen, and they unto the circumcision.

What comes after Jerusalem is given to us chiefly by tradition, and it should be heard as tradition. Irenaeus, who had known Polycarp, says that John lived on at Ephesus into old age, in the time of Trajan. The churches of Asia remembered him as their apostle. Revelation itself, which is Scripture, tells us that he was on the island of Patmos "for the word of God, and for the testimony of Jesus Christ" (Revelation 1:9). The book does not say how he came there. Later writers say he was exiled under the emperor Domitian, and that when Domitian died he was free to return to Ephesus. There, the same tradition says, he died in peace around the year 100, the only one of the Twelve who was not put to death. Jerome tells that in extreme old age he would be carried into the assembly, and would say only, "Little children, love one another." When they asked him why he always said the same thing, he answered that it was the Lord's command, and that if it were kept, it would be enough.

The churches he shepherded

Ephesus is the city to which the tradition of his last years is bound, and it is the first of the seven churches of Asia to whom the risen Lord speaks in Revelation 2–3. Those seven churches lay along the roads of the Roman province: Ephesus, Smyrna, Pergamum, Thyatira, Sardis, Philadelphia and Laodicea. In the vision they are not a map only. They are communities of faith, praised, warned and comforted: Ephesus, which had left its first love; Smyrna, told to be faithful unto death; Pergamum, holding fast where "Satan's seat" was; Thyatira, rich in works yet in danger; Sardis, with a name that it lived, though it was dead; Philadelphia, given an open door; Laodicea, lukewarm, and still loved, still invited to open the door.

Whether John himself founded each of them cannot be proved from Scripture. What the book does show is a pastor's heart for the churches of Asia, and the Church has long believed that the apostle who lived at Ephesus was the one who watched over that flock.

Irenaeus, writing late in the second century, is our strongest early witness for the next generation. He says that Polycarp, the bishop of Smyrna who was later martyred, had been a disciple of John, and that Irenaeus himself, as a boy, had heard Polycarp speak of what he had learned from those who had seen the Lord. That chain — John, Polycarp, Irenaeus — is one of the precious threads by which the Church has felt its nearness to the apostles.

Later tradition also places Ignatius of Antioch in John's circle. The evidence for that is thinner. What can be said more securely is that Ignatius belonged to the next generation of the Asian churches, a contemporary of Polycarp, and that the letters he wrote on the road to his martyrdom are full of the same love of Christ.

The eagle, his symbol

The Church has long given each of the four evangelists a living creature from the visions of Ezekiel and of John himself:

KJV · Ezekiel 1:10
As for the likeness of their faces, they four had the face of a man, and the face of a lion, on the right side: and they four had the face of an ox on the left side; they four also had the face of an eagle.
KJV · Revelation 4:7
And the first beast was like a lion, and the second beast like a calf, and the third beast had a face as a man, and the fourth beast was like a flying eagle.

Jerome and Augustine gave John the eagle, because his Gospel does not begin on the earth. It begins in the height of heaven: "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God" (John 1:1). The eagle, they said, is the creature that can look upon the sun. Irenaeus, writing earlier, had assigned the symbols differently, and gave John the lion; the Church in the West followed Jerome, and the eagle has been John's sign ever since. (The Book of Durrow, one of the oldest of the Insular gospel books, still follows that older assignment, and gives the eagle to Mark; its lion stands for John. The pages below follow the tradition the West has kept.)

The painters of those gospel books made the eagle unforgettable. In the Lindisfarne Gospels, John sits with a scroll across his knees, and above his halo the bird itself is labelled imago aquilae, "the image of the eagle." In the Book of Kells he is enthroned in a frame of knotwork, a book in his hand; and on the great symbols page the eagle takes its place with the man, the lion and the ox. In the Codex Aureus of Echternach, Christ sits in majesty in a mandorla, and the four creatures surround him; the eagle is John's sign in that golden vision.

John in art

Artists have loved John as the young disciple at Jesus' side, as the son given to Mary at the cross, and as the aged seer on Patmos, an eagle at his feet, a book open on his knee. The painting at the head of this page is one of the greatest of those Patmos pictures.

At the Last Supper the Church has always looked for him next to the Lord. Leonardo da Vinci, in the refectory of Santa Maria delle Grazie in Milan (about 1495–98), places a youthful, fair disciple immediately at Christ's right hand — to our left of the Lord — drawn back as Peter leans in to speak. The older tradition, taken straight from John 13:23, shows him leaning on Jesus' breast. That tenderness was carved again and again in the late Middle Ages, in the sculptures called a Johannesminne or Christus-Johannes-Gruppe. One of the most moving, from Swabia or the Lake Constance country early in the fourteenth century, is now in the Bayerisches Nationalmuseum in Munich: Christ sits quietly with a book; John, in a green robe, rests his head against the Lord's heart.

On the closed view of Matthias Grünewald's Isenheim Altarpiece (about 1512–16), painted for the hospital of the Antonite monks at Isenheim and now in the Musée Unterlinden in Colmar, John stands at the foot of a terrible and merciful cross. In a red robe he supports the Virgin as she faints; Mary Magdalene kneels; on the other side of the cross John the Baptist points to the Lamb and to the Crucified.

Hans Memling painted the two Saints John for the hospital that bore their name in Bruges. The central panel of his St John Altarpiece (1479), still in the Sint-Janshospitaal, shows the Virgin and Child between them: John the Baptist with his lamb at the left, and John the Evangelist at the right, in red, holding the chalice that later legend gave him.

The island of Patmos drew some of the finest pictures of all. Hieronymus Bosch, about 1489, seats a young John in a wide river landscape, a book on his knee, looking up at the woman clothed with the sun; an eagle watches at his feet, and one of Bosch's small, strange creatures crouches in the grass. The panel is in the Gemäldegalerie in Berlin. Titian and his workshop, about 1553–55, made a ceiling picture, now in the National Gallery of Art in Washington, in which an older John is thrown back with both arms raised, the eagle beside him, and God the Father appearing in the clouds. Diego Velázquez, as a young painter in Seville around 1618, gave us the vision that opens this page: John, still almost a youth, looks up from his book, the ink not yet dry, as the revelation breaks over him. That painting is now in the National Gallery in London.

El Greco, in the Apostolado painted about 1610–14 for Toledo and now in the Museo del Greco, gives us a very different John: tall, intense, in green and rose, holding the poisoned cup from which, in the old legend, a serpent or dragon rises as the apostle blesses it. And in Parma, in the abbey church of San Giovanni Evangelista, Correggio opened the dome (about 1520–24) as if the roof itself had been taken away. Christ ascends in a burst of gold light; the apostles sit on clouds around the rim, and John, in his own church, beholds the glory he had written of.

No artist has made the book of Revelation more unforgettable than Albrecht Dürer. In 1498, at Nuremberg, he published a series of fifteen large woodcuts of the Apocalypse. The most famous of them, The Four Horsemen, shows conquest, war, famine and death riding down upon the world, an angel above them, the people falling under the hooves. It is terrible, and it is faithful to the vision John wrote down; and it has taught countless readers to see that book with new eyes.

Path to sainthood

An apostle, venerated from the beginning. No formal canonisation (pre-Congregation).

Patronages

Writers, theologians, friendship.

Where to visit

The great stone church raised over John's tomb is now a ruin, and it is still a place of pilgrimage. At Selçuk, above ancient Ephesus, the emperor Justinian built a vast basilica in the sixth century on the traditional site of the apostle's grave. Marble columns still stand among the grass, and Ayasuluk fortress looks down from the hill. In the crossing, four spiral columns and a simple inscribed stone mark the tomb of St John.

On the island of Patmos, tradition points to the cave where John received the Revelation. The Cave of the Apocalypse is now a shrine, a white chapel built against the rock, with a mosaic of the vision over the door; it is a short walk below the fortified Monastery of Saint John the Theologian, founded in 1088 by St Christodoulos. Cave and monastery together are a UNESCO World Heritage site, and pilgrims still climb the path that joins them.

In Rome the cathedral of the Bishop of Rome, the Archbasilica of St John Lateran, is dedicated to Christ the Saviour and also to both Saints John, the Baptist and the Evangelist. The inscription on Alessandro Galilei's façade still says so: Christo Salvatori, and in honorem SS. Ioannis Baptistae et Evangelistae. It is the pope's own cathedral, omnium urbis et orbis ecclesiarum mater et caput, the mother and head of all the churches.

Nearby, just inside the Latin Gate, stands the much quieter church of San Giovanni a Porta Latina, an ancient dedication to the apostle, with a Romanesque campanile and a simple portico. A small oratory close by, San Giovanni in Oleo, marks the later legend that Domitian had John plunged into boiling oil before the Latin Gate, from which he emerged unharmed, and was then exiled to Patmos. The story is legend, not history; the little church keeps the memory of it.

In Parma the abbey church of San Giovanni Evangelista holds Correggio's dome, already shown above: a whole sky opened for the apostle whose Gospel begins in heaven.

Trivia

  • By tradition he is the only one of the Twelve who was not martyred, dying of old age.
  • A legend says he was handed a poisoned cup and blessed it, and the poison left as a serpent; that's why he is often painted holding a chalice with a snake.
  • His symbol among the four evangelists is the eagle, because his Gospel soars into theology from its first line, "In the beginning was the Word".
