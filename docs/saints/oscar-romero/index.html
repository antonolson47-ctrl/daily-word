St Óscar RomeroThe archbishop shot at the altar

Archbishop Óscar Romero (1978), Archdiocese of San Salvador · public domain, via Wikimedia Commons

Lived1917–1980, Ciudad Barrios–San Salvador
Feast day24 March
RecognisedCanonised 14 October 2018 (Francis), as a martyr
PlaceSan Salvador, El Salvador

His story

Óscar Arnulfo Romero y Galdámez was born on 15 August 1917 at Ciudad Barrios, in the hills of El Salvador. He was a carpenter's apprentice at thirteen and then a seminarian, ordained in Rome in 1942. He came home a careful, pious, slightly anxious priest, good at administration, uneasy with politics. He was auxiliary bishop, then bishop of Santiago de María, and in February 1977 he was installed as archbishop of San Salvador. The oligarchy and the nuncio's circle thought he was a safe choice. A conservative intellectual, they said, who talked about holiness. He was.

Three weeks later, on 12 March 1977, his friend Rutilio Grande, a Jesuit who worked with landless farmworkers, was shot dead on the road to El Paisnal with an old man and a boy. Romero stood over the bodies in the cathedral. He said later that looking at Rutilio he understood he would have to walk the same road. He cancelled the city's Masses the next Sunday and celebrated one Mass in the cathedral, which in San Salvador was read as a provocation. It was also a funeral.

El Salvador was already in the years that became the civil war: death squads, disappearances, a landless majority, a military that did not distinguish a catechist from a guerrilla. Romero's Sunday homilies on the archdiocesan radio, YSAX, listed the week's dead by name. More people listened than to any other programme in the country. He angered the right, and he would not bless the armed left either. He wrote to President Carter in February 1980 asking the United States to stop sending military aid. He told a reporter, days before he died, that if he were killed he would rise in the Salvadoran people. On 23 March 1980, in the cathedral, he spoke straight to the conscripts: no soldier is obliged to obey an order against the law of God. "In the name of God, then, and in the name of this suffering people whose cries rise to heaven more loudly each day, I beg you, I beseech you, I order you in the name of God: stop the repression."

The next evening, 24 March, he was at a priests' meeting and then at Mass in the chapel of the Hospital Divina Providencia, a small cancer hospital where he lived in a bungalow behind the wards. The homily was on the grain of wheat that falls into the ground. He finished, stepped to the altar for the offertory, and a gunman in the door of the chapel shot him once through the heart. The United Nations Commission on the Truth for El Salvador reported in 1993 that Major Roberto D'Aubuisson, founder of the death squads and of the party that later governed, had ordered the killing. D'Aubuisson was never convicted.

Romero was buried in the crypt of the Metropolitan Cathedral. At the funeral, bombs and gunfire killed dozens in the square. The crypt is still the pilgrimage. So is the chapel at the hospital, with the small rooms where he slept.

Path to sainthood

The cause was delayed for years, partly because Rome had been warned that he was a political bishop. Francis beatified him in San Salvador on 23 May 2015 and canonised him in Rome on 14 October 2018, as a martyr, together with Paul VI and others. At that Mass the pope wore the bloodstained cincture Romero had on when he was shot. His optional memorial is 24 March.

Patronages

El Salvador, the Americas in the company of other patrons, persecuted Christians, and pastoral workers who have to say in public what they have heard in private. Caritas and justice offices invoke him constantly. He was a diocesan bishop, not the founder of a movement.

Trivia

  • The 1978 photograph, from the archdiocese, is the one on the banners: glasses, pectoral cross, a face that still looks as if it would rather be in a library.
  • His radio station was blown up more than once. He kept a spare transmitter.
  • The sentence from 23 March is on walls all over San Salvador. It was a Sunday homily, the day before he died, not the short homily at the hospital. The hospital homily is the one about the grain of wheat. Both are his.
