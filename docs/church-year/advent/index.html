History

The word comes from the Latin adventus, "coming". A season of preparation before Christmas appears in Gaul and Spain in the late 4th and 5th centuries: the Council of Saragossa (380) asked the faithful to be in church daily from 17 December to Epiphany, and Bishop Perpetuus of Tours (d. 490) set a fast from St Martin's Day (11 November), which was nicknamed "St Martin's Lent". Rome kept a shorter season, and Pope Gregory the Great (590–604) is credited with fixing it at four Sundays, the form the West still keeps (Catholic Encyclopedia, "Advent").

Why it is observed

Advent looks two ways. It prepares for the celebration of Christ's first coming at Bethlehem, and it turns the Church toward his promised return in glory. That is why the first Sunday's Gospel is about the end of the age, the middle Sundays belong to John the Baptist, and the fourth Sunday finally arrives at Mary and the Annunciation. The mood is expectant and hopeful, more like waiting at a window than fasting in the desert.

Colours and symbols

Advent wreath: an evergreen circle (eternity, life that does not die) with four candles, three violet and one rose, lit one more each Sunday; a white Christ candle is often added at Christmas. The wreath began in Lutheran Germany: Johann Hinrich Wichern lit candles on a cartwheel for the boys of his Hamburg mission school in 1839. O Antiphons: seven ancient antiphons sung at Vespers from 17 to 23 December ("O Wisdom", "O Root of Jesse", "O Key of David"…) that became the hymn O come, O come, Emmanuel. Jesse Tree, Advent calendar, Rorate Masses by candlelight before dawn.

Catholic practice

Violet vestments, rose on Gaudete Sunday (3rd). The Gloria is omitted at Sunday Mass so that it sounds new at Christmas. Weekday readings follow a fixed pattern: Isaiah's promises through 16 December, then from 17 December the Gospels of Jesus' ancestry and birth. Feasts inside Advent include St Andrew (30 Nov), St Nicholas (6 Dec), the Immaculate Conception (8 Dec, a holy day of obligation in the US), St Juan Diego (9 Dec), Our Lady of Guadalupe (12 Dec) and St Lucy (13 Dec).

Lutheran practice

ELCA congregations mostly use blue (hope) rather than violet (penitence), a practice confirmed in Evangelical Lutheran Worship (2006); the LCMS permits both. The Revised Common Lectionary (ELCA) and the LCMS three-year lectionary run close to the Catholic readings. Luther's hymn Savior of the Nations, Come (Nun komm, der Heiden Heiland, 1524), his translation of St Ambrose's 4th-century Veni redemptor gentium, is the classic Lutheran Advent hymn.
