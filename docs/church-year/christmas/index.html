When25 December to the Baptism of the Lord (Catholic; 10 January 2027) or to 5 January, the Twelve Days (Lutheran).

History

The earliest firm evidence for a feast of Christ's birth on 25 December is the Roman Chronograph of 354, whose list of martyrs (drawn up c. 336) begins: "VIII kal. Ian. natus Christus in Betleem Iudeae". The East kept 6 January for both birth and Epiphany, and gradually took up 25 December in the late 4th century; St John Chrysostom preached in Antioch in 386 that the December feast had reached them only a few years before. The date may come from calculation (the conception reckoned on 25 March, nine months earlier) as much as from any Roman festival (Catholic Encyclopedia, "Christmas").

Why it is observed

The Word became flesh (John 1:14). Christmas celebrates that God did not save the world from a distance but entered it as a child. The season holds a cluster of feasts that look at the Incarnation from different sides: St Stephen (the first martyr), St John the Evangelist, the Holy Innocents, the Holy Family, Mary Mother of God / the Name of Jesus (1 January).

Colours and symbols

Crib or crèche (St Francis staged the first living one at Greccio in 1223), the Christmas tree (German and Lutheran custom popularised in the 16th–19th centuries), candles and lights (Christ the light of the world), three Masses on Christmas Day in the Roman tradition: at night, at dawn and during the day.

Catholic practice

Christmas is a solemnity with an octave; 1 January, the Solemnity of Mary, Mother of God, closes the octave and is a holy day of obligation. The season ends with the Baptism of the Lord. Midnight Mass is one of the most attended liturgies of the year.

Lutheran practice

Lutherans keep the Twelve Days to 5 January. The RCL appoints three sets of propers for Christmas Eve and Day, and 1 January is kept as the Name of Jesus. Luther's carol From Heaven Above to Earth I Come (Vom Himmel hoch, 1535) was written for his own family's Christmas Eve.
