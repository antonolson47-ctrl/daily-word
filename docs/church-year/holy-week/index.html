History

The fullest early account comes from Egeria, a pilgrim (probably from Spain or Gaul) who kept Holy Week in Jerusalem around 381–384 and wrote it down for her sisters at home: a procession with palm and olive branches down the Mount of Olives, the night watch at Gethsemane, the veneration of the wood of the Cross on Friday, and the great Paschal vigil. Pilgrims carried these customs home and they shaped every later liturgy (Catholic Encyclopedia, "Holy Week").

Why it is observed

Holy Week walks day by day through Jesus' last days: his entry into Jerusalem, the Last Supper and washing of feet, his arrest, trial and crucifixion, and the silence of the tomb. The Triduum ("three days") is one celebration spread over three days, the high point of the whole year.

Colours and symbols

Palms, the washing of feet (mandatum, whence "Maundy" Thursday), stripping of the altar, the bare cross venerated on Good Friday, the new fire and Paschal candle at the Easter Vigil, with the ancient Exsultet.

Catholic practice

Palm Sunday reads the whole Passion. On Holy Thursday morning the bishop blesses the holy oils at the Chrism Mass; in the evening the Mass of the Lord's Supper ends with the Eucharist carried to an altar of repose. Good Friday has no Mass: the Passion of John, the solemn intercessions, veneration of the Cross and Communion. The Easter Vigil is "the mother of all vigils" (St Augustine), with baptisms.

Lutheran practice

Lutherans keep the same shape: Sunday of the Passion, Maundy Thursday with confession and the Lord's Supper and the stripping of the altar, Good Friday (often Tenebrae, with candles put out one by one), and a growing return to the Easter Vigil. Bach's Passions were written for Good Friday Vespers in Leipzig.
