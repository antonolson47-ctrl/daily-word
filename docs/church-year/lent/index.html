History

A fast before Easter is very ancient. The Council of Nicaea (325) already speaks of "the forty days" (tessarakoste), and St Athanasius's Festal Letters of the 330s urge the Egyptian Church to keep them. The number recalls Moses on Sinai, Elijah's journey to Horeb and above all Christ's forty days in the wilderness. Lent was also the final preparation of catechumens for baptism at Easter, which is why its Gospels (the Samaritan woman, the man born blind, Lazarus) are baptismal. Ashes on the first day were at first given to public penitents; by the 11th century everyone received them (Catholic Encyclopedia, "Lent").

Why it is observed

Lent is forty days of return: prayer, fasting and almsgiving (Matthew 6), to clear room for God and for the poor, and to walk with Christ toward his Passion. Pope St Leo the Great's Lenten sermons (5th century) describe it as a yearly renewal of the whole Church.

Colours and symbols

Ashes ("Remember that you are dust", Genesis 3:19), the purple of penitence, the Alleluia buried until Easter, veiled crosses from the 5th Sunday, Stations of the Cross, rose vestments on Laetare (4th) Sunday, and the Lenten fish Friday.

Catholic practice

Ash Wednesday and Good Friday are days of fasting and abstinence for adults; Fridays of Lent are days of abstinence from meat. The Gloria and Alleluia are silent. RCIA candidates are enrolled and pass through the "Scrutinies" on the 3rd, 4th and 5th Sundays (with Year A readings).

Lutheran practice

Lutherans keep Ash Wednesday with imposition of ashes, midweek Lenten services (often working through the Passion or the Catechism), and fasting as a freely chosen discipline. Luther kept fasting as a good outward training while warning that it earns nothing before God.
