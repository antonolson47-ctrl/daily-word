Verse a Day #37 · November 17, 2026

Matthew 5:14

Alignment: Very close across Greek, Latin and English

Setting the scene

Jesus has gone up into a mountain in Galilee. "When he was set, his disciples came unto him: and he opened his mouth, and taught them" (Matthew 5:1-2). He has just spoken the eight Beatitudes, blessing the poor in spirit, the mourners, the meek, the merciful, the pure in heart, the peacemakers and the persecuted. Then he turns to the small company in front of him, fishermen and ordinary folk from the lakeside villages, and tells them who they are: "Ye are the salt of the earth" (5:13), and in the next breath, "Ye are the light of the world." Around the Sea of Galilee, towns sat on the hillsides, and after dark their lamps could be seen from far across the water. A city set on a hill cannot be hid.

Four witnesses

Greek · SBLGNT
Ὑμεῖς ἐστε τὸ φῶς τοῦ κόσμου. οὐ δύναται πόλις κρυβῆναι ἐπάνω ὄρους κειμένη·
Hymeis este to phōs tou kosmou. ou dynatai polis krybēnai epanō orous keimenē.
Latin · Vulgate
Vos estis lux mundi. Non potest civitas abscondi supra montem posita.
KJV
Ye are the light of the world. A city that is set on an hill cannot be hid.
ESV
You are the light of the world. A city set on a hill cannot be hidden.

Key words

  • hymeis este → vos estis → ye are / you are. The pronoun is emphatic and plural: "You, and no one else, are." Jesus does not say "try to become" or "you ought to be." He says what his disciples already are because they belong to him. The same emphatic "ye are" opened the salt saying in verse 13.
  • to phōs tou kosmou → lux mundi → the light of the world. Phōs is light itself, the light God spoke on the first day (Genesis 1:3 in the Greek Old Testament). Later Jesus will say of himself, "I am the light of the world" (John 8:12; 9:5). His disciples are light as the moon is light: they shine because they face the Sun. Kosmos, "world," reaches past Israel to all the nations.
  • polis … epanō orous keimenē → civitas supra montem posita → a city set on a hill. Keimenē means "lying, set, situated." Greek and Latin both say "mountain" (oros, mons), the same word used for the mountain where Jesus is sitting (5:1). Many hearers have pictured Jerusalem on Mount Zion, the city on the hill to which the prophets said the nations would come.
  • ou dynatai … krybēnai → non potest … abscondi → cannot be hid. A passive infinitive: a city on a height is not able to be hidden, whether it wishes to be or not. Verses 15-16 draw the lesson: no one lights a lamp to put it under a bushel, so "let your light so shine before men, that they may see your good works, and glorify your Father which is in heaven."

Alignment

Very close across Greek, Latin and English. Every Greek edition on Bible Hub (Nestle 1904, Westcott–Hort with NA27/UBS4, the Byzantine Majority Text, the Greek Orthodox text, Tischendorf and both Textus Receptus editions) agrees word for word, differing only in punctuation. The Vulgate follows the Greek almost word for word: vos estis lux mundi. The one small shift is in English: Greek oros and Latin mons mean "mountain," which the Douay-Rheims keeps ("a city seated on a mountain"), while the KJV and ESV soften it to "hill."

The story

St John Chrysostom, preaching on the Sermon on the Mount in his Homilies on the Gospel of Matthew (Homily 15), marveled that Jesus said "the world," not one city or one nation, to a handful of poor fishermen, and taught that their lives were to be so bright that no one could miss them. Centuries later the phrase crossed the ocean. In 1630, aboard the Arbella on the way to New England, John Winthrop told his fellow settlers in "A Model of Christian Charity" that "we shall be as a city upon a hill, the eyes of all people are upon us." Presidents from John F. Kennedy to Ronald Reagan echoed his words. But the light in the Lord's saying is never our own: in verse 16 it shines so that people will "glorify your Father which is in heaven." At every baptism the Church gives the newly baptized a candle lit from the Easter candle, the sign of Christ the Light, entrusted to a disciple who is now light in the Lord (Ephesians 5:8).

Dig Deeper: Roots in Hebrew & Jewish Sources

"Light of the world" was not a new phrase to Jewish ears. The prophet Isaiah heard God call his Servant to be "a light to the nations" (Hebrew l'or goyim, לְאוֹר גּוֹיִם; Isaiah 42:6 and 49:6), and the Greek Septuagint renders it eis phōs ethnōn, using the very word phōs that Jesus uses here. Isaiah also saw "the mountain of the LORD's house" raised above the hills, with all nations streaming to it (2:2-3), and he sang to Jerusalem, "nations shall walk by thy light" (60:3). That is the picture behind a city set on a mountain: Zion, shining for the whole earth. Later rabbinic teaching kept the same image. The midrash Bereshit Rabbah (compiled around the 5th century AD, well after the Gospels) says, "Jerusalem is the light of the world," quoting Isaiah 60:3. Before Christ, the Dead Sea Scrolls community called its members "the sons of light," set against "the sons of darkness" (in the War Scroll and the Community Rule). Jesus takes all of this and gives it to his small band of disciples: through them, joined to him who is the Light, Israel's calling to enlighten the nations goes out to the world. Dig Deeper is background study, not new Scripture, and not an endorsement of any later theology.

Where to dig

  1. Isaiah 49 in Hebrew and English, on Sefaria. The Servant song where God says, "I will give thee for a light to the Gentiles" (49:6), l'or goyim in Hebrew.
  2. Isaiah 49 in the Greek Septuagint, on Bible Hub. The Greek Old Testament of the apostles' day, where verse 6 reads eis phōs ethnōn, "for a light of the nations."
  3. Bereshit Rabbah 59:5, on Sefaria. A later rabbinic midrash that calls Jerusalem "the light of the world" and cites Isaiah 60:3.
  4. Matthew 5:14 Greek interlinear, on Bible Hub. Word by word through the verse, so you can see hymeis, phōs, kosmou and polis in place.
