Verse a Day #42 · November 22, 2026

Matthew 22:37

Alignment: Very close

Setting the scene

It is the last week of Jesus' earthly life, and the Temple courts in Jerusalem have become a battlefield of questions. Only days before, he rode into the city to shouts of "Hosanna" (21:9) and drove the money changers from the Temple (21:12). Now the leaders come at him one group after another, each trying to trap him. The Pharisees and the Herodians ask about paying taxes to Caesar, and he silences them with a coin (22:15-22). The Sadducees, who deny the resurrection, spin a riddle about a woman with seven husbands, and he silences them too: God "is not the God of the dead, but of the living" (22:32). When the Pharisees hear that the Sadducees have been put to silence, they gather together, and one of them, a lawyer, an expert in the Law of Moses, steps forward to test him: "Master, which is the great commandment in the law?" (22:36). Jewish teachers loved to debate how the many commandments of the Torah fit together. Jesus does not reach for something new or clever. He reaches for the words every faithful Jew prayed morning and evening, the Shema of Deuteronomy 6, and he sets them at the very top of everything God has ever asked of his people.

Key words

  • agapēseis → diliges → thou shalt love. The future tense, "you shall love," is the voice of a covenant command, just like the Greek of Deuteronomy. Agapaō is the love that chooses, gives and stays faithful, the same verb as "God so loved the world" (John 3:16). Jerome chose diliges, from diligo, "to esteem, to choose out and cherish," the root of our word "diligent." Love for God is not a passing feeling; it is a whole life pointed toward him.
  • kyrion ton Theon sou → Dominum Deum tuum → the Lord thy God. Not a distant power but "your God," the God who bound himself to his people by covenant. In the Hebrew of Deuteronomy, "the Lord" stands for God's holy name, YHWH, which the Greek renders kyrios.
  • kardia → corde → heart. In the Bible the heart is not only the seat of emotion but the center of the whole person: will, choices, thoughts and desires. It is where God writes his law (Jeremiah 31:33).
  • psychē → anima → soul. Psychē means life itself, the breathing, living self. The same word appears when Jesus says, "whosoever will lose his life (psychē) for my sake shall find it" (Matthew 16:25). To love God with all your soul is to love him with your very life.
  • dianoia → mente → mind. Dianoia means understanding, the thinking mind, the way a person reasons and imagines. The Hebrew of Deuteronomy 6:5 has me'od, "might" or "muchness"; Matthew's "mind" makes sure no one thinks love of God leaves the intellect behind. Mark (12:30) and Luke (10:27) give four terms, heart, soul, mind and strength.
  • en holē → ex toto / in tota → with all. Said three times. Holos means whole, entire, undivided. God does not ask for a portion; he asks for everything, because he gives everything.

Alignment

Very close. All the Greek texts on Bible Hub agree on the words of the commandment; the only differences are in the opening phrase and a single article: the Byzantine Majority Text, the Greek Orthodox text and both Textus Receptus editions name "Jesus" (Ἰησοῦς) as the speaker, the Textus Receptus uses εἶπεν ("said") instead of ἔφη, and Westcott–Hort and the Majority Text drop "the" (τῇ) before "heart" (bracketed in NA27/UBS4). The KJV follows the Textus Receptus with "Jesus said unto him"; the ESV follows the critical text with "he said to him." The Vulgate also names Jesus and is word for word, with one small turn of phrase: "ex toto corde" (out of the whole heart) for the first item, then "in tota anima" and "in tota mente." Interestingly, the Aramaic Peshitta lists four things, heart, soul, strength and mind, like Mark's Gospel.

The story

St. Augustine of Hippo, around AD 397, made this verse and its partner, "Thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself" (22:39), the master key for reading the whole Bible. In his book On Christian Doctrine he wrote: "Whoever, then, thinks that he understands the Holy Scriptures, or any part of them, but puts such an interpretation upon them as does not tend to build up this twofold love of God and our neighbor, does not yet understand them as he ought." Jesus himself said the same thing in the very next breath: "On these two commandments hang all the law and the prophets" (22:40). Jerome's Latin pictures it beautifully: universa lex pendet, the whole Law hangs on these two, like a door on its hinges. And there is something tender here too. When Jesus answered the lawyer, he was quoting the words he himself had prayed since boyhood in Nazareth, the Shema that Jewish families still recite at rising up and lying down.

Dig Deeper: Roots in Hebrew & Jewish Sources

When Jesus answered the lawyer, he did not invent a new law; he quoted the heart of Israel's daily prayer. Deuteronomy 6:4-5 begins, "Hear, O Israel (Shema Yisrael): the LORD our God is one LORD," and continues, "And thou shalt love the LORD thy God with all thine heart, and with all thy soul, and with all thy might" (Hebrew ve-ahavta et YHWH Eloheikha bekhol levavkha uvekhol nafshekha uvekhol me'odekha). Every person in that Temple crowd would have known these words by heart, because Deuteronomy itself commands that they be spoken at home and on the way, at lying down and at rising up (6:7). We can even hold a pre-Christian copy in our imagination: the Nash Papyrus, a small Hebrew sheet from Egypt dated to the second century BC, carries the Ten Commandments followed by the Shema, possibly from a set of tefillin used in daily prayer, much like the tefillin later found among the Dead Sea Scrolls at Qumran. Centuries after Jesus, the Mishnah (compiled around AD 200) preserved a beautiful rabbinic reading of this verse: "with all your heart" means with both your good and evil inclinations; "with all your soul" means even if God takes your life; "with all your might" means with all your possessions. Those rabbinic words were written down after the Gospels, so they are not where Matthew got his ideas; they show how seriously Jesus' own people took this commandment. Against that background, his answer shines: the love God asked of Israel from Sinai is the very love he calls us to now, and in him we see that love lived out completely. Dig Deeper is background study, not new Scripture, and not an endorsement of any later theology.

Where to dig

  1. Deuteronomy 6:5 in Hebrew and English, on Sefaria. The Shema's great command, "you shall love the LORD your God," exactly the words Jesus quoted.
  2. The Nash Papyrus (MS Or.233), Cambridge University Digital Library. A second-century BC Hebrew sheet with the Ten Commandments and the Shema, older than Jesus and one of the oldest known Hebrew biblical texts outside the Dead Sea Scrolls.
  3. Mishnah Berakhot 9:5, on Sefaria. The rabbinic explanation of "heart," "soul" and "might," with Hebrew original and English.
  4. Strong's G1271, dianoia ("mind"), on Blue Letter Bible. The Greek word behind "with all thy mind," with its meanings and every New Testament verse where it appears.
