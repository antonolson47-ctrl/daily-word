Verse a Day #8 · October 19, 2026

Matthew 1:23

Alignment: Very close, with the real question one step upstream in Hebrew

Setting the scene

Nazareth, a small farming village in the hills of Galilee, around 6–4 BC, while Herod the Great still rules Judea. Joseph, a builder, is betrothed to a young woman named Mary. In Jewish custom a betrothal was already a binding marriage contract; only a divorce could end it, even before the couple lived together. Then Mary is found to be with child, and Joseph, "a just man," plans to end things quietly so she will not be shamed (Matthew 1:19). An angel stops him in a dream: the child is from the Holy Spirit, and his name will be Jesus, "for he shall save his people from their sins" (1:21). Matthew then steps back and tells his readers what this means. He reaches about 700 years into Israel's past, to the prophet Isaiah standing before King Ahaz of Judah around 735 BC, when Jerusalem was terrified of an invasion by the kings of Aram and Israel. Isaiah offered the king a sign: a child called Immanuel, "God with us." Matthew says that sign comes to its full meaning in the baby Joseph will name Jesus.

Four witnesses

Greek · SBLGNT
Ἰδοὺ ἡ παρθένος ἐν γαστρὶ ἕξει καὶ τέξεται υἱόν, καὶ καλέσουσιν τὸ ὄνομα αὐτοῦ Ἐμμανουήλ, ὅ ἐστιν μεθερμηνευόμενον Μεθ’ ἡμῶν ὁ Θεός.
Idou hē parthenos en gastri hexei kai texetai huion, kai kalesousin to onoma autou Emmanouēl, ho estin methermēneuomenon Meth' hēmōn ho Theos.
Latin · Vulgate
Ecce virgo in utero habebit, et pariet filium: et vocabunt nomen ejus Emmanuel, quod est interpretatum Nobiscum Deus.
KJV
Behold, a virgin shall be with child, and shall bring forth a son, and they shall call his name Emmanuel, which being interpreted is, God with us.
ESV
"Behold, the virgin shall conceive and bear a son, and they shall call his name Immanuel" (which means, God with us).

Key words

  • hē parthenos → virgo → the virgin / a virgin. Parthenos is the ordinary Greek word for a virgin or unmarried girl, and Jerome's virgo means the same. Matthew did not pick it himself: it is the word the Septuagint, the Greek translation of the Hebrew Bible made by Jewish scholars in Egypt before Christ, had already used in Isaiah 7:14. The Greek has the article ("the virgin"), which the ESV keeps and the KJV drops ("a virgin").
  • ʿalmāh (Hebrew, Isaiah 7:14) → parthenos → virgo. Isaiah's Hebrew word means a young woman of marriageable age, a maiden. Hebrew has a more specific word for virgin, bᵉtûlāh, and that is why this verse has been argued over for centuries. But ʿalmāh is used of Rebekah before her marriage (Genesis 24:43), whom the same chapter calls a virgin (24:16), and Jewish translators chose parthenos for it long before Jesus was born.
  • en gastri hexei → in utero habebit → shall be with child / shall conceive. Literally "she will have in the womb." The Latin follows the Greek word for word.
  • Emmanouēl → Emmanuel → Immanuel. The name is Hebrew, ʿimmānû ʾēl: ʿimmānû "with us," ʾēl "God." The KJV spelling Emmanuel follows the Greek and Latin; the ESV's Immanuel follows the Hebrew.
  • methermēneuomenon → interpretatum → being interpreted / which means. Matthew translates the Hebrew name for Greek-speaking readers, a small sign that his Gospel was meant to travel beyond Hebrew and Aramaic speakers.
  • Meth' hēmōn ho Theos → Nobiscum Deus → God with us. Matthew's whole Gospel is framed by this promise: it opens with "God with us" here and closes with the risen Jesus saying, "I am with you always, to the end of the age" (Matthew 28:20).

Alignment

Very close across Greek, Latin and English; the real question sits one step upstream, in Hebrew. All three agree word for word on the virgin, the son, the name and its meaning. The small differences are the article (ESV "the virgin" follows the Greek; KJV has "a virgin") and the spelling of the name. The famous debate is between Isaiah's Hebrew ʿalmāh, "young woman," and the Greek parthenos, "virgin," which Matthew inherited from the Jewish Septuagint.

The story

Matthew quotes the Septuagint almost exactly, with one change. The Greek Isaiah says "you shall call his name Emmanuel," speaking to King Ahaz, and the Hebrew reads "she shall call his name." Matthew writes "they shall call," turning the naming into something all of God's people do. The Great Isaiah Scroll from Qumran, copied about 125 BC, shows this prophecy was already old, treasured Scripture more than a century before the angel spoke to Joseph. And "Emmanuel" lives on in Advent: the hymn "O Come, O Come, Emmanuel" grew out of the medieval "O Antiphons," Latin chants sung in the last days before Christmas, the last of which begins "O Emmanuel."

Dig Deeper: Roots in Hebrew & Jewish Sources

Matthew tells us outright that he is quoting an older text: "All this was done, that it might be fulfilled which was spoken of the Lord by the prophet" (Matthew 1:22). So this verse, more than most, sends us back to Israel's own Scriptures. The prophecy is Isaiah 7:14, spoken to King Ahaz around 735 BC. In Hebrew it reads:

לָכֵן יִתֵּן אֲדֹנָי הוּא לָכֶם אוֹת הִנֵּה הָעַלְמָה הָרָה וְיֹלֶדֶת בֵּן וְקָרָאת שְׁמוֹ עִמָּנוּ אֵל

"Therefore the Lord himself will give you a sign: behold, the young woman (ha-ʿalmāh) is with child and will bear a son, and she will call his name Immanuel." Between that Hebrew and Matthew's Greek stands the Septuagint, the Greek translation of the Hebrew Bible made by Jewish scholars in the last centuries before Christ, which gave Matthew the word parthenos, "virgin." Seeing that chain for yourself, Hebrew prophet, Jewish Greek translation, then Matthew, is the reason to dig here. The links below are background study, not Scripture, and including them is not an endorsement of any later theology. Watch the dates: the Great Isaiah Scroll from the Dead Sea was copied before Jesus was born, so it cannot be a Christian edit. The Mishnah (about AD 200), the Talmud (about AD 400–600) and medieval commentators such as Rashi (11th century), who read Isaiah's sign as fulfilled in the prophet's own day, all come after the New Testament and show later Jewish reading, not sources Matthew used.

Where to dig

  1. Isaiah 7:14 in Hebrew and English, on Sefaria. Sefaria's free library of Jewish texts shows the verse side by side in Hebrew and English, with links to classic Jewish commentaries on it.
  2. The Great Isaiah Scroll, Israel Museum Digital Dead Sea Scrolls. You can browse the real scroll (1QIsaᵃ, Qumran Cave 1, about 125 BC) in high resolution, all 66 chapters of Isaiah, copied about a century before Jesus. The Israel Antiquities Authority's Leon Levy Dead Sea Scrolls Digital Library holds the wider scroll archive.
  3. Matthew 1:23 interlinear, on Bible Hub. This shows Matthew's Greek word by word, including parthenos (Strong's 3933), next to the English.
  4. ʿalmāh in the Blue Letter Bible Hebrew lexicon. Here you'll find the lexicon entry for Strong's 5959 and every other place the word appears in the Hebrew Bible, so you can judge its range of meaning yourself.

A quick timeline, so the sources don't blur together

Source Date What it is
Isaiah (Tanakh / Old Testament) about 740–700 BC The Hebrew prophecy to King Ahaz, the root text
Septuagint (LXX) 3rd–2nd century BC Jewish Greek translation of the Hebrew Bible, which gives parthenos
Great Isaiah Scroll (Dead Sea Scrolls) copied about 125 BC Oldest near-complete Isaiah, a pre-Christian witness
Gospel of Matthew 1st century AD Quotes Isaiah 7:14 of Jesus: "God with us"
Mishnah / Talmud about AD 200 / AD 400–600 Later rabbinic teaching, not a source Matthew used
