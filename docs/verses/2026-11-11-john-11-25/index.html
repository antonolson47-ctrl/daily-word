Verse a Day #31 · November 11, 2026

John 11:25

Alignment: Very close across Greek, Latin and English

Setting the scene

Bethany is a small village on the far slope of the Mount of Olives, about two miles from Jerusalem. Jesus loved the family who lived there: Martha, Mary and their brother Lazarus (John 11:5). When Lazarus fell sick, the sisters sent word, "Lord, behold, he whom thou lovest is sick" (11:3). Jesus waited two more days, and by the time he arrived Lazarus had been in the tomb four days. Mourners had come out from Jerusalem. Martha ran to meet Jesus on the road and said, "Lord, if thou hadst been here, my brother had not died." Jesus told her, "Thy brother shall rise again," and Martha, a faithful Jewish believer, answered that she knew he would rise "in the resurrection at the last day" (11:21-24). Jesus' reply moves the hope from a distant day to his own Person. Minutes later he will stand at the tomb, weep, and call, "Lazarus, come forth."

Four witnesses

eipen autē ho Iēsous, Egō eimi hē anastasis kai hē zōē; ho pisteuōn eis eme kan apothanē zēsetai.
Latin · Vulgate
Dixit ei Jesus: Ego sum resurrectio et vita: qui credit in me, etiam si mortuus fuerit, vivet.
KJV
Jesus said unto her, I am the resurrection, and the life: he that believeth in me, though he were dead, yet shall he live:
ESV
Jesus said to her, "I am the resurrection and the life. Whoever believes in me, though he die, yet shall he live,"

Key words

  • egō eimi → ego sum → I am. The fifth of the seven great "I am" sayings in John (bread, light, door, shepherd, resurrection, way, vine). Jesus does not say "I will bring the resurrection" but "I am the resurrection." The power over death is not a tool he uses; it is who he is.
  • anastasis → resurrectio → resurrection. From ana ("up, again") and histēmi ("to stand"): a "standing up again." Young's Literal renders it "the rising again." Jerome's resurrectio comes from resurgo, "rise again," the same verb Martha uses in the Latin of verse 24 (resurget), and it gives English its word.
  • zōē → vita → life. Greek has bios for the length and manner of a life and zōē for life itself, the life God has. John uses zōē for the life that is "in him" from the beginning (John 1:4). Jesus is not only the One who raises the dead; he is the life they are raised into.
  • pisteuōn eis eme → qui credit in me → he that believeth in me. A present participle, "the one who keeps on believing," and the preposition eis, literally "into me." Faith in John is not only agreeing with facts but entrusting oneself to a Person.
  • kan apothanē → etiam si mortuus fuerit → though he were dead / though he die. "Even if he dies." Jesus does not deny that believers die; Lazarus has. He denies that death has the last word.
  • zēsetai → vivet → shall live. A simple future: he will live. The next verse adds, "whosoever liveth and believeth in me shall never die. Believest thou this?" (11:26).

Alignment

Very close across Greek, Latin and English. Nestle 1904, Westcott–Hort, Tischendorf, the Byzantine Majority Text and both Textus Receptus editions read the same words, differing only in capital letters and punctuation. A few early witnesses, among them the third-century papyrus P45 and the Sinaitic Syriac, lack "and the life," but the great majority of manuscripts include it, and all the major modern and traditional translations do. Jerome follows the Greek word for word. One small difference shows the Latin's influence on English: Jerome's etiam si mortuus fuerit ("even if he shall have died") lies behind the KJV and Douay-Rheims "though he were dead," while the ESV's "though he die" and the NASB's "even if he dies" stay closer to the Greek subjunctive apothanē. All say the same thing: death cannot hold those who belong to Christ.

The story

For nearly five hundred years, many English-speaking Christians have first heard this verse at a graveside. When Thomas Cranmer prepared the Book of Common Prayer in 1549, he began the Order for the Burial of the Dead with the priest meeting the body at the entrance of the churchyard and saying, "I am the resurrection and the life, saith the Lord: he that believeth in me, though he were dead, yet shall he live." The words are still said at the start of funerals in many churches today, spoken over the coffin before anything else. And the village itself remembers the miracle: Bethany is known today by the Arabic name al-Eizariya, from the name of Lazarus, and visitors still descend the steps into the rock-cut tomb shown as his.

Dig Deeper: Roots in Hebrew & Jewish Sources

Martha's answer just before this verse, "I know that he shall rise again in the resurrection at the last day" (John 11:24), is a Jewish hope with deep roots in the Hebrew Scriptures. Isaiah sang, "Thy dead men shall live, together with my dead body shall they arise" (Isaiah 26:19), and Daniel was told, "many of them that sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake, some to everlasting life" (Daniel 12:2). In Jesus' day the Pharisees held to this hope while the Sadducees denied it (Acts 23:8), so Martha was speaking the faith of her people. Among the Dead Sea Scrolls, a Hebrew text copied before the time of Christ, known as the Messianic Apocalypse (4Q521), praises the Lord who will heal the wounded and give life to the dead in the days of his anointed one; it is background, and the Scrolls are not Scripture. Later, the Mishnah (compiled about AD 200) counts denial of the resurrection of the dead among the beliefs that cost a person his share in the world to come, which shows how central the hope remained in Jewish teaching. What is new in John 11:25 is not the hope of resurrection but the Person: Jesus does not only promise the last day, he says the resurrection is standing in front of Martha. Dig Deeper here is background study, not new Scripture, and not an endorsement of any later theology.

Where to dig

  1. Daniel 12 in Hebrew and English, on Sefaria. The clearest Old Testament promise that those who sleep in the dust will awake to everlasting life, the hope Martha voices in John 11:24.
  2. 4Q521 Messianic Apocalypse, Leon Levy Dead Sea Scrolls Digital Library. Images of a pre-Christian scroll from Qumran that links the Lord's coming anointed one with healing and raising the dead.
  3. Mishnah Sanhedrin 10:1, on Sefaria. The later rabbinic teaching that "all Israel have a share in the world to come," except those who deny the resurrection of the dead.
  4. Blue Letter Bible lexicon for ἀνάστασις (anastasis). Every New Testament use of the word "resurrection," so you can trace it from Martha's home to the empty tomb and 1 Corinthians 15.
