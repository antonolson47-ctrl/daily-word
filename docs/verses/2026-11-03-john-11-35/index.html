Verse a Day #23 · November 3, 2026

John 11:35

Alignment: Exact

Setting the scene

Bethany, a village about two miles from Jerusalem on the slope of the Mount of Olives, a few weeks before the last Passover. Jesus' friend Lazarus, brother of Martha and Mary, has fallen sick. The sisters sent word, "Lord, behold, he whom thou lovest is sick" (John 11:3), yet Jesus waited two days before going, and by the time he arrives Lazarus has been in the tomb four days. Martha meets him on the road with faith and grief mixed together; then Mary comes, falls at his feet weeping, and the mourners with her are weeping too. Jesus "groaned in the spirit, and was troubled," and asked, "Where have ye laid him?" They answer, "Lord, come and see" (John 11:33-34). Then comes this verse. Minutes later he will call Lazarus out of the grave.

Four witnesses

Greek · SBLGNT
ἐδάκρυσεν ὁ Ἰησοῦς.
edakrysen ho Iēsous.
Latin · Vulgate
Et lacrimatus est Jesus.
KJV
Jesus wept.
ESV
Jesus wept.

Key words

  • edakrysen → lacrimatus est → wept. The aorist of dakryō, "to shed tears," from dakryon, "a tear." This verb appears only here in the whole New Testament. It describes quiet tears running down the face, a different word from the loud wailing of the mourners.
  • klaiō (verse 33) → plorare → weeping. Mary and the mourners are "weeping" with klaiō, the word for audible, lamenting grief. John deliberately switches verbs for Jesus: he does not join the professional-style wailing; he simply sheds tears. The Vulgate keeps the distinction (plorantem for Mary, lacrimatus est for Jesus).
  • ho Iēsous → Jesus → Jesus. The Greek uses the article, "the Jesus," as Greek normally does with names. The subject comes last in Greek, so the sentence lands on his name: "wept, Jesus."
  • embrimaomai (verses 33 and 38) → infremuit → groaned. The verb around this verse, used of snorting horses and stern rebuke, suggests deep indignation, not only sorrow. Many commentators read it as anger at death itself standing before him.

Alignment

Exact. All the major Greek editions (Nestle 1904, Westcott–Hort, the Byzantine Majority Text, Tischendorf and both Textus Receptus editions) agree word for word: ἐδάκρυσεν ὁ Ἰησοῦς. Jerome's "lacrimatus est" matches dakryō closely ("shed tears," from lacrima, a tear), and the KJV and ESV both render it "Jesus wept." The Douay-Rheims follows the Latin's "Et" with "And Jesus wept."

The story

"Jesus wept" is famous as the shortest verse in the English Bible, but that is an accident of history: the verse numbers were added in 1551 by the printer Robert Estienne (Stephanus), centuries after John wrote. In Greek it isn't even the shortest; 1 Thessalonians 5:16, "Rejoice always" (Pantote chairete), has fewer letters. What makes it memorable is the scene: Jesus knows he is about to raise Lazarus, and he weeps anyway. Grief and hope sit side by side. The mourners' reaction says it plainly: "Behold how he loved him!" (John 11:36). Jesus weeps again over Jerusalem (Luke 19:41), and Hebrews 5:7 remembers his prayers "with strong crying and tears."

Dig Deeper: Roots in Hebrew & Jewish Sources

Jesus wept at a Jewish funeral, and John 11 is full of Jewish mourning customs that make the scene clearer. Lazarus was buried the same day he died, mourners came from Jerusalem "to comfort them concerning their brother" (John 11:19), the women's loud wailing (klaiō) was the expected sound of grief, and Martha stresses that her brother has been dead four days (John 11:39). Later rabbinic writings record two ideas that help explain why "four days" mattered: the first three days were the time of heaviest weeping, and a popular belief held that the soul hovered near the body for three days before departing. By the fourth day, no one could pretend Lazarus was merely unconscious. Behind it all stands the Hebrew prophets' promise that God himself would one day "wipe away tears from off all faces" (Isaiah 25:8), the very hope Jesus is about to act out at the tomb. These links are background study tools, not new Scripture, and including them is not an endorsement of any later theology. Keep the dates straight: Isaiah and the Psalms are Hebrew Bible (Tanakh), written centuries before Christ; John wrote in the late 1st century AD; the Talmud and Leviticus Rabbah were compiled several centuries after Jesus, so they describe customs and beliefs that may be older but are written down later. There is no strong Dead Sea Scroll parallel specific to this verse, so none is listed.

Where to dig

  1. Isaiah 25:8 in Hebrew and English, on Sefaria. "The Lord GOD will wipe away tears from off all faces," the prophetic hope behind both the tears of John 11 and the promise of Revelation 21:4.
  2. Psalm 56:9 in Hebrew and English, on Sefaria. "Put thou my tears into thy bottle" (Psalm 56:8 in English Bibles), David's confidence that God notices every tear.
  3. Talmud, Moed Katan 27b, on Sefaria. The rabbinic rule "three days for weeping, seven for eulogizing, thirty" for other mourning customs, written down centuries after Jesus but describing the rhythm of Jewish grief.
  4. Leviticus Rabbah 18:1, on Sefaria. Rabbi Levi's saying that "for the first three days the soul hovers over the body," the later rabbinic belief that helps explain why Martha's "four days" meant there was no hope left.
