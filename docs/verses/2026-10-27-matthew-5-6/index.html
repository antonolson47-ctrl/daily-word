Verse a Day #16 · October 27, 2026

Matthew 5:6

Alignment: Very close

Setting the scene

Still the hillside above the Sea of Galilee near Capernaum, around AD 28. Jesus has blessed the poor in spirit, those who mourn and the meek. Now he turns to appetite. His listeners knew real hunger: day laborers paid a denarius a day, families one bad harvest from want, and a dry land where a cistern could fail by late summer. Into that world Jesus says the people who are truly blessed are the ones who crave righteousness as badly as a hungry man craves bread and a thirsty man craves water, and he promises they will be filled.

Four witnesses

Greek · SBLGNT
μακάριοι οἱ πεινῶντες καὶ διψῶντες τὴν δικαιοσύνην, ὅτι αὐτοὶ χορτασθήσονται.
makarioi hoi peinōntes kai dipsōntes tēn dikaiosynēn, hoti autoi chortasthēsontai.
Latin · Vulgate
Beati qui esuriunt et sitiunt justitiam: quoniam ipsi saturabuntur.
KJV
Blessed are they which do hunger and thirst after righteousness: for they shall be filled.
ESV
"Blessed are those who hunger and thirst for righteousness, for they shall be satisfied."

Key words

  • makarioi → Beati → Blessed. The fourth verbless declaration in a row: "Blessed, the hungry and thirsty." It announces how God sees them.
  • peinōntes kai dipsōntes → qui esuriunt et sitiunt → those who hunger and thirst. Present participles: people who keep on hungering and keep on thirsting. This is a lasting craving, not a passing wish. Latin esurio and sitio are the plain everyday words for being hungry and thirsty.
  • tēn dikaiosynēn → justitiam → righteousness. Dikaiosynē means being right with God and doing what is right before him and toward others, and it covers both personal holiness and justice. Matthew uses it seven times, including "seek first the kingdom of God and his righteousness" (Matthew 6:33). Latin justitia gives English "justice," which keeps that wider sense.
  • autoi → ipsi → they. Emphatic: they themselves will be filled.
  • chortasthēsontai → saturabuntur → shall be filled / satisfied. From chortazō, which first meant feeding animals with fodder (chortos, grass) until they were full. Matthew uses the same verb when the five thousand "all ate and were satisfied" (Matthew 14:20). Latin saturabuntur comes from satur, "full," the root of English "saturate." The passive voice points to God as the one who does the filling.

Alignment

Very close. All the major Greek editions on Bible Hub (Nestle 1904, Westcott–Hort, Tischendorf, the Byzantine Majority Text and both Textus Receptus editions) have the same words, differing only in capitals and punctuation. The Vulgate follows the Greek word for word. The KJV's "filled" and the ESV's "satisfied" are both fair renderings of chortazō. One small grammar detail survives only in Greek: verbs of hungering and thirsting normally take the genitive case ("hungry for some of"), but here righteousness is in the accusative, which many grammarians read as craving the whole thing, not just a taste.

The story

Luke's version of this Beatitude is shorter and sharper: "Blessed are you who are hungry now, for you will be satisfied" (Luke 6:21), with a matching warning, "Woe to you who are full now" (Luke 6:25). Matthew adds "and thirst" and "for righteousness," so the blessing reaches both the physically hungry and everyone who longs for God's right order. The promise is kept in Matthew's own story. In chapter 14 Jesus feeds five thousand in a deserted place and the same verb returns: they all ate and were filled, with twelve baskets left over. In John's Gospel he says it outright: "I am the bread of life; whoever comes to me shall not hunger, and whoever believes in me shall never thirst" (John 6:35).

Dig Deeper: Roots in Hebrew & Jewish Sources

Jesus' listeners had grown up on Hebrew Scriptures that already used hunger and thirst as pictures of longing for God. Psalm 42 says, "my soul thirsts for God, the living God" (Psalm 42:3 in Hebrew numbering, 42:2 in English). Psalm 107:9 praises God "for satisfying the thirsty, and filling the hungry with all good things" (JPS), and the Greek Old Testament there uses the very words Matthew records: peinaō (hungry) and chortazō (to fill). Isaiah 55:1 calls out, "Ho, all who are thirsty, come for water, even if you have no money," and Amos 8:11 warns of a coming famine "not a hunger for bread or a thirst for water, but for hearing the words of GOD." Behind the Greek word for righteousness stand the Hebrew tsedeq and tsedaqah, which join being right with God to doing justice for the poor. These links are background study tools, not Scripture, and including them is not an endorsement of any later theology. Keep the dates straight: the Psalms, Isaiah and Amos are Hebrew Bible (Tanakh), written centuries before Christ; the Septuagint is the Greek translation made before the Gospels; later rabbinic writings like the Mishnah (about AD 200) and Talmud (about AD 400–600) are not sources Matthew used. No Dead Sea Scroll offers a parallel strong enough to list here.

Where to dig

  1. Psalm 107:9 in Hebrew and English, on Sefaria. Sefaria's free library of Jewish texts shows the promise that God fills "the hungry with all good things," side by side in Hebrew and English.
  2. Isaiah 55:1 in Hebrew and English, on Sefaria. The prophet's invitation to the thirsty to "come for water" without money, the Old Testament picture behind this Beatitude.
  3. dikaiosynē in the Blue Letter Bible Greek lexicon. The entry for Strong's 1343, "righteousness," with every New Testament use, including Matthew's seven.
  4. chortazō in the Blue Letter Bible Greek lexicon. The entry for Strong's 5526, "to fill, satisfy," from feeding animals to the feeding of the five thousand.
