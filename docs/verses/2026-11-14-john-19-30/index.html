Verse a Day #34 · November 14, 2026

John 19:30

Alignment: Very close across Greek, Latin and English

Setting the scene

It is Friday afternoon outside the walls of Jerusalem, at the place called Golgotha. Jesus has hung on the cross since the morning; above his head Pilate's sign reads "Jesus of Nazareth the King of the Jews" in Hebrew, Greek and Latin (John 19:19-20). The soldiers have divided his garments, and from the cross he has given his mother into the care of the beloved disciple: "Woman, behold thy son!" (19:26). Then, "knowing that all things were now accomplished," he says, "I thirst" (19:28). A sponge full of vinegar is lifted to his lips on a branch of hyssop (19:29). He takes it, and speaks one last word.

Four witnesses

Greek · SBLGNT
ὅτε οὖν ἔλαβεν τὸ ὄξος ὁ Ἰησοῦς εἶπεν Τετέλεσται, καὶ κλίνας τὴν κεφαλὴν παρέδωκεν τὸ πνεῦμα.
hote oun elaben to oxos ho Iēsous eipen Tetelestai, kai klinas tēn kephalēn paredōken to pneuma.
Latin · Vulgate
Cum ergo accepisset Jesus acetum, dixit: Consummatum est. Et inclinato capite tradidit spiritum.
KJV
When Jesus therefore had received the vinegar, he said, It is finished: and he bowed his head, and gave up the ghost.
ESV
When Jesus had received the sour wine, he said, "It is finished," and he bowed his head and gave up his spirit.

Key words

  • oxos → acetum → vinegar / sour wine. The cheap, sour wine soldiers drank. It fulfills Psalm 69:21, "in my thirst they gave me vinegar to drink," where the Greek Old Testament uses the same word.
  • tetelestai → consummatum est → it is finished. One Greek word, the perfect passive of teleō, "to bring to its goal." The perfect tense means an action completed with lasting results: it has been finished and stands finished. John used the same verb two verses earlier, "all things were now accomplished" (19:28), and Jesus had prayed, "I have finished the work which thou gavest me to do" (17:4). This is not a cry of defeat but of completion.
  • klinas tēn kephalēn → inclinato capite → he bowed his head. The same verb Jesus used of himself: "the Son of man hath not where to lay his head" (Matthew 8:20, klinē). On the cross he lays it down at last.
  • paredōken → tradidit → gave up / handed over. Paradidōmi runs through the Passion: Judas "handed over" Jesus, the priests handed him to Pilate, Pilate handed him over to be crucified (19:16). Now Jesus himself hands over his spirit. No one takes his life from him: "I lay it down of myself" (10:18).
  • pneuma → spiritum → spirit / ghost. Breath, spirit. The KJV's "gave up the ghost" uses the old English word for spirit, as in "Holy Ghost."

Alignment

Very close across Greek, Latin and English. Nestle 1904, Westcott–Hort, the Byzantine Majority Text, the Greek Orthodox text and both Textus Receptus editions agree word for word apart from punctuation and the spelling elabe/elaben; Tischendorf omits the name "Jesus" (ho Iēsous). Jerome turns the single word tetelestai into two Latin words, consummatum est, keeping the passive sense. The KJV and ESV agree; the main difference is "vinegar" versus "sour wine" for oxos, and the KJV's older "gave up the ghost" for "gave up his spirit."

The story

"It is finished" is the sixth of the traditional Seven Last Words of Christ from the cross, gathered from all four Gospels. In 1724 Johann Sebastian Bach set the moment in his St John Passion with the alto aria "Es ist vollbracht," Luther's German for this word, a slow lament that bursts into a triumphant middle section, "Der Held aus Juda siegt mit Macht" (the hero from Judah wins with might), before sinking back into stillness. There is also a well-loved reading of tetelestai as "paid in full." Moulton and Milligan's Vocabulary of the Greek Testament (1930) noted that the verb teleō is used in Greek papyrus receipts for taxes paid, and preachers have drawn the picture of a debt marked settled. Scholars caution that the papyri show the verb in general use, not a stamped one-word formula, but the image fits what the Church has always confessed: "he is the propitiation for our sins" (1 John 2:2).

Dig Deeper: Roots in Hebrew & Jewish Sources

John tells us that Jesus spoke this word "knowing that all things were now accomplished, that the scripture might be fulfilled" (John 19:28). That sends the reader straight back to the Hebrew Scriptures. The sour wine he has just received echoes Psalm 69:21, "in my thirst they gave me vinegar to drink," where the Greek Septuagint uses the same word, oxos. Earlier the same afternoon, John notes that the soldiers divided his garments (John 19:24), quoting Psalm 22:18; that psalm, which Matthew and Mark record Jesus praying from the cross ("My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me?"), ends with the line "he hath done this" (Psalm 22:31). Many readers hear tetelestai as the answer to that closing line. There is also an echo of the creation account: "Thus the heavens and the earth were finished" (Genesis 2:1), which the Septuagint renders with a related verb, synetelesthēsan. Jesus dies on the sixth day of the week, the day man was made, and then rests in the tomb on the Sabbath. Behind the Greek stands the Aramaic of the Peshitta, which renders the word ha mshalam, from the root sh-l-m, the family of shalom: whole, complete, paid. No Dead Sea Scroll passage bears directly on this word, so none is listed here. Dig Deeper is background study, not new Scripture, and not an endorsement of any later theology.

Where to dig

  1. Psalm 22 in Hebrew and English, on Sefaria. The psalm of the suffering righteous one, from "My God, my God" (verse 1) and the divided garments (verse 18) to "he hath done this" (verse 31).
  2. Psalm 69 in Hebrew and English, on Sefaria. The lament where "in my thirst they gave me vinegar to drink" (verse 21; verse 22 in Hebrew numbering).
  3. Genesis 2 in Hebrew and English, on Sefaria. "Thus the heavens and the earth were finished" and God's rest on the seventh day (verses 1-3).
  4. Blue Letter Bible lexicon for τελέω (teleō). Every New Testament use of the verb behind tetelestai, including "all things were now accomplished" two verses earlier (John 19:28).

A quick timeline, so the sources don't blur together

Source Date What it is
Genesis 2 and Psalms 22 and 69 (Tanakh / Old Testament) Hebrew Scriptures, written centuries before Christ Creation "finished"; the psalms of the suffering righteous one
Septuagint (Greek Old Testament) translated about 250-100 BC The Greek Bible the evangelists often quote, with oxos in Psalm 69
Gospel of John 1st century AD "It is finished"
Peshitta (Syriac/Aramaic New Testament) standard by about the 5th century AD Renders the word ha mshalam
