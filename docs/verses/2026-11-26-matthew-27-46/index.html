Verse a Day #46 · November 26, 2026

Matthew 27:46

Alignment: Very close

Setting the scene

It is Friday, the day after the Last Supper, and Jesus has been hanging on the cross outside the walls of Jerusalem since the third hour, about nine in the morning (Mark 15:25). Passersby wag their heads, the chief priests mock, "He trusted in God; let him deliver him now" (27:43), and the soldiers have already parted his garments and cast lots (27:35). Then, at noon, "there was darkness over all the land unto the ninth hour" (27:45). Three hours of silence and shadow. And at the ninth hour, about three in the afternoon, the very hour when the temple's evening sacrifice was offered and the faithful gathered for prayer (Acts 3:1), Jesus lifts his voice, not in a whisper but in a great cry, and prays the opening words of Psalm 22: "Eli, Eli, lama sabachthani?" Every devout Jew within earshot knew that psalm. It begins in the deepest darkness a human heart can know, but it does not end there. It moves through mockery, pierced hands and feet and divided garments, all things happening around the cross that very afternoon, and it climbs into thanksgiving in "the great congregation" (Psalm 22:22-25) and ends with the nations turning to the Lord and a people yet unborn hearing "that he hath done this" (Psalm 22:31). On this Thanksgiving Day, it is worth remembering that the Saviour's darkest cry opened the door to the church's greatest song of thanks.

Key words

  • Ēli Ēli → Eli, Eli → My God, my God. This is the Hebrew of Psalm 22:1, ʾēlî ʾēlî, "my God, my God" (Psalm 22:2 in Hebrew Bibles, where the title counts as verse 1). Mark gives the Aramaic form Elōi, Elōi (Mark 15:34). Matthew's Eli also explains the bystanders' mistake: "This man calleth for Elias" (27:47). Even in agony Jesus says "my God," a cry of trust held fast in the dark.
  • lema → lamma → why. The Hebrew of the psalm is lāmâ, the Aramaic lĕmā; the Greek copyists spell it lema, lima or lama, which is why the KJV says "lama" and the ESV "lema."
  • sabachthani → sabacthani → hast thou forsaken me. Here Jesus speaks the Aramaic of his own home and table. The Hebrew psalm reads ʿăzabtānî, but sabachthani comes from the Aramaic verb shĕbaq, "to leave, to let go." The Aramaic Peshitta reads shbaqtani, and the ancient Aramaic Targum of the Psalms renders this very verse ʾēlî ʾēlî mĕṭûl mah shĕbaqtanî. The Gospel writers kept the sound of his actual voice before translating it for us.
  • enkatelipes → dereliquisti → forsaken. Enkataleipō means "to leave behind, abandon," the same word the Greek Septuagint uses in Psalm 21:2 (our Psalm 22:1). Jerome's dereliquisti gave the church the name "the cry of dereliction." And here is the wonder: because the Son was forsaken in our place, the same Greek verb carries God's promise to us, "I will never leave thee, nor forsake thee" (Hebrews 13:5), and Peter's Easter sermon, "thou wilt not leave my soul in hell" (Acts 2:27).
  • phōnē megalē → voce magna → with a loud voice. Not a faint murmur but a great cry. The Letter to the Hebrews remembers how in the days of his flesh Jesus "offered up prayers and supplications with strong crying and tears unto him that was able to save him from death, and was heard" (Hebrews 5:7).

Alignment

Very close. Every Greek text on Bible Hub agrees on the heart of the verse, "Thee mou thee mou, hina ti me enkatelipes," My God, my God, why have you forsaken me? The small differences are in how the Semitic words were spelled. Nestle 1904 and Tischendorf read Ἡλεὶ Ἡλεὶ λεμὰ σαβαχθανεί; the Byzantine Majority Text and the Greek Orthodox text read Ἠλί, Ἠλί, λιμὰ σαβαχθανί; both Textus Receptus editions read λαμὰ; and Westcott–Hort prints the Aramaic Ἐλωί ἐλωί, the form found in Mark, while NA27/UBS4 keep ηλι, "Eli." Westcott–Hort also reads ἐβόησεν ("cried") where most read ἀνεβόησεν ("cried out"). The KJV's "Eli, Eli, lama" follows the Textus Receptus; the ESV's "Eli, Eli, lema" follows the modern critical text. The Vulgate is word for word: clamavit Jesus voce magna ... Deus meus, Deus meus, ut quid dereliquisti me? The Aramaic Peshitta reads ʾIl ʾIl lmana shbaqtani. And the Greek translation agrees with the Hebrew of Psalm 22:1, ʾēlî ʾēlî lāmâ ʿăzabtānî, word for word.

The story

The first Christians treasured Psalm 22 as a portrait of the cross painted a thousand years ahead of time. Around AD 155, St. Justin Martyr walked a Jewish teacher named Trypho through the whole psalm, line by line, in his Dialogue with Trypho (chapters 97-106): the mockers who "shoot out the lip" and "shake the head," the pierced hands and feet, the garments parted by lot, and then the psalm's turn to praise, which Justin saw fulfilled when the risen Jesus stood among his brothers, the apostles. The Letter to the Hebrews had already put Psalm 22:22 on the lips of the risen Christ: "I will declare thy name unto my brethren, in the midst of the church will I sing praise unto thee" (Hebrews 2:12). Many churches still pray this psalm on Maundy Thursday night while the altar is stripped bare, and many believers have heard in the psalm's last words, "he hath done this," an echo of Jesus' own final cry from the cross: "It is finished" (John 19:30). The psalm that begins with "Why?" ends with "Done." That is a reason for thanksgiving on any day, and especially on this one.

Dig Deeper: Roots in Hebrew & Jewish Sources

Today's verse is a prayer from the Hebrew Scriptures, spoken in the Aramaic of first-century Galilee, at the hour of the temple's afternoon sacrifice, so the Hebrew and Jewish roots are close to the surface. Jesus did not invent new words for his suffering; he prayed Israel's own prayer book. The Hebrew of Psalm 22 says ʾēlî ʾēlî lāmâ ʿăzabtānî, and the Gospels preserve his cry in its everyday Aramaic sound, sabachthani, the language spoken in Jewish homes and synagogues of the Second Temple era. Psalm scrolls found among the Dead Sea Scrolls, copied before the time of Christ, show how carefully Jews of that era copied and prayed these psalms. Much later, the Aramaic Targum of the Psalms, a synagogue paraphrase written down centuries after the New Testament, renders this very verse with the same verb Jesus used, shĕbaqtanî. These are background study, not Scripture, and not an endorsement of any later theology.
