Verse a Day #29 · November 9, 2026

John 3:3

Alignment: Very close across Greek, Latin and English

Setting the scene

It is night in Jerusalem, early in Jesus' ministry, just after he has cleansed the Temple at Passover (John 2:13-25). A man named Nicodemus comes to find him in the dark. He is a Pharisee and "a ruler of the Jews," a member of the high council, a respected teacher of Scripture. He comes with a polite, careful compliment: "Rabbi, we know that thou art a teacher come from God: for no man can do these miracles that thou doest, except God be with him" (John 3:2). Jesus does not trade compliments. He goes straight to Nicodemus' heart with one of the most famous sentences he ever spoke. All the learning, rank and good works Nicodemus has spent his life on cannot get him into the kingdom of God. He needs what no man can give himself: a new birth, from God. This same conversation goes on to John 3:16, and Nicodemus himself, the man who came by night, will later stand up for Jesus in the council (John 7:50-51) and help bury him with honor (John 19:39).

Key words

  • amēn amēn → amen, amen → verily, verily / truly, truly. A Hebrew word carried straight into Greek and Latin untranslated. It means firm, sure, trustworthy. In the Old Testament, God's people said "amen, amen" after a word to agree with it (Nehemiah 8:6); in John's Gospel Jesus says it before he speaks, because his own word is the guarantee.
  • gennēthē → renatus fuerit → be born. The ordinary word for a baby being born. Jesus is not talking about turning over a new leaf; he means a whole new beginning of life, as real as the first one, and given, not achieved. No one makes himself be born.
  • anōthen → denuo → again / from above. The richest word of the day. Anōthen can mean "from above" (from the top, from heaven) or "again, anew, from the beginning." John uses it to mean "from above" in 3:31 ("He that cometh from above is above all") and 19:11, and Nicodemus hears it as "a second time" (3:4). Jerome chose denuo, "anew, once more," and placed it beside renatus, "re-born," so the Latin leans firmly toward "again"; the KJV and ESV follow with "born again." Many modern versions (NRSV, NAB, NET) say "born from above." Both meanings are true at once: it is a second birth, and it comes from heaven.
  • idein → videre → see. Not just to glimpse but to experience and share in. In 3:5 Jesus will say "enter." Without new birth a man cannot even perceive the kingdom, let alone live in it.
  • basileian tou Theou → regnum Dei → the kingdom of God. God's own reign, which Jesus came preaching (Mark 1:15). The door into it is not lineage or learning but new life from God.

Alignment

Very close across Greek, Latin and English. Nestle 1904, Westcott–Hort, Tischendorf and the Greek Orthodox text read the same words; the Byzantine Majority and both Textus Receptus editions add only the article "ho" before Iēsous ("the Jesus"), which does not change the meaning. Jerome's Vulgate follows the Greek phrase by phrase: Amen amen dico tibi for Amēn amēn legō soi, regnum Dei for basileian tou Theou. The one real translation choice is anōthen. The Vulgate (denuo), KJV, Douay-Rheims, ESV, NIV and NASB say "again" or "anew"; Young's, the NRSV, NAB and NET say "from above"; the ASV says "anew." The Syriac Peshitta also chose "from the beginning," that is, anew. Church Fathers already read it both ways (Chrysostom notes both), so neither rendering is wrong; each brings out one side of a single gift.

The story

"Born again" has become one of the most familiar phrases in the English language, but its first home is this quiet night conversation with one cautious, honest seeker. Nicodemus did not understand at first; he asked, "How can a man be born when he is old?" (John 3:4). Yet John shows us that the seed took root. In John 7 he speaks up for Jesus when the council wants to condemn him unheard. And in John 19, after the crucifixion, when most of the disciples have scattered, Nicodemus comes again, this time not hidden by night, carrying about seventy-five pounds of myrrh and aloes to give Jesus a king's burial. Christian tradition honors him as Saint Nicodemus. The man who came in the dark asking questions ended at the foot of the cross in the light. That is what new birth from above looks like: God begins it, often slowly, and he finishes it.

Dig Deeper: Roots in Hebrew & Jewish Sources

Nicodemus was "the teacher of Israel" (John 3:10), so Jesus expected him to recognize what he was hearing. The Hebrew prophets had already promised that God himself would one day wash his people and give them a new heart and a new spirit: "I will sprinkle clean water upon you... A new heart also will I give you, and a new spirit will I put within you... and I will put my spirit within you" (Ezekiel 36:25-27), and "I will put my law in their inward parts, and write it in their hearts" (Jeremiah 31:33). Two verses later Jesus speaks of being born "of water and of the Spirit" (John 3:5), and that is the promise Ezekiel had made. Among the Dead Sea Scrolls, copied by a Jewish community in the last centuries before Christ, the Community Rule also hopes for a day when God will cleanse his people "with a spirit of holiness" and sprinkle them with a spirit of truth like purifying water, which shows how alive this hope was in Jesus' day; it is background, and the Scrolls are not Scripture. Much later, the Babylonian Talmud (compiled about AD 500) records the rabbinic saying that a Gentile who converts to Judaism "is like a newborn child." That is a later legal teaching about conversion, not about the Spirit's work, but it shows that "new birth" language was at home in Jewish speech. Jesus goes further than any of these: not a new legal status, but new life from God above. Dig Deeper here is background study, not new Scripture, and not an endorsement of any later theology.

Where to dig

  1. Ezekiel 36:25-27 in Hebrew and English, on Sefaria. The prophet's promise of clean water, a new heart and God's own Spirit within, the background to "born of water and of the Spirit."
  2. Jeremiah 31:33 in Hebrew and English, on Sefaria. The new covenant promise that God will write his law on his people's hearts, an inward change only God can make.
  3. Babylonian Talmud, Yevamot 22a, on Sefaria. The later rabbinic saying that a convert is "like a newborn child," useful for seeing how Jewish teachers used birth language, centuries after John.
  4. Blue Letter Bible lexicon for ἄνωθεν (anōthen). Every New Testament use of the word behind "again / from above," so you can see both meanings side by side.
