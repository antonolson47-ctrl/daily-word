Verse a Day #15 · October 26, 2026

Matthew 5:5

Alignment: Very close

Setting the scene

Still the Galilean hillside near Capernaum, around AD 28, with Jesus seated and the crowds from Galilee, the Decapolis, Jerusalem and Judea gathered below (Matthew 4:25–5:1). He has blessed the poor in spirit and those who mourn. The third blessing goes to the meek. Many of his listeners lived under Roman soldiers, Herodian tax men and absentee landlords, and some were ready to take the land back by the sword. Jesus tells them the earth will belong to the gentle, and he quotes their own Psalm to say it.

Four witnesses

Greek · SBLGNT
μακάριοι οἱ πραεῖς, ὅτι αὐτοὶ κληρονομήσουσιν τὴν γῆν.
makarioi hoi praeis, hoti autoi klēronomēsousin tēn gēn.
Latin · Vulgate
Beati mites: quoniam ipsi possidebunt terram.
KJV
Blessed are the meek: for they shall inherit the earth.
ESV
"Blessed are the meek, for they shall inherit the earth."

Key words

  • makarioi → Beati → Blessed. The same verbless declaration as in 5:3 and 5:4: "Blessed, the meek." It is an announcement of how God sees them, not a wish.
  • praeis → mites → meek. From praus, gentle, mild, not harsh or vengeful. Meekness is not weakness: it is strength held under control. Jesus uses the same word of himself, "I am gentle (praus) and lowly in heart" (Matthew 11:29), and Matthew uses it of the King riding into Jerusalem "humble (praus), and mounted on a donkey" (Matthew 21:5, quoting Zechariah 9:9). Latin mitis means soft, mild, ripe and tame.
  • autoi → ipsi → they. Emphatic: they themselves, not the grabbers and the powerful, will inherit.
  • klēronomēsousin → possidebunt → shall inherit. From klēronomeō, to receive a share by lot or inheritance, the word for Israel receiving the Promised Land. An inheritance is given, not seized. The Vulgate's possidebunt ("they shall possess") stresses the result rather than the gift.
  • tēn gēn → terram → the earth. Gē can mean "land" or "earth." In Psalm 37 it is the Promised Land; on the lips of Jesus the promise widens to the whole earth made new (compare Romans 4:13; Revelation 21:1).

Alignment

Very close. Greek, Latin, KJV and ESV say the same thing word for word. The only wrinkles are small: the Westcott–Hort and Scrivener editions spell the verb klēronomēsousi without the final n, and Scrivener writes πρᾳεῖς with an older iota subscript. The real difference is the verse order. Most Greek editions put "the meek" at 5:5, but Tischendorf's 8th edition and the Vulgate put it at 5:4, before "those who mourn," following a minority of manuscripts led by Codex Bezae. The Latin possidebunt ("possess") is a shade stronger than the Greek "inherit," but the meaning holds.

The story

This Beatitude is almost a word-for-word quotation. Psalm 37:11 in the Greek Old Testament reads hoi de praeis klēronomēsousin gēn, "but the meek shall inherit the land," the same three key words as Matthew 5:5. The Psalm was written to people tempted to "fret because of evildoers" (Psalm 37:1); its answer is to wait on the Lord, who will give the land to the humble. Four centuries before Christ, Aristotle had praised praotēs (meekness) as the virtue of being angry at the right things, at the right time and in the right measure (Nicomachean Ethics IV.5). Jesus goes further: the meek do not get their way by force, and God gives them the earth anyway.

Dig Deeper: Roots in Hebrew & Jewish Sources

Matthew wrote in Greek, but this Beatitude is a quotation from Hebrew Scripture. Psalm 37:11 says va'anavim yirshu-aretz, "But the lowly shall inherit the land" (JPS), and the Greek translation of that Psalm used the very words Matthew records: praeis (meek) and klēronomēsousin (shall inherit). The Hebrew anavim are the humble or lowly, people who are afflicted and still trust God instead of grabbing for themselves. The same root describes Moses, "very humble, more so than any other human being on earth" (Numbers 12:3). In Jesus' own century, a Dead Sea Scrolls commentary on Psalm 37 read "the meek shall inherit the land" as a promise to "the congregation of the poor" who endure a time of trouble, which shows faithful Jews were already leaning on this verse while they waited for God to act. These sources are background study, not Scripture, and including them is not an endorsement of any later theology. Keep the dates straight: the Psalms are Hebrew Bible (Tanakh); the Qumran Pesher on Psalms (4Q171) is Dead Sea Scrolls material copied before the Gospels were written; later rabbinic writings like the Mishnah (about AD 200) and Talmud (about AD 400–600) are not sources Matthew used.

Where to dig

  1. Psalm 37:11 in Hebrew and English, on Sefaria. Sefaria's free library of Jewish texts shows "the lowly shall inherit the land" side by side in Hebrew and English, inside the whole Psalm about not fretting over evildoers.
  2. 4Q171, the Pesher on Psalms, in the Leon Levy Dead Sea Scrolls Digital Library. The Israel Antiquities Authority's high-resolution images of the Qumran commentary that applies Psalm 37 to "the congregation of the poor."
  3. praus in the Blue Letter Bible Greek lexicon. The entry for Strong's 4239, "meek, gentle," with every New Testament use, including Jesus' "I am gentle and lowly in heart."
  4. klēronomeō in the Blue Letter Bible Greek lexicon. The entry for Strong's 2816, "to inherit," the word for receiving a promised share as a gift.
