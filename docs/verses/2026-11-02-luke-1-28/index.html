Verse a Day #22 · November 2, 2026

Luke 1:28

Alignment: Close, with one textual difference and one famous translation choice

Setting the scene

Nazareth, a small village in the hills of Galilee, a few years before the turn of the era. Six months after the angel Gabriel told the old priest Zechariah in the Temple that his wife Elizabeth would bear a son (John the Baptist), "the angel Gabriel was sent from God unto a city of Galilee, named Nazareth, to a virgin espoused to a man whose name was Joseph, of the house of David; and the virgin's name was Mary" (Luke 1:26-27). Zechariah got his news at the altar in Jerusalem; Mary gets hers in an obscure village. This verse is the angel's first word to her, the opening of the Annunciation, and Luke tells us next that she "was greatly troubled at the saying" and wondered what kind of greeting this was (Luke 1:29).

Four witnesses

Greek · SBLGNT
καὶ εἰσελθὼν πρὸς αὐτὴν εἶπεν Χαῖρε, κεχαριτωμένη, ὁ Κύριος μετὰ σοῦ.
kai eiselthōn pros autēn eipen Chaire, kecharitōmenē, ho Kyrios meta sou.
Latin · Vulgate
Et ingressus angelus ad eam dixit: Ave gratia plena: Dominus tecum: benedicta tu in mulieribus.
KJV
And the angel came in unto her, and said, Hail, thou that art highly favoured, the Lord is with thee: blessed art thou among women.
ESV
And he came to her and said, "Greetings, O favored one, the Lord is with you!"

Key words

  • Chaire → Ave → Hail / Greetings. The everyday Greek hello, but literally "rejoice!" (a present imperative of chairō). Jerome used Ave, the ordinary Latin greeting, so the word "Ave" in "Ave Maria" comes straight from this verse.
  • kecharitōmenē → gratia plena → highly favoured / O favored one. A perfect passive participle of charitoō, "to grace, to show favor," built on charis, "grace." Perfect tense: she has been graced and remains so. The verb appears only twice in the New Testament, here and in Ephesians 1:6, where God "made us accepted" (echaritōsen) in the Beloved. Notice the wordplay: chaire, kecharitōmenē, "rejoice, graced one."
  • ho Kyrios meta sou → Dominus tecum → the Lord is with thee. No verb in Greek or Latin, just "the Lord with you," a greeting the Old Testament reserves for people God is about to send on a task (Gideon in Judges 6:12).
  • eulogēmenē sy en gynaixin → benedicta tu in mulieribus → blessed art thou among women. Found in the Byzantine and Textus Receptus Greek, followed by the Vulgate and KJV; not in Nestle, Westcott–Hort or Tischendorf. The same words appear for certain in Luke 1:42, from Elizabeth's lips.

Alignment

Close, with one textual difference and one famous translation choice. Nestle 1904 and Westcott–Hort have only "he said" (no "the angel") and stop at "the Lord is with you," which the ESV follows. The Byzantine Majority Text and both Textus Receptus editions add "the angel" and "blessed are you among women," which the Vulgate and KJV keep. The big choice is kecharitōmenē: Jerome's "gratia plena" ("full of grace," kept by the Douay-Rheims) versus the KJV's "highly favoured" and the ESV's "O favored one," which stay closer to the Greek verb form ("graced, favored").

The story

Luke 1:28 is the first half of one of the most-prayed sentences in history. The "Hail Mary" (Ave Maria) joins Gabriel's greeting here with Elizabeth's words in Luke 1:42 ("blessed is the fruit of thy womb"), straight from the Latin Vulgate. The translation of kecharitōmenē has been argued over ever since the Reformation: "full of grace" can sound like grace Mary possesses, while "highly favoured" stresses grace God gave her. The Greek word itself points to the giver: it is passive, "the one who has been graced." Mary's own response a few verses later says the same: "Behold the handmaid of the Lord" (Luke 1:38).

Dig Deeper: Roots in Hebrew & Jewish Sources

Gabriel's greeting is short, but nearly every phrase has an echo in the Hebrew Scriptures Mary would have heard in the synagogue at Nazareth. "Rejoice!" (chaire) is the word the Greek Old Testament (the Septuagint) uses in Zephaniah 3:14, "Rejoice greatly, daughter of Zion," a prophecy that goes on to promise "the LORD your God is in your midst" (3:17). Many scholars see Luke's greeting deliberately recalling that passage, with Mary standing for Zion. "The Lord is with you" is exactly what the angel of the LORD said to Gideon in Judges 6:12 before calling him to deliver Israel. And "blessed among women" (in the longer text) echoes Judges 5:24, where Deborah's song calls Jael "most blessed of women." Even the Hebrew translation of Luke on Bible Hub renders kecharitōmenē as "eshet chen," "woman of grace (chen)," using the same Hebrew word for favor found in Noah, who "found grace (chen) in the eyes of the LORD" (Genesis 6:8). These links are background study tools, not new Scripture, and including them is not an endorsement of any later theology. Keep the dates straight: Zephaniah and Judges are Hebrew Bible (Tanakh), written centuries before Christ; the Septuagint is the Greek translation Jews made in the last centuries BC; Luke wrote in the 1st century AD. There is no strong Dead Sea Scroll or rabbinic parallel specific to this greeting, so none is listed.

Where to dig

  1. Zephaniah 3:14 in Hebrew and English, on Sefaria. "Shout for joy, daughter of Zion," the passage whose Greek "rejoice" (chaire) many scholars hear in Gabriel's first word; read on to verse 17, "the LORD your God is in your midst."
  2. Judges 6:12 in Hebrew and English, on Sefaria. The angel of the LORD greets Gideon with "The LORD is with you," the same words spoken to Mary before a calling from God.
  3. Judges 5:24 in Hebrew and English, on Sefaria. Deborah's song: "Most blessed of women be Jael," the Old Testament background to "blessed art thou among women."
  4. charitoō in the Blue Letter Bible Greek lexicon. The entry for Strong's 5487, the verb behind kecharitōmenē, with its only two New Testament uses (Luke 1:28 and Ephesians 1:6).
