Verse a Day #17 · October 28, 2026

Matthew 5:7

Alignment: Very close

Setting the scene

Still the hillside above the Sea of Galilee near Capernaum, around AD 28. The first four Beatitudes describe people who are empty-handed before God: the poor in spirit, the mourners, the meek, the hungry for righteousness. With the fifth, Jesus turns outward to how they treat others. His listeners lived among beggars at the town gate, widows without support, debtors who could be sold into servitude, and a Roman occupation that showed little pity. Into that world Jesus says the people who are truly blessed are the ones who show mercy, and he promises they will receive mercy from God.

Four witnesses

Greek · SBLGNT
μακάριοι οἱ ἐλεήμονες, ὅτι αὐτοὶ ἐλεηθήσονται.
makarioi hoi eleēmones, hoti autoi eleēthēsontai.
Latin · Vulgate
Beati misericordes: quoniam ipsi misericordiam consequentur.
KJV
Blessed are the merciful: for they shall obtain mercy.
ESV
"Blessed are the merciful, for they shall receive mercy."

Key words

  • makarioi → Beati → Blessed. The fifth verbless declaration in a row: "Blessed, the merciful." It announces how God sees them.
  • eleēmones → misericordes → the merciful. Eleēmōn means compassionate in action, not just in feeling: someone who sees need and does something about it. It is rare in the New Testament, used only here and in Hebrews 2:17, where Jesus himself is the "merciful and faithful high priest." The same root gives eleēmosynē, "almsgiving," which passed through Latin into English "alms." Latin misericors joins miser (wretched) and cor (heart): a heart for the wretched.
  • autoi → ipsi → they. Emphatic: they themselves will be shown mercy.
  • eleēthēsontai → misericordiam consequentur → shall obtain / receive mercy. Greek uses one passive verb, "they will be mercied," which points to God as the one who shows mercy. Jerome expanded it into a verb plus noun, "they will obtain mercy" (consequor, to attain or obtain), and the KJV's "obtain mercy" follows the same pattern.

Alignment

Very close. All the major Greek editions on Bible Hub (Nestle 1904, Westcott–Hort, Tischendorf, the Byzantine Majority Text and both Textus Receptus editions) have the same words, differing only in capitals and punctuation. The Vulgate keeps the meaning but turns the single Greek passive verb into "obtain mercy," and the KJV follows that wording. The ESV's "receive mercy" stays a little closer to the Greek passive. The Greek also plays on one root twice (eleēmones / eleēthēsontai), and the Latin keeps the echo with misericordes / misericordiam.

The story

This is the only Beatitude where the reward is the same thing as the virtue: the merciful get mercy. Jesus keeps returning to that in Matthew. Right after the Lord's Prayer he says, "If you forgive others their trespasses, your heavenly Father will also forgive you" (Matthew 6:14). Twice he quotes the prophet Hosea, "I desire mercy, and not sacrifice" (Matthew 9:13; 12:7). And in the parable of the unforgiving servant, the king who canceled a huge debt asks the man who would not cancel a small one, "Should not you also have had mercy on your fellow servant, as I had mercy on you?" (Matthew 18:33). James sums it up: "Mercy triumphs over judgment" (James 2:13).

Dig Deeper: Roots in Hebrew & Jewish Sources

Mercy runs deep in the Hebrew Scriptures Jesus' listeners knew. Two Hebrew words stand behind it: chesed, God's loyal, covenant kindness, and rachamim, tender compassion, from the same root as the word for a mother's womb. Psalm 18:26 (18:25 in English) says that with the chasid, the loyal or merciful one, God shows himself loyal in return; the KJV renders it, "With the merciful thou wilt shew thyself merciful." Hosea 6:6 has God say he desires chesed, not sacrifice, and the Greek Old Testament translates chesed there with eleos, the root of Matthew's word; Jesus quotes that verse twice in Matthew (9:13; 12:7). The Aramaic Peshitta, an early Syriac translation of the Gospels, renders this Beatitude with the r-ḥ-m root of rachamim. These links are background study tools, not Scripture, and including them is not an endorsement of any later theology. Keep the dates straight: Psalms, Hosea and Proverbs are Hebrew Bible (Tanakh), written centuries before Christ. The Talmud saying below is much later rabbinic literature, a parallel idea and not a source Matthew used. No Dead Sea Scroll offers a parallel strong enough to list here.

Where to dig

  1. Hosea 6:6 in Hebrew and English, on Sefaria. Sefaria's free library of Jewish texts shows the prophet's line that God desires chesed rather than sacrifice, the verse Jesus quotes twice in Matthew.
  2. Psalm 18:26 in Hebrew and English, on Sefaria. The psalm's promise that God deals faithfully with the faithful, the same give-and-receive pattern as this Beatitude.
  3. Babylonian Talmud, Shabbat 151b, on Sefaria. A later rabbinic teaching, drawn from Deuteronomy 13:18, that whoever has compassion on God's creatures receives compassion from Heaven; it is a parallel, written centuries after the Gospels.
  4. eleēmōn in the Blue Letter Bible Greek lexicon. The entry for Strong's 1655, "merciful," which appears only twice in the New Testament: here and of Jesus in Hebrews 2:17.
