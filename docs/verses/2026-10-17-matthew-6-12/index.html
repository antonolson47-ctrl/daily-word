Verse a Day #6 · October 17, 2026

Matthew 6:12

Alignment: Close, with a tense difference

Setting the scene

Still on the hillside above the Sea of Galilee near Capernaum, around AD 28–30, in the middle of the Sermon on the Mount. Jesus has taught his followers to ask for God's name to be honored, his kingdom to come, and bread for today. Now he turns to debt, a word his listeners felt in their bones. Under Roman rule, Galilean farmers paid tribute to Rome, taxes to Herod Antipas, and tithes to the Temple, and a bad harvest could push a family into loans they could never repay. Debtors could lose their land or even be sold into servitude. The Law of Moses promised a release of debts every seventh year (Deuteronomy 15), but by Jesus' day lenders often sidestepped it with a legal device called the prosbul, credited to Rabbi Hillel. Many hoped the Messiah would bring a true jubilee and wipe the slate clean. A young rabbi from Nazareth tells them to ask God to cancel their debts, and in the same breath ties that request to canceling the debts others owe them. He speaks as a man in the flesh to people who did not yet know who was standing in front of them.

Four witnesses

Greek · SBLGNT
καὶ ἄφες ἡμῖν τὰ ὀφειλήματα ἡμῶν, ὡς καὶ ἡμεῖς ἀφήκαμεν τοῖς ὀφειλέταις ἡμῶν·
Kai aphes hēmin ta opheilēmata hēmōn, hōs kai hēmeis aphēkamen tois opheiletais hēmōn;
Latin · Vulgate
et dimitte nobis debita nostra, sicut et nos dimittimus debitoribus nostris.
KJV
And forgive us our debts, as we forgive our debtors.
ESV
and forgive us our debts, as we also have forgiven our debtors.

Key words

  • opheilēmata → debita → debts. A plain money word for what is owed. In Aramaic, the language Jesus spoke, one word (ḥôbā) meant both "debt" and "sin," so the picture of sin as an unpaid debt came naturally. Luke's version of the prayer (Luke 11:4) says "forgive us our sins," then keeps the money picture: "for we ourselves forgive everyone who is indebted to us."
  • aphes → dimitte → forgive. Aphiēmi means "let go, release, send away." It was used for canceling a loan, freeing a prisoner, or leaving something behind. Jerome's dimitte has the same sense of "send off, release," and English "forgive" (literally "give away") carries the idea of handing over what was owed.
  • aphēkamen / aphiemen → dimittimus → have forgiven / forgive. The oldest manuscripts, followed by the SBLGNT and the ESV, use the aorist aphēkamen, "we have forgiven," as if the forgiving is already done before we ask. Later Byzantine manuscripts and the Textus Receptus behind the KJV read the present aphiemen, "we forgive," and Jerome's dimittimus is also present tense.

Alignment

Close, with a tense difference. All three languages keep the same money image of debts and debtors and the same link between God's forgiveness and ours. The only real shift is tense: the critical Greek text and the ESV say "have forgiven," while the Vulgate, the Byzantine Greek and the KJV say "forgive."

The story

Why do some churches pray "trespasses" when neither Matthew nor Luke says it? William Tyndale's 1526 English New Testament rendered this line "forgeve vs oure treaspases, even as we forgeve oure trespacers," borrowing the word from Jesus' comment two verses later (Matthew 6:14–15). The 1549 Book of Common Prayer kept "trespasses," and it stuck in Anglican and Catholic worship, while churches shaped by the Geneva Bible and the KJV kept "debts." One verse, two English prayers, and both trace back to the same Greek.
