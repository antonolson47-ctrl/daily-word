Verse a Day #5 · October 16, 2026

Matthew 6:11

Alignment: Notable difference

Setting the scene

Same hillside above the Sea of Galilee, near Capernaum, around AD 28–30, still early in Jesus' Galilean ministry and still in the Sermon on the Mount. After teaching his disciples to ask for God's kingdom and will, Jesus turns to something as ordinary as bread. The people listening knew hunger: day laborers often lived one meal ahead, and the memory of manna in the wilderness still shaped Jewish prayer. Under Rome, tax and tithe bit hard into a family's grain. Many expected a Messiah who would feed Israel the way God fed the exodus generation. Instead, a young rabbi from Nazareth tells them to ask the Father for enough for today—not for barns, not for Caesar's storehouses, just today's loaf. He speaks as a man in the flesh to people who did not yet know who was standing in front of them.

Four witnesses

Greek · SBLGNT
τὸν ἄρτον ἡμῶν τὸν ἐπιούσιον δὸς ἡμῖν σήμερον·
Ton arton hēmōn ton epiousion dos hēmin sēmeron;
Latin · Vulgate
Panem nostrum supersubstantialem da nobis hodie,
KJV
Give us this day our daily bread.
ESV
Give us this day our daily bread.

Key words

  • epiousios → supersubstantialem → daily. This adjective appears nowhere else in classical Greek; Origen said the evangelists coined it. Possible roots include epi + ousia ("for existence / needed for being") or hē epiousa ("the coming day"). Jerome rendered Matthew's line as supersubstantialem ("above-substance," later read eucharistically) while Luke's parallel often uses cotidianum ("daily"). English "daily" is the common gloss; the ESV footnote also offers "our bread for tomorrow."
  • artos → panem → bread. Ordinary loaf, not a fancy feast word. In Jewish ears it could also echo manna—the bread God gave one day at a time. The petition asks for food, not luxury.
  • dos / sēmeron → da / hodie → give / today. Dos is an aorist imperative: "give," a direct ask. Sēmeron / hodie anchors it to this day. Trust for tomorrow is left in the Father's hands.

Alignment

Notable difference. Greek epiousios is famously opaque. Jerome's supersubstantialem in Matthew leans toward a richer, even sacramental sense, while English "daily bread" (KJV and ESV) follows the everyday reading shared with Luke's cotidianum tradition. Sense overlaps—ask God for what sustains you—but the Latin and English do not land on the same nuance.

The story

Origen of Alexandria (c. AD 185–254), in his treatise On Prayer, paused over epiousios and admitted he knew no other Greek writer who used it. He concluded the evangelists invented the word for this prayer. Nearly eighteen centuries later, scholars still argue over it—one of the rare New Testament words that remains a genuine linguistic puzzle.
