Verse a Day #14 · October 25, 2026

Matthew 5:4

Alignment: Very close

Setting the scene

The same Galilean hillside near Capernaum, around AD 28. Jesus is seated, his disciples close, the crowds from Galilee, the Decapolis, Jerusalem and Judea pressing in behind them (Matthew 4:25–5:1). He has just pronounced the kingdom of heaven on the beggar-poor in spirit. Now comes the second blessing, and it goes to the people in the crowd who are grieving: those who have buried someone, those who weep over their own sin, those who mourn over the brokenness of their people. Many of them had come to be healed. He tells them their tears are not the end of the story.

Four witnesses

Greek · SBLGNT
Μακάριοι οἱ πενθοῦντες, ὅτι αὐτοὶ παρακληθήσονται.
Makarioi hoi penthountes, hoti autoi paraklēthēsontai.
Latin · Vulgate
Beati qui lugent: quoniam ipsi consolabuntur.
KJV
Blessed are they that mourn: for they shall be comforted.
ESV
"Blessed are those who mourn, for they shall be comforted."

Key words

  • Makarioi → Beati → Blessed. As in 5:3, a declaration with no verb: "Blessed, the mourning ones." Jesus is not wishing them well; he is announcing what is true of them before God.
  • penthountes → qui lugent → they that mourn. A present participle of pentheō, the word for grieving the dead and for open lament, not quiet sadness. It is the word used of the disciples "as they mourned and wept" after the crucifixion (Mark 16:10) and of James's call to "be wretched and mourn and weep" over sin (James 4:9). Lugeo in Latin is likewise the word for wearing mourning and lamenting the dead.
  • autoi → ipsi → they. Emphatic: "they themselves," these very people and no one else, will be comforted.
  • paraklēthēsontai → consolabuntur → shall be comforted. Future passive of parakaleō, "to call alongside, to comfort." The passive leaves the subject unspoken, a reverent way of saying God himself will do the comforting. The same root gives paraklēsis, the "consolation of Israel" that Simeon waited for (Luke 2:25), and Paraklētos, the "Comforter" Jesus promises to send (John 14:16).
  • hoti → quoniam → for. Each Beatitude gives its reason. Here the promise is future tense, unlike the "is" of 5:3: the comfort has begun in Christ but will be complete when God wipes away every tear (Revelation 21:4).

Alignment

Very close. Greek, Vulgate, KJV and ESV match word for word, and the ESV's "those who mourn" and the KJV's "they that mourn" are the same participle. The one real difference is the verse order. Most Greek editions (Nestle 1904, Westcott–Hort, the Byzantine Majority Text and the Textus Receptus) put "those who mourn" at 5:4 and "the meek" at 5:5. Tischendorf's 8th edition reverses them, following a minority of manuscripts led by Codex Bezae, and that reversed order is the one the Vulgate kept: its verse 4 is "Beati mites" (Blessed are the meek) and its verse 5 is "Beati qui lugent." Same words, same Lord, just a different order in the line.

The story

When Isaiah 61 was translated into Greek before Christ, the translators wrote that the Anointed One would come "to comfort all who mourn": parakalesai pantas tous penthountas. Those are the exact two verbs of Matthew 5:4. Anyone in the crowd who knew Isaiah could hear Jesus quietly claiming that promise as his own, the same chapter he read aloud in Nazareth, saying, "Today this Scripture has been fulfilled in your hearing" (Luke 4:21). Seventeen centuries later, Handel's Messiah (first performed in Dublin in 1742) gave its very first sung words to Isaiah 40:1, "Comfort ye, comfort ye my people," before it tells the story of Christ. The comfort of the second Beatitude is not a mood; it is a Person.

Dig Deeper: Roots in Hebrew & Jewish Sources

Matthew wrote in Greek, but "those who mourn" and "comforted" carry a long Hebrew story. The prophet Isaiah opens his great message of hope with nachamu, nachamu ammi, "Comfort, oh comfort My people" (Isaiah 40:1), and later describes an Anointed One sent "to comfort all who mourn," to give "the mourners in Zion" festive oil "instead of mourning" (Isaiah 61:2–3). The Hebrew word for the mourners there is avelim, the word used for people in grief for the dead. By Jesus' day, faithful Jews spoke of waiting for "the consolation of Israel," the comfort God had promised through the prophets; Luke uses exactly that phrase of Simeon, who takes the infant Jesus in his arms (Luke 2:25). The Greek Old Testament translated Isaiah 61:2 with the same two verbs Jesus uses here, "to comfort (parakalesai) all who mourn (penthountas)." That is why this Beatitude would have sounded to his hearers like the promise of Isaiah arriving in person. These sources are background study, not Scripture, and including them is not an endorsement of any later theology. Keep the dates straight: Isaiah is Hebrew Bible (Tanakh); the Great Isaiah Scroll from Qumran, which contains these chapters, is pre-Christian Dead Sea Scrolls material (about the 2nd century BC); later rabbinic writings like the Mishnah (about AD 200) and Talmud (about AD 400–600) are not sources Matthew used.

Where to dig

  1. Isaiah 40:1 in Hebrew and English, on Sefaria. Sefaria's free library of Jewish texts shows the double "nachamu, nachamu," "Comfort, oh comfort My people," side by side in Hebrew and English.
  2. Isaiah 61:2 in Hebrew and English, on Sefaria. The promise "to comfort all who mourn," continuing the passage Jesus read in the Nazareth synagogue (Luke 4:18–21).
  3. pentheō in the Blue Letter Bible Greek lexicon. The entry for Strong's 3996, the word for grieving the dead, with every New Testament use.
  4. parakaleō in the Blue Letter Bible Greek lexicon. The entry for Strong's 3870, "to call alongside, to comfort," the root behind paraklēsis ("consolation") and Paraklētos ("Comforter").
