Verse a Day #20 · October 31, 2026

Matthew 5:10

Alignment: Very close

Setting the scene

Still the hillside above the Sea of Galilee near Capernaum, around AD 28. This is the eighth and last of the short "Blessed are" sayings, and it closes the circle: it ends with the same promise as the first, "theirs is the kingdom of heaven." Jesus has just blessed the peacemakers, and now he is honest about what that kind of life can bring. Galilee was ruled by Herod Antipas, who would soon imprison and behead John the Baptist for speaking the truth. Jesus' first hearers did not have to imagine persecution for doing right. They had watched it happen.

Four witnesses

Greek · SBLGNT
μακάριοι οἱ δεδιωγμένοι ἕνεκεν δικαιοσύνης, ὅτι αὐτῶν ἐστιν ἡ βασιλεία τῶν οὐρανῶν.
makarioi hoi dediōgmenoi heneken dikaiosynēs, hoti autōn estin hē basileia tōn ouranōn.
Latin · Vulgate
Beati qui persecutionem patiuntur propter justitiam: quoniam ipsorum est regnum cælorum.
KJV
Blessed are they which are persecuted for righteousness' sake: for theirs is the kingdom of heaven.
ESV
"Blessed are those who are persecuted for righteousness' sake, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven."

Key words

  • makarioi → Beati → Blessed. The eighth and final verbless declaration: "Blessed, the persecuted." God's verdict, not their feelings.
  • dediōgmenoi → qui persecutionem patiuntur → they which are persecuted. A perfect passive participle of diōkō, "to pursue, to chase." The perfect tense pictures people who have been hunted and still carry the marks. Jerome turns one Greek word into a phrase, "who suffer persecution," and his Latin persequor ("to follow all the way through") gives English "persecute."
  • heneken dikaiosynēs → propter justitiam → for righteousness' sake. Not for any trouble, but trouble that comes because they live rightly. Dikaiosynē is the same righteousness they hungered for in 5:6.
  • autōn estin → ipsorum est → theirs is. Present tense, exactly as in 5:3. The kingdom is already theirs, not only someday.
  • basileia tōn ouranōn → regnum cælorum → the kingdom of heaven. Matthew's reverent way of saying "kingdom of God," the reign of God that brackets all eight Beatitudes.

Alignment

Very close. The Greek editions on Bible Hub (Nestle 1904, Westcott–Hort, the Byzantine Majority Text, Tischendorf and both Textus Receptus editions) agree word for word, differing only in accents and punctuation. The Vulgate expands the single Greek participle into "who suffer persecution" but keeps the meaning. The KJV and ESV are nearly identical, differing only in "they which" versus "those who."

The story

Because 5:3 and 5:10 end with the same promise, many scholars see the eight Beatitudes as a single framed unit, a set of bookends around the kingdom. Then Jesus turns straight to his listeners in 5:11: "Blessed are ye, when men shall revile you, and persecute you... for my sake." In 5:10 it is "for righteousness' sake," and in 5:11 it is "for my sake," quietly placing himself where righteousness stands. And 5:12 reminds them that "so persecuted they the prophets which were before you."

Dig Deeper: Roots in Hebrew & Jewish Sources

Jesus' listeners already knew that standing with God's righteousness could cost you. The prophet Isaiah told "you who know righteousness" not to fear the reproach of men (Isaiah 51:7), and the book of Daniel pictured God's "holy ones" being worn down by a hostile power, then receiving a kingdom that lasts forever (Daniel 7:21 and 7:27). That is very close to the shape of this Beatitude: persecuted now, given the kingdom. In the century before Jesus, the Dead Sea Scrolls community wrote about its own "Teacher of Righteousness" being hunted by a "Wicked Priest" (the Habakkuk Commentary, 1QpHab, 1st century BC), so "persecuted for righteousness" was a live idea in Jesus' day, not an abstraction. Much later, the Babylonian Talmud keeps a saying of Rabbi Abbahu (about AD 300): "A person should always be among those who are pursued and not among the pursuers." These links are background study tools, not Scripture, and including them is not an endorsement of any later theology. Keep the dates straight: Isaiah and Daniel are Hebrew Bible (Tanakh), written before Christ; the Habakkuk Commentary is a pre-Christian Dead Sea Scroll; the Talmud was compiled centuries after Jesus. The Dead Sea Scroll is described here but not linked, because its official online viewer couldn't be reached to check today.

Where to dig

  1. Isaiah 51:7 in Hebrew and English, on Sefaria. Sefaria's free library of Jewish texts shows the prophet's word to those who know righteousness: do not fear people's insults.
  2. Daniel 7:27 in Hebrew/Aramaic and English, on Sefaria. The everlasting kingdom given to "the holy ones of the Most High" after their suffering (this part of Daniel is written in Aramaic).
  3. Bava Kamma 93a, on Sefaria. The Talmud page with Rabbi Abbahu's saying about being among the pursued rather than the pursuers, a later rabbinic parallel.
  4. diōkō in the Blue Letter Bible Greek lexicon. The entry for Strong's 1377, "to pursue, to persecute," the verb behind dediōgmenoi in this verse.
