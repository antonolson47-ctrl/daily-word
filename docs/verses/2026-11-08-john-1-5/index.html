Verse a Day #28 · November 8, 2026

John 1:5

Alignment: Very close across Greek, Latin and English

Setting the scene

John begins his Gospel where Genesis begins: "In the beginning." Before any manger, before any miracle, he shows us the eternal Word who was with God and was God, through whom all things were made (John 1:1-3). Then he says, "In him was life, and the life was the light of men" (John 1:4). Today's verse follows at once, and it is one of the most hopeful sentences ever written. John looks at a world darkened by sin and sorrow and announces that the light of Christ keeps shining in it, and the darkness has never been able to put it out. In a few verses he will tell us that this light came into the world and was not received by many (John 1:9-11). Yet the light keeps shining, all the way to the cross and out of the empty tomb.

Four witnesses

Greek · SBLGNT
καὶ τὸ φῶς ἐν τῇ σκοτίᾳ φαίνει, καὶ ἡ σκοτία αὐτὸ οὐ κατέλαβεν.
kai to phōs en tē skotia phainei, kai hē skotia auto ou katelaben.
Latin · Vulgate
et lux in tenebris lucet, et tenebræ eam non comprehenderunt.
KJV
And the light shineth in darkness; and the darkness comprehended it not.
ESV
The light shines in the darkness, and the darkness has not overcome it.

Key words

  • phōs → lux → light. The light of John 1:4 is the life of the Word himself. Later Jesus will say plainly, "I am the light of the world" (John 8:12). This is not a vague glow of good ideas; it is a Person.
  • phainei → lucet → shines. Present tense in Greek and Latin alike: the light is shining, and keeps shining. John moves from "was" in verses 1-4 to "shines" here, as if to say this is still true today.
  • skotia → tenebræ → darkness. In John, darkness is more than the absence of daylight. It is the world turned away from God: sin, blindness, and fear (John 3:19; 12:35). Notice that the light shines in the darkness, not only beside it. Christ comes right into it.
  • katelaben → comprehenderunt → comprehended / overcome. The richest word of the day. Katalambanō means to seize, lay hold of, grasp. That can mean grasping with the mind (understanding) or seizing with force (overpowering, overtaking). Jerome chose comprehenderunt, which in Latin can mean both "grasped" and "seized," and the KJV followed with "comprehended." Modern versions like the ESV choose "overcome," the sense John uses in 12:35, "lest darkness overtake you." Both are true of the Gospel story: the darkness neither understood the light nor could snuff it out.

Alignment

Very close across Greek, Latin and English. Nestle 1904, Westcott–Hort, the Byzantine Majority, the Greek Orthodox text, Tischendorf and both Textus Receptus editions read the same Greek, word for word. Jerome's Vulgate follows the Greek almost exactly: phōs / lux, skotia / tenebræ, phainei / lucet, katelaben / comprehenderunt. The one real translation choice is katelaben. The KJV, Douay-Rheims and NASB 1995 say "comprehended"; the ASV says "apprehended"; Young's says "did not perceive"; the ESV, NIV and NAB say "has not overcome." The Greek can carry both meanings, and the early Church Fathers read it both ways, so neither rendering is wrong; each brings out a different side of one truth.

The story

This verse has been a lamp for Christians in hard seasons for two thousand years. Every Easter Vigil, in a darkened church, a single paschal candle is lit and carried in while the deacon sings "Lumen Christi" (the Light of Christ), and the light spreads candle to candle until the whole church glows. That ancient liturgy is John 1:5 acted out: one light, carried into the darkness, and the darkness cannot stop it. The same hope stands behind the old saying that it is better to light one candle than to curse the darkness. When the world feels dim, John does not tell us the darkness is unreal. He tells us something better: the Light is already shining in it, and the darkness has not won, and will not win.

Dig Deeper: Roots in Hebrew & Jewish Sources

John 1:5 reaches all the way back to the first page of the Hebrew Bible. John has just opened his Gospel with "In the beginning" (the same words that open Genesis), and now light shines in darkness, just as on the first day when God said, "Let there be light," and separated the light from the darkness (Genesis 1:3-4). The prophets carried that picture forward as a promise: "The people that walked in darkness have seen a great light" (Isaiah 9:2 in English Bibles, 9:1 in the Hebrew numbering), a verse Matthew 4:16 applies to Jesus beginning his ministry in Galilee. Among the Dead Sea Scrolls, copied by a Jewish community in the last centuries before Christ and into the first century AD, the War Scroll and the Community Rule speak of "the sons of light" and "the sons of darkness." That vocabulary shows the light-and-darkness language John uses was alive in Jewish speech of his day; it is background, not a claim that John borrowed from that community, and the Scrolls are not Scripture. Later rabbinic writings (Mishnah and Talmud, compiled from about AD 200 onward) also reflect on the light of creation, but they are later than John. Dig Deeper here is background study, not new Scripture, and not an endorsement of any later theology.

Where to dig

  1. Genesis 1:3-4 in Hebrew and English, on Sefaria. "Let there be light": the first light God speaks into the darkness, which John's prologue deliberately echoes.
  2. Isaiah 9:1 (English 9:2) in Hebrew and English, on Sefaria. The prophet's promise of a great light dawning on people who walked in darkness, fulfilled in Christ.
  3. The 4Q War Scroll, Leon Levy Dead Sea Scrolls Digital Library (Israel Antiquities Authority). Photographed fragments from Qumran Cave 4 of the War Scroll, a Jewish text from before and around the time of Christ, built around the struggle of the "sons of light" against the "sons of darkness."
  4. Blue Letter Bible lexicon for καταλαμβάνω (katalambanō). The verb behind "comprehended / overcome," with every New Testament use, so you can see why translators render it both ways.
