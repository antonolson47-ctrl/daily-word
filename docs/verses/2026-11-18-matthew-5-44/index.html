Verse a Day #38 · November 18, 2026

Matthew 5:44

Alignment: Close in meaning, with a well-known difference in length

Setting the scene

Jesus is still seated on the mountain in Galilee, teaching his disciples in the Sermon on the Mount. After calling them the salt of the earth and the light of the world, he takes up the Law and goes to its heart in six sayings that begin "Ye have heard that it was said… but I say unto you." This is the sixth and last of them. "Ye have heard that it hath been said, Thou shalt love thy neighbour, and hate thine enemy" (5:43). The first half is the Law itself, Leviticus 19:18. The second half is found nowhere in the Law; it was the way some people narrowed the commandment. Then the Lord speaks with his own authority, and the circle of love opens wide enough to take in the very people who hate and hunt his disciples. The reason follows at once: "that ye may be the children of your Father which is in heaven: for he maketh his sun to rise on the evil and on the good, and sendeth rain on the just and on the unjust" (5:45).

Four witnesses

Greek · SBLGNT
ἐγὼ δὲ λέγω ὑμῖν, ἀγαπᾶτε τοὺς ἐχθροὺς ὑμῶν καὶ προσεύχεσθε ὑπὲρ τῶν διωκόντων ὑμᾶς·
egō de legō hymin, agapate tous echthrous hymōn kai proseuchesthe hyper tōn diōkontōn hymas.
Latin · Vulgate
Ego autem dico vobis: Diligite inimicos vestros, benefacite his qui oderunt vos, et orate pro persequentibus et calumniantibus vos.
KJV
But I say unto you, Love your enemies, bless them that curse you, do good to them that hate you, and pray for them which despitefully use you, and persecute you;
ESV
But I say to you, Love your enemies and pray for those who persecute you,

Key words

  • egō de legō hymin → ego autem dico vobis → but I say unto you. The pronoun egō is emphatic: "But I, I say to you." No prophet ever spoke this way; the prophets said, "Thus saith the LORD." Here the Lord who gave the Law speaks in his own name, and at the end of the Sermon the crowds are astonished, "for he taught them as one having authority" (7:29).
  • agapate → diligite → love. A present imperative, so it means "keep on loving." This is agapē love, the self-giving love of God, and it is a command about what we do and will, not only about how we feel. In Latin, diligo carries the sense of choosing and esteeming someone.
  • tous echthrous hymōn → inimicos vestros → your enemies. Echthros comes from a root meaning "to hate": the one who is hostile to you. Latin inimicus is literally "not a friend" (in + amicus). Jesus speaks of personal foes, not abstractions.
  • proseuchesthe hyper tōn diōkontōn hymas → orate pro persequentibus → pray for those who persecute you. Hyper means "on behalf of." Diōkō, to pursue or hunt down, is the same verb used in the last Beatitude: "Blessed are they which are persecuted" (5:10). Prayer is the first thing love can do for an enemy, even when nothing else is possible.

Alignment

Close in meaning, with a well-known difference in length. The critical Greek texts on Bible Hub (Nestle 1904, Westcott–Hort with NA27/UBS4, Tischendorf) have the short form: love your enemies and pray for your persecutors, which the ESV follows. The Byzantine Majority Text, the Greek Orthodox text and both Textus Receptus editions add "bless them that curse you, do good to them that hate you" and "them which despitefully use you" (epēreazontōn), which the KJV follows. These added phrases match Luke's version of the same teaching (Luke 6:27-28), and many scholars think copyists brought them over from Luke. The Vulgate stands in between: it adds "do good to them that hate you" (benefacite his qui oderunt vos) and "calumniate you" (calumniantibus), which the Douay-Rheims renders "Love your enemies: do good to them that hate you: and pray for them that persecute and calumniate you." In every form, the core command is the same: love your enemies and pray for them.

The story

The first Christians took this word literally. St Stephen, the first martyr, knelt while the stones were flying and cried, "Lord, lay not this sin to their charge" (Acts 7:60), and a young man named Saul stood by, holding the coats (7:58). Saul became St Paul. The Didache, one of the oldest Christian writings outside the New Testament (late 1st or early 2nd century), teaches new believers: "Bless those who curse you, and pray for your enemies, and fast for those who persecute you… But love those who hate you, and you shall not have an enemy." Nineteen centuries later Corrie ten Boom, who had lost her father and her sister Betsie after their family hid Jews from the Nazis, told how in 1947, after she spoke in Munich about God's forgiveness, a former guard from Ravensbrück came up to shake her hand. She could not feel forgiveness, so she prayed and put out her hand, and she said that love for him then flooded her heart. "Love your enemies" is a command Christ gives together with the grace to keep it.

Dig Deeper: Roots in Hebrew & Jewish Sources

Jesus frames this verse against Israel's own Scriptures, so the Hebrew roots matter. "Thou shalt love thy neighbour" is Leviticus 19:18, v'ahavta l're'acha kamocha (וְאָהַבְתָּ לְרֵעֲךָ כָּמוֹךָ), which opens with "Thou shalt not avenge, nor bear any grudge." The Law already reached toward the enemy: "If thou meet thine enemy's ox or his ass going astray, thou shalt surely bring it back to him again" (Exodus 23:4), and Proverbs taught, "If thine enemy be hungry, give him bread to eat" (25:21), a verse St Paul quotes in Romans 12:20. But nowhere does the Law say "hate thine enemy." That phrase reflects attitudes found in some circles of Jesus' own day. Before Christ, the Dead Sea Scrolls community pledged in its Community Rule (1QS 1:9-11) "to love all the Sons of Light… and to hate all the Sons of Darkness." Against such narrowing, Jesus returns to the generous heart of the Law and fulfills it: his disciples are to love as the Father loves, who sends sun and rain on all. Dig Deeper is background study, not new Scripture, and not an endorsement of any later theology.

Where to dig

  1. Leviticus 19:18 in Hebrew and English, on Sefaria. The commandment Jesus quotes, "love your neighbor as yourself," with its call to take no vengeance and bear no grudge.
  2. Exodus 23:4 in Hebrew and English, on Sefaria. The Law tells Israel to return an enemy's stray ox or donkey, kindness to a foe already built into the Torah.
  3. Proverbs 25:21 in Hebrew and English, on Sefaria. "If your enemy is hungry, give him bread to eat," the proverb St Paul quotes in Romans 12:20.
  4. Ari Mermelstein, "Love and Hate at Qumran" (Yeshiva University). A scholarly article (PDF) that quotes and explains the Community Rule's "love the Sons of Light, hate the Sons of Darkness."

A quick timeline, so the sources don't blur together

Source Date What it is
Exodus, Leviticus, Proverbs (Tanakh / Old Testament) Hebrew Scriptures, written and gathered over many centuries before Christ Love your neighbor; help your enemy's animal; feed your hungry enemy
Dead Sea Scrolls (Community Rule, 1QS) copy from about the 1st century BC, before Christ A Jewish community pledging to love insiders and hate "the Sons of Darkness"
Gospel of Matthew 1st century AD "Love your enemies, and pray for them which persecute you"
Didache late 1st or early 2nd century AD Early Christian teaching: "pray for your enemies"
