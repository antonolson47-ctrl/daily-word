Verse a Day #13 · October 24, 2026

Matthew 5:3

Alignment: Exceptionally close

Setting the scene

Galilee, a hillside near Capernaum on the northwest shore of the Sea of Galilee, around AD 28. Jesus has called his first fishermen (Matthew 4:18–22) and has been healing "every disease and every affliction" across the region. Great crowds have followed him from Galilee, the Decapolis, Jerusalem, Judea and beyond the Jordan (Matthew 4:25). Seeing them, he goes up on the mountain, sits down the way a rabbi sits to teach, and his disciples gather close. The first words of the Sermon on the Mount are not a command but a blessing, and it lands on the people least likely to expect one.

Four witnesses

Greek · SBLGNT
Μακάριοι οἱ πτωχοὶ τῷ πνεύματι, ὅτι αὐτῶν ἐστιν ἡ βασιλεία τῶν οὐρανῶν.
Makarioi hoi ptōchoi tō pneumati, hoti autōn estin hē basileia tōn ouranōn.
Latin · Vulgate
Beati pauperes spiritu: quoniam ipsorum est regnum cælorum.
KJV
Blessed are the poor in spirit: for theirs is the kingdom of heaven.
ESV
"Blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven."

Key words

  • Makarioi → Beati → Blessed. Not a wish ("may you be blessed") but a declaration: there is no verb in the Greek, just "Blessed, the poor in spirit." Makarios describes the deep well-being that comes from God's favor, not a passing mood. From Beati comes the English word "Beatitudes."
  • ptōchoi → pauperes → poor. Greek had two words for poor: penēs, the working poor who scrape by, and ptōchos, the beggar who crouches with nothing at all. Jesus chose the stronger word. Luke's version reads simply "Blessed are you who are poor" (Luke 6:20).
  • tō pneumati → spiritu → in spirit. A dative of respect: poor with regard to the spirit. It does not mean poor in courage or poor in the Holy Spirit; it means the person who knows they stand before God with empty hands.
  • basileia tōn ouranōn → regnum cælorum → the kingdom of heaven. Matthew's usual phrase where Mark and Luke say "kingdom of God," following the Jewish habit of reverently saying "heaven" in place of God's name. It is the same kingdom Jesus has just announced as "at hand" (Matthew 4:17).
  • hoti autōn estin → quoniam ipsorum est → for theirs is. Present tense, and emphatic: the kingdom belongs to them now, not only someday. This verse and the eighth Beatitude (5:10) both say "is," bracketing the future-tense promises in between.

Alignment

Exceptionally close. Greek, Latin, KJV and ESV line up word for word, and the Greek editions agree (the only differences are punctuation and capitalization). The one translation nuance is the Latin spiritu: pauperes spiritu is so compact that some later readers took it to mean "poor-spirited," but Jerome kept the Greek structure exactly.

The story

Augustine wrote a whole commentary on the Sermon on the Mount around AD 394 and noticed that the first Beatitude and the last both promise "the kingdom of heaven," so he read the eight as a ladder of the Christian life that begins and ends in the same place, starting with humility. He tied poverty of spirit to Isaiah 66:2, where God says he looks to the one who is "humble and contrite in spirit." Centuries later Martin Luther made the same point on his deathbed in 1546: one of the last notes he wrote reads, "We are beggars. This is true." The Sermon on the Mount opens with good news for exactly those people.

Dig Deeper: Roots in Hebrew & Jewish Sources

Matthew wrote in Greek, but "the poor" who are blessed here have deep Hebrew roots. In the Hebrew Scriptures the ʿanavim and ʿaniyyim ("the poor, the lowly, the afflicted") are not only people without money but people who depend on God because they have no one else, and the Psalms keep promising that God hears them. Jesus' first Beatitude echoes Isaiah 61:1, "The Spirit of the Lord GOD is upon me... to bring good news to the poor," the very passage he reads aloud in the Nazareth synagogue in Luke 4:18, and Isaiah 66:2, where God looks to the one who is "humble and contrite in spirit." Psalm 34:18 says the LORD saves "the crushed in spirit." Most striking of all, the exact phrase "poor in spirit" (ʿanvei ruaḥ) appears in Hebrew in the War Scroll from the Dead Sea Scrolls (1QM, column 14, line 7), written before Jesus' ministry, where it describes the humble faithful whom God strengthens. That shows Jesus was speaking a phrase his Jewish hearers could recognize. These sources are background study, not Scripture, and including them is not an endorsement of any later theology. Keep the dates straight: Isaiah and the Psalms are Hebrew Bible (Tanakh); the War Scroll is pre-Christian Dead Sea Scrolls material (about the 1st century BC); later rabbinic writings like the Mishnah (about AD 200) and Talmud (about AD 400–600) are not sources Matthew used.

Where to dig

  1. Isaiah 61:1 in Hebrew and English, on Sefaria. Sefaria's free library of Jewish texts shows the "good news to the poor" promise side by side in Hebrew and English, the passage Jesus later applies to himself in Luke 4.
  2. Isaiah 66:2 in Hebrew and English, on Sefaria. The verse where God says he looks to the one who is "humble and contrite in spirit," which Augustine connected to this Beatitude.
  3. The War Scroll in the Israel Museum's Digital Dead Sea Scrolls. High-resolution images and an introduction to the pre-Christian scroll from Qumran Cave 1 whose column 14 uses the Hebrew phrase "poor in spirit."
  4. ptōchos in the Blue Letter Bible Greek lexicon. The entry for Strong's 4434, the "beggar-poor" word Jesus chose, with every New Testament use.
