Verse a Day #24 · November 4, 2026

John 8:32

Alignment: Exact

Setting the scene

Jerusalem, in the temple courts, during or just after the Feast of Tabernacles (John 7:2, 7:37), about six months before the cross. Jesus has been teaching "in the treasury" (John 8:20), calling himself "the light of the world" (John 8:12) and warning that "ye shall die in your sins" unless they believe that "I am he" (John 8:24). As he speaks, "many believed on him" (John 8:30). So he turns to those new believers: "If ye continue in my word, then are ye my disciples indeed" (John 8:31). This verse finishes the sentence. The very next line shows how it landed: "We be Abraham's seed, and were never in bondage to any man: how sayest thou, Ye shall be made free?" (John 8:33).

Four witnesses

Greek · SBLGNT
καὶ γνώσεσθε τὴν ἀλήθειαν, καὶ ἡ ἀλήθεια ἐλευθερώσει ὑμᾶς.
kai gnōsesthe tēn alētheian, kai hē alētheia eleutherōsei hymas.
Latin · Vulgate
et cognoscetis veritatem, et veritas liberabit vos.
KJV
And ye shall know the truth, and the truth shall make you free.
ESV
and you will know the truth, and the truth will set you free.

Key words

  • gnōsesthe → cognoscetis → ye shall know. Future of ginōskō, "to come to know." In John this is personal, relational knowing, not just collecting facts; it is the same verb Jesus uses in John 17:3, "this is life eternal, that they might know thee." It comes as the result of verse 31: continue in his word, then you will know.
  • alētheia → veritas → truth. From a-lēthēs, literally "not hidden" or "not forgotten": what is real and unconcealed. John uses the word about 25 times in his Gospel, and six chapters later Jesus says it of himself: "I am the way, the truth, and the life" (John 14:6). The Greek repeats it with the article both times, "the truth... the truth," and so does every translation.
  • eleutherōsei → liberabit → shall make free / will set free. Future of eleutheroō, "to liberate," from eleutheros, a free person as opposed to a slave (doulos). The word sets up the argument that follows: "Whosoever committeth sin is the servant (doulos) of sin... If the Son therefore shall make you free, ye shall be free indeed" (John 8:34, 36).
  • hymas → vos → you. Plural: Jesus is speaking to the whole group of new believers, not one person.

Alignment

Exact. All the major Greek editions (Nestle 1904, Westcott–Hort, the Byzantine Majority Text, Tischendorf, the Greek Orthodox text and both Textus Receptus editions) agree word for word, differing only in a comma. Jerome's Latin follows the Greek one word for one word: cognoscetis for gnōsesthe, veritatem and veritas for the two alētheia, liberabit for eleutherōsei. The KJV's "make you free" and the ESV's "set you free" are both fair renderings of the single verb eleutheroō; the Douay-Rheims, from the Latin, reads the same as the KJV.

The story

This may be the most carved verse in America. "Veritas vos liberabit," Jerome's Latin word for word, is the motto of Johns Hopkins University, and Caltech's motto is the English, "The truth shall make you free." The KJV wording is also cut into the marble wall of the original CIA headquarters lobby in Langley, Virginia. But on buildings the first half of the sentence usually gets left off. In John the promise is conditional: "If ye continue in my word... ye shall know the truth" (John 8:31-32). And the truth in view is not knowledge in general but Jesus himself, the Son who makes people "free indeed" (John 8:36). His listeners bristled because they heard "free" politically; Jesus meant freedom from sin.

Dig Deeper: Roots in Hebrew & Jewish Sources

Jesus spoke this verse to Jewish listeners in the temple, and both halves of it, "truth" and "freedom," carried deep Hebrew roots. Behind the Greek alētheia stands the Hebrew emet, which means not only accurate but firm, faithful and reliable; it is a quality of God himself ("abundant in goodness and truth," Exodus 34:6). Freedom was Israel's founding story: God brought the people out of slavery in Egypt, which is why the crowd's reply, "we were never in bondage to any man," is so striking. The prophets promised a greater release still: the anointed one would "proclaim liberty to the captives" (Isaiah 61:1), the passage Jesus read aloud in the Nazareth synagogue (Luke 4:18). Two Jewish sources outside the Bible help, too. The Dead Sea Scrolls' Community Rule, copied about a century before Jesus, speaks of "the spirit of truth," "sons of truth" and walking in truth versus deceit, language strikingly close to John's. And a later rabbinic saying links freedom to God's word in a way that echoes "continue in my word": on the stone tablets the writing was "graven" (charut), and the rabbis taught, "read not charut but cherut (freedom), for there is no free man but one who occupies himself with the study of Torah." These links are background study tools, not new Scripture, and including them is not an endorsement of any later theology. Keep the dates straight: Exodus and Isaiah are Hebrew Bible (Tanakh), written centuries before Christ; the Community Rule is pre-Christian Dead Sea Scrolls material; John wrote in the late 1st century AD; and the "graven/freedom" saying comes from the sixth chapter of Pirkei Avot, a later addition to the Mishnah attributed to rabbis who lived after Jesus.

Where to dig

  1. Exodus 32:16 in Hebrew and English, on Sefaria. The verse about the writing of God "graven upon the tables," the Hebrew word (charut) behind the rabbis' later wordplay on freedom.
  2. Pirkei Avot 6:2, on Sefaria. Rabbi Joshua ben Levi's saying, "Read not charut but cherut... there is no free man but one who occupies himself with the study of Torah," a later Jewish parallel to freedom through abiding in God's word.
  3. Isaiah 61:1 in Hebrew and English, on Sefaria. "To proclaim liberty to the captives," the messianic promise of release that Jesus claimed for himself in Luke 4:18.
  4. The Community Rule (1QS), Digital Dead Sea Scrolls at the Israel Museum. High-resolution images and background on the pre-Christian Qumran scroll whose "spirit of truth" and "sons of truth" language is often compared with John's Gospel.
