History. A Benedictine abbey founded in the 10th century on a granite island in a bay with some of the largest tides in Europe. It grew into the Gothic "Merveille" and later served as a prison. The Mont and its bay became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1979 (UNESCO).

Legend. In 708 the Archangel appeared three times to Aubert, Bishop of Avranches, asking for a sanctuary on the rock. When Aubert hesitated, Michael pressed a finger into his skull. A skull with a hole, said to be Aubert's, is kept at the Basilica of Saint-Gervais in Avranches.

The Mont in depth

Founding stories: legend and history

History or legend? The founding story comes from the Revelatio ecclesiae sancti Michaelis, a text written in the early 9th century, about a hundred years after the events. Aubert was a real bishop of Avranches in the early 8th century. The three apparitions, the finger in the skull and the miracles are legend. The arrival of Benedictine monks in 966, the Romanesque abbey and the Gothic Merveille are documented history.

Aubert's dream. The rock was then called Mont Tombe and stood in the forest of Scissy. According to the Revelatio, in 708 the Archangel Michael appeared in a dream to Aubert, Bishop of Avranches, and told him to build a church on the rock. Aubert thought the dream was a trick of the devil and did nothing. Michael came again, and again Aubert hesitated. The third time, the Archangel pressed his finger into the bishop's head, leaving a mark. Only then did Aubert obey.

The signs on the rock. The story goes on with three signs: - A bull, hidden by thieves on the summit, showed where the church should stand. This echoes the Gargano bull at Monte Sant'Angelo. - Morning dew left one patch dry, which marked out its round shape. - A huge rock that no one could move was toppled when a child touched it with his foot.

Aubert also sent monks to Monte Sant'Angelo in Italy to fetch relics: a piece of the red cloak Michael left there, and a fragment of the marble altar where he stood. Aubert's church was consecrated on 16 October 709, a date still celebrated as the feast of St Michael "in Monte Tumba". Tradition says the forest was swallowed by a great tide at the same time, leaving the rock as an island. Geologists date the bay's formation much earlier.

The history. Aubert set up a small group of canons. In 966 Duke Richard I of Normandy replaced them with Benedictine monks. The abbey grew rich on pilgrims and land, and in the 11th century the monks built a Romanesque church on the summit, resting on crypts that wrap around the rock. In 1204 Breton allies of the French king burned part of the abbey. The king, Philip Augustus, then paid for the Gothic Merveille, built roughly 1211–1228. In the Hundred Years' War the Mont held out against the English for some 30 years and was never taken. Two English bombards (giant cannons), the Michelettes, abandoned in 1434, still sit by the entrance gate. After the French Revolution the abbey was turned into a prison until 1863, and the state has looked after it since 1874. Since 2001 the Monastic Fraternities of Jerusalem have lived there and keep up daily prayer. The Mont and its bay were listed by UNESCO in 1979.

Architecture and special features

  • The abbey church. It sits at the summit, about 80 m up. The nave is Romanesque (11th–12th century). The choir is Flamboyant Gothic, rebuilt after it collapsed in 1421. The spire carries a gilded statue of St Michael by Emmanuel Frémiet (1897), some 150 m above the bay.
  • The Merveille ("the Marvel"). It is a three-storey Gothic block on the north side, and the levels match the medieval order of society. Alms for the poor were given at the bottom, guests were received in the middle, and monks lived at the top. It holds:
  • the Almonry and Cellar;
  • the Guests' Hall and the Knights' Hall (in fact the monks' scriptorium);
  • the refectory, with its narrow, deep-set windows;
  • the cloister, which seems to hang in the sky. Its double rows of slender columns are set out of line so the eye never rests.
  • The crypts. These are the Crypt of the Great Pillars, the Chapel of St Martin and the Chapel of Our Lady Underground (Notre-Dame-sous-Terre). Notre-Dame-sous-Terre is a 10th-century pre-Romanesque church, perhaps on the site of Aubert's first sanctuary.
  • The great wheel. This treadmill wheel dates from the prison years. Prisoners walked inside it to haul supplies up the cliff.
  • The ramparts and Grande Rue. The 15th-century walls, the King's Gate and the narrow main street were where pilgrims bought badges, lodged and ate, as visitors still do.

Relics and sacred objects

  • The skull of St Aubert is kept in the treasury of the Basilica of Saint-Gervais in Avranches, in a gilded reliquary. It has an oval hole in it, said to be from the Archangel's finger. Scientists who have studied the skull suggest the hole may come from surgery (trepanation) or from a natural defect. Its identification as Aubert's is traditional.
  • The relics from Gargano, the piece of the red cloak and the fragment of the altar, were famous in the Middle Ages. They did not survive the Revolution.
  • The manuscripts. The abbey's great library of more than 200 medieval manuscripts was moved to Avranches. Many are now shown at the Scriptorial d'Avranches museum, including the oldest copies of the Revelatio.
  • Pilgrim badges made of lead, showing Michael and the dragon, are found by archaeologists across Europe. They are the best evidence of how many people came.

Notable pilgrims through history

  • Kings of France. Philip IV, Charles VI and Louis XI all came. Louis XI founded the chivalric Order of St Michael in 1469, taking the Archangel as the patron of France.
  • The pastoureaux (child pilgrims). In the 14th and 15th centuries bands of children walked to the Mont from Germany and elsewhere, sometimes against their parents' will. Chroniclers called them "miquelots".
  • Harold Godwinson. The Bayeux Tapestry shows him pulling Norman soldiers out of the quicksand of the Couesnon near the Mont during Duke William's Breton campaign around 1064.
  • Pilgrims to Santiago often added the Mont to their journey. The "Chemins du Mont-Saint-Michel", the old pilgrim routes, are signposted again today.

The surrounding area

The landscape. The bay has some of the largest tides in continental Europe, with a range of up to about 14 m at the highest spring tides. The tide comes in quickly over flat sand and the bay has quicksands. A large project finished in 2015 cleared the silted bay, built a dam on the Couesnon river and replaced the old causeway road with a light bridge, so that at very high tides the Mont is an island again. Look out for salt-meadow sheep grazing on the tidal flats.

Local food. - Omelette de la Mère Poulard, a puffy omelette beaten in copper bowls since the 1880s. It is a tourist ritual, and pricey. - Agneau de pré-salé, the salt-meadow lamb of the bay, which has a protected appellation. - Oysters from Cancale, Norman cider, crêpes and galettes (Brittany starts at the Couesnon), and butter biscuits.

Getting there. Take the train to Rennes (TGV from Paris in about 1.5 hours), then the direct coach to the Mont (about 1h10). You can also take the train to Pontorson and a local bus. Cars must park on the mainland. The free shuttle (Le Passeur) or a 45-minute walk brings you to the gate. To walk across the bay, you must go with a licensed guide, setting off from Genêts or Bec d'Andaine.

Best time to visit. Spring and autumn have fewer crowds. Come early or stay into the evening, when the day visitors have gone and the abbey sometimes holds evening openings in summer. The equinox spring tides (March and September) are the most dramatic.

Nearby sights. - Avranches, with the Scriptorial and the Saint-Gervais treasury holding the skull. - Tombelaine, the islet in the bay (a bird reserve). - Cancale for oysters. - Saint-Malo, the walled corsair city. - Granville, a harbour town on its headland. - The Abbey of Hambye.
