St Michael's MountMarazion, Cornwall, England

Photo: St Michael's Mount from Marazion beach by Chris Gorringe, CC BY-SA 2.0, via Wikimedia Commons

CountryEngland
TypeTidal island
AccessCauseway at low tide
Run byNational Trust & St Aubyn family

History. A tidal island crowned by a castle and church. In the Middle Ages it was a priory of the Benedictine abbey of Mont-Saint-Michel, until England dissolved its "alien priories" in 1414. It later became a fortress. Colonel John St Aubyn bought it in 1659, and his family still lives in the castle. In 1954 the 3rd Lord St Levan gave most of the island to the National Trust, and the family kept a 999-year lease to live there.

Legend. Fishermen are said to have seen St Michael standing on the rock (traditionally in AD 495). Cornish folklore also says the giant Cormoran lived here until Jack the Giant Killer defeated him.

The Mount in depth

Founding stories: legend and history

History or legend? The apparition of 495 is a legend: no record from that time exists. The link with Mont-Saint-Michel in Normandy is history, although the famous charter in which Edward the Confessor gives the Mount to the Norman abbey is thought by historians to be a later forgery or reworking. The Benedictine priory, the 1135 building work, the miracles of 1262–63 and the St Aubyn purchase are all documented.

The fishermen's vision. The tradition, retold by the Mount itself and by Marazion Town Council, dates the first apparition to 495 AD. Fishermen in Mount's Bay were being drawn onto the rocks, some versions say by mermaids. Then they saw the Archangel Michael standing on a ledge on the western side of the rock, and he warned them away from danger. The place became sacred to Michael, the patron of seafarers. This made the Mount a sister to Monte Sant'Angelo in Italy, whose famous apparition is dated to almost the same years. It is likely that the Cornish story borrowed the date from the Italian one.

Older legends. The Cornish name, Karrek Loos y'n Koos, means "the grey rock in the wood". It recalls a time before the sea rose, when the Mount stood in forest; tree stumps from a drowned forest do appear in the bay at very low tides. The best-loved folk tale is about the giant Cormoran, who lived on the Mount and stole cattle until Jack, a Marazion boy, dug a pit on the causeway and lured him into it with a blast on his horn. A heart-shaped stone on the castle path is still called the giant's heart.

The history. By the time of the Norman Conquest the Mount belonged to the Benedictine abbey of Mont-Saint-Michel. Building of the church and priory on the summit began in 1135. The priory was small, a few monks under a prior. It was an "alien priory" (one that answered to a mother house abroad), so during the wars with France the English crown repeatedly took it over. In the 15th century it passed to the Bridgettine nuns of Syon Abbey. In 1193 Henry de la Pomeray seized the Mount by having his men disguise themselves as pilgrims. After the Reformation it became a fortress. In 1659 Colonel John St Aubyn bought it, and the St Aubyn family still live there. They gave most of it to the National Trust in 1954 while keeping a long lease and the right to live in the castle.

The "St Michael's line". The Mount is the first link in the chain of sanctuaries this guide follows. The idea that the seven shrines from Skellig to Carmel lie on one straight "sword-stroke" of the Archangel is a modern legend. On a real map they do not form a perfect line. In England, the "St Michael ley line" running from the Mount through Glastonbury Tor to the Norfolk coast was made popular by 1960s–70s writers on earth mysteries. Neither idea comes from medieval sources. What is historical is the network of Michael shrines on high places and islands, linked by monks, relics and pilgrims, as the Mount's ties to Normandy show.

Architecture and special features

  • The priory church. It stands at the very top. Much of what you see is a 14th- and 15th-century rebuild of the 12th-century church, after an earthquake damaged it in the 13th century. It has two fine rose windows. Mass and services are still held here, and it is a working Anglican church.
  • St Michael's Chair. This is a stone lantern on the church tower, probably once a beacon for ships. A Cornish saying holds that whichever newly married partner sits in it first will rule the household. It is not open to visitors.
  • The Chevy Chase Room. This was the monks' refectory (dining hall). Its 17th-century plaster frieze shows hunting scenes and is named after the old ballad of the Chevy Chase hunt.
  • The terraced gardens. They cling to the south face, warmed by the granite and the Gulf Stream, with succulents, aloes and plants from South Africa and the Canaries that would not survive elsewhere in Britain.
  • The harbour and causeway. The harbour was rebuilt in the 1720s, and the granite causeway leads to Marazion at low tide. Mount's Bay was a busy trading port in the Middle Ages, and the small village of islanders' houses below the castle survives.

Relics and sacred objects

Medieval pilgrims came to venerate relics in the priory and for the indulgences offered to visitors. The Mount's own history page records four miracles in 1262–63 that drew pilgrims from far away. No medieval relics survive on the Mount today. In the church you can see late medieval alabaster panels and the ledge of rock where the Archangel was said to have stood.

Notable pilgrims and visitors

  • Medieval pilgrims crossed the causeway barefoot, often on the way to or from Santiago de Compostela, since Mount's Bay was a port of departure for Spain.
  • Henry de la Pomeray (1193), whose "pilgrims" were soldiers.
  • John de Vere, Earl of Oxford, held the Mount for the Lancastrian cause in 1473–74 during the Wars of the Roses.
  • Lady Catherine Gordon, wife of the pretender Perkin Warbeck, took refuge on the Mount in 1497.
  • In 1588 the first beacon warning of the Spanish Armada was lit on the church tower, according to the Mount's history.

The surrounding area

The town. Marazion, facing the Mount, claims to be one of the oldest chartered towns in Britain (with a charter of 1257). It has galleries, cafés and a long sandy beach. A mile or so west is Penzance, a larger town with the station, the Jubilee Pool and the Penlee House Gallery. Along the coast are Newlyn, a working fishing harbour, and Mousehole, a picture-postcard village.

Local food. Try a Cornish pasty, which has protected status, and a cream tea (jam first, then clotted cream, in the Cornish way). Seafood from Newlyn includes crab, mackerel and pilchards, sold as "Cornish sardines". There is also saffron cake and Cornish yarg cheese, wrapped in nettles. On the Mount itself, the Sail Loft and Island Café serve lunches in season.

Getting there. Trains from London Paddington take about 5 hours to Penzance, or you can take the Night Riviera sleeper. From Penzance it is a local bus or a 50-minute coastal walk to Marazion. The causeway is only open around low tide. Use the official Causeway Calculator. At high tide, island boats take you across in season.

Best time to visit. The garden is open roughly April to September. Low spring tides give the longest causeway windows. Early morning, before the day-trippers, is magical. In winter the castle has shorter, limited opening.

Nearby sights. - Prussia Cove, once a smugglers' cove. - The open-air Minack Theatre at Porthcurno. - Land's End. - The Iron Age village at Chysauster. - Tate St Ives. - The St Michael's Way, a 12.5-mile walking route from Lelant to the Mount that is part of the European Camino network.
