St Paul Outside the WallsStation 2 · San Paolo fuori le Mura, the tomb of the Apostle of the Nations

Two miles outside the ancient city gate on the road to Ostia, the apostle Paul was buried after his beheading, by tradition at Tre Fontane around AD 67. Over his grave stands the second-largest church in Rome, a vast five-aisled basilica that looks ancient but was almost entirely rebuilt after a fire in 1823. For the walk, it is the second station, and for Paul's own story, see our Footsteps of St Paul guide.

Good to know

  • Entry to the basilica is free; the cloister and the archaeological area have a small fee.
  • Getting there: Metro B to Basilica S. Paolo, then a 5-minute walk. On the walk it's about 7 km from St Peter's.
  • Look up: the frieze of mosaic portraits of every pope runs around the nave and aisles.
  • The basilica belongs to the Holy See and is served by the Benedictine monks of the adjoining abbey; it is part of the UNESCO World Heritage site of Rome.

Deep Dive

History

History. A small Constantinian church marked the grave by the 320s. In 386 the emperors Valentinian II, Theodosius and Arcadius began a basilica worthy of the apostle, finished under Honorius, and for nearly 1,500 years it was the largest church in Rome until the new St Peter's. It survived Saracen raids in 846 (after which Pope John VIII walled the area as "Giovannipoli") and earthquakes. On the night of 15–16 July 1823 a fire started by workmen repairing the roof destroyed most of it. Leo XII appealed to the whole world; gifts came even from the Viceroy of Egypt (the alabaster columns) and the Tsar (the malachite altars). Pius IX consecrated the rebuilt basilica on 10 December 1854, two days after he defined the dogma of the Immaculate Conception.

The tomb of Paul

Under the papal altar lies a large marble sarcophagus, never opened in modern times, beneath a slab inscribed PAULO APOSTOLO MART, "To Paul, apostle, martyr," cut in the 4th century. Excavations in 2002–2006 made part of the sarcophagus visible to pilgrims through a window under the altar. In June 2009, closing the Pauline Year, Benedict XVI announced that a probe inserted through a small hole had found purple linen with gold sequins, blue fabric, grains of incense and tiny bone fragments that carbon dating placed in the 1st or 2nd century, which "seems to confirm the unanimous and uncontested tradition that these are the mortal remains of the Apostle Paul." The chain displayed in the confessio is venerated as the chain Paul wore in prison.

Altars, art and architecture

  • The Gothic ciborium over the altar by Arnolfo di Cambio (1285), one of the few treasures to survive the fire.
  • The paschal candlestick (late 12th century), more than 5 m of carved marble by Nicola d'Angelo and Pietro Vassalletto, with scenes of the Passion.
  • The apse mosaic commissioned by Honorius III (around 1220) from Venetian craftsmen: Christ enthroned with Peter, Paul, Andrew and Luke, and tiny Honorius at his feet.
  • The triumphal arch mosaic, from the time of Galla Placidia (5th century), heavily restored.
  • The papal medallions: a portrait for every pope from Peter to the present. The Roman joke that the world ends when the spaces run out is only a joke: new rows are added as needed.
  • The 11th-century bronze Holy Door cast in Constantinople, now inside, and the modern Holy Door on the façade.

Legend. At Tre Fontane ("Three Fountains"), about 2.5 km away, a tradition first written down in the Middle Ages says that Paul's severed head bounced three times and a spring rose at each place. The Trappist Abbey there keeps the pillar he is said to have been tied to; it's a peaceful detour off the walk.

Next on the walk

From San Paolo the pilgrims climbed east along the Via delle Sette Chiese, about 3 km through quiet streets, to San Sebastiano on the Appian Way.
