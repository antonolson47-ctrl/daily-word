St Peter's BasilicaStation 1 · the tomb of the Apostle Peter on the Vatican Hill

The largest church in Christendom stands over a humble grave on the side of the Vatican Hill, where, by ancient tradition, the apostle Peter was buried after his martyrdom under Nero around AD 64–67. Philip Neri's pilgrims started here, at the tomb of the fisherman Jesus called the "rock."

Good to know

  • Entry to the basilica is free; expect airport-style security in the colonnade, with the shortest lines early in the morning. The dome climb and the Vatican Museums charge separately.
  • The Scavi (excavations under the basilica, to Peter's tomb) can only be visited on small guided tours booked in advance through the Excavations Office; under-15s are not admitted.
  • Papal events: the Wednesday general audience and Sunday Angelus can close the square or the basilica. Check the calendar.
  • Dress: shoulders and knees covered; this is strictly enforced.
  • Getting there: Metro A to Ottaviano, then a 10-minute walk, or bus 40/64 to the Via della Conciliazione.

Deep Dive

History: from a grave to a basilica

History. Around AD 160 a small monument, the trophy of Gaius mentioned by a Roman priest about the year 200, marked a grave in a pagan necropolis on the Vatican Hill. In the early 4th century Constantine levelled part of the hill, at enormous cost, to build a five-aisled basilica with that monument at its focus. Old St Peter's stood for over a thousand years; Charlemagne was crowned there on Christmas Day 800.

By the 15th century the old basilica was failing. Julius II laid the foundation stone of a new church in 1506 to designs by Bramante; Raphael, Peruzzi and Sangallo followed, and Michelangelo, from 1547, gave it the great dome, completed by Giacomo della Porta in 1590. Carlo Maderno lengthened the nave and built the façade (finished 1612), and Urban VIII consecrated the church on 18 November 1626. Bernini then filled it: the baldacchino, the Cathedra, and in 1656–67 the colonnade, the "arms of the Church" embracing the square.

The tomb of Peter

Excavations under the basilica in 1939–1949 found the necropolis and the trophy of Gaius. In 1950 Pius XII announced that the apostle's tomb had been found; in 1968 Paul VI said that bones found in a niche of a nearby wall, the so-called "graffiti wall," could be identified as Peter's "in a way we can consider convincing." The identification is a matter of serious scholarly argument, and the Holy See has never declared it infallible; that this is the grave venerated as Peter's since the 2nd century is solid archaeology. In 2013 Pope Francis displayed some of those bone fragments publicly for the first time.

Altars and architecture

  • The papal altar and baldacchino: Bernini's twisting bronze canopy, nearly 29 m high, rises over the altar where only the Pope (or a cardinal he delegates) celebrates. Tradition says bronze was stripped from the Pantheon portico for it; the documents show bronze from the Pantheon was used for the basilica works, hence the Roman quip "what the barbarians did not do, the Barberini did."
  • The Confessio: the sunken chapel in front of the altar, lit by 99 oil lamps, leading down to the level of the tomb.
  • The Cathedra Petri (1657–66): Bernini's gilded bronze throne held up by four Doctors of the Church, enclosing an old wooden chair long venerated as Peter's (in fact largely a 9th-century throne given by Charles the Bald), under the alabaster window of the Holy Spirit as a dove.
  • The dome: 136.6 m to the top of the cross; the drum's mosaic inscription reads TV ES PETRVS ET SVPER HANC PETRAM AEDIFICABO ECCLESIAM MEAM, "You are Peter, and on this rock I will build my church" (Matthew 16:18, in Latin).
  • The Holy Door: the farthest right of the five doors, opened only in Jubilee years.

Relics and devotions

  • The bronze statue of St Peter (usually attributed to Arnolfo di Cambio, late 13th century), its right foot worn smooth by pilgrims' kisses; it is robed in papal vestments on 29 June.
  • The four great relics displayed from the balconies of the dome's piers in Holy Week: a fragment of the True Cross, the lance of St Longinus, the Veil of Veronica, and (until it was returned to Patras in 1964) the head of St Andrew. Statues of the four saints stand below.
  • The tombs of popes, including St John Paul II (in the chapel of St Sebastian) and St John XXIII (under the altar of St Jerome), and the Vatican Grottoes beneath the floor.

Legend. The obelisk in the square once stood in Nero's circus, where Christians were martyred; medieval Romans believed the gilded ball on its top held Julius Caesar's ashes. When it was opened in 1586, it held only dust. Another tradition, recorded in the apocryphal Acts of Peter, says Peter asked to be crucified upside down, being unworthy to die as his Lord had.

Notable art

  • Michelangelo's Pietà (1498–99), carved when he was about 24, the only work he ever signed (on the sash across Mary's breast). After a man attacked it with a hammer in 1972 it was restored and placed behind glass.
  • Bernini's monument to Alexander VII, with a skeleton lifting a marble shroud over an hourglass.
  • Mosaics, not paintings: almost every "painting" over the altars is a mosaic copy, made because damp and candle smoke damaged canvases.

Next on the walk

From St Peter's, Philip's pilgrims crossed the city to St Paul Outside the Walls, about 7 km south along the Tiber.
