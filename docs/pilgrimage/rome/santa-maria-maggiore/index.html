The walk ends with Mary. Santa Maria Maggiore, on the top of the Esquiline Hill, is the largest and oldest of Rome's churches dedicated to the Mother of God, and the only one of the four papal basilicas whose 5th-century structure still stands. It keeps the beloved icon of the Salus Populi Romani, "Protectress of the Roman People," and since April 2025 it is also the burial place of Pope Francis.

Good to know

  • Entry is free. The museum, the loggia mosaics and the bell tower have paid guided visits.
  • Getting there: a 5-minute walk from Termini (Metro A and B); Metro B Cavour is also close.
  • Pope Francis's tomb is in the left aisle near the Pauline Chapel, under a plain slab inscribed simply Franciscus. Expect a queue.
  • 5 August, the feast of Our Lady of the Snows: white petals are released from the ceiling of the Pauline Chapel during Mass.

Deep Dive

History

History. Pope Sixtus III (432–440) built the basilica just after the Council of Ephesus (431) proclaimed Mary Theotokos, "Mother of God," and its mosaics are the great monument of that council. It was rebuilt and extended, never replaced: Nicholas IV added the transept and apse in the 1290s, the Sistine Chapel (Sixtus V, 1585) and the Pauline or Borghese Chapel (Paul V, 1611) were added on either side, and Ferdinando Fuga gave it its 18th-century façade (1741–43) for Benedict XIV. Its Romanesque bell tower (1377) is the tallest in Rome at about 75 m.

Legend: Our Lady of the Snows. In a story written down centuries later, a childless Roman patrician named John and his wife asked Mary how to give their fortune to her. On the night of 4–5 August 352, she appeared to them and to Pope Liberius in dreams, and in the morning snow lay on the Esquiline in the shape of the church to be built. The Pope traced its plan in the snow. Hence the old name Our Lady of the Snows and the falling white petals of 5 August. Historians date the present church to Sixtus III, and find no 4th-century church on this exact site.

Altars and relics

  • The Salus Populi Romani, in the Pauline Chapel: an icon of Mary holding the Christ Child, venerated by tradition as painted by St Luke. Art historians date it variously between the 6th and the 13th century, with the most recent restoration (completed in 2018) supporting an early medieval date. Popes have carried it through Rome in times of plague and war; Pope Francis prayed before it before and after more than a hundred journeys, and it was brought out in 2020 for his blessing Urbi et Orbi during the pandemic.
  • The Holy Crib (Sacra Culla): in the confessio under the high altar, a crystal and silver reliquary (1802) with pieces of wood venerated as from the manger of Bethlehem. In 2019 Pope Francis returned a small fragment to Bethlehem.
  • The Sistine Chapel of Sixtus V, under which is the old Chapel of the Nativity with the oldest surviving Christmas crib figures, carved by Arnolfo di Cambio around 1290.
  • The tomb of Gian Lorenzo Bernini, marked by a modest step near the high altar.

Art and architecture

  • The 5th-century mosaics: 36 panels of the Old Testament above the nave columns, and on the triumphal arch, the childhood of Christ, with Mary dressed as a Roman empress.
  • The apse mosaic by Jacopo Torriti (1295), the Coronation of the Virgin, and below it his scenes of Mary's life including the Nativity.
  • The coffered ceiling by Giuliano da Sangallo (around 1500), gilded, by tradition, with the first gold brought from the Americas, given by Ferdinand and Isabella of Spain to Pope Alexander VI.
  • The Pauline Chapel's dome, painted by Cigoli (1612), whose Virgin stands on a moon with craters, drawn as his friend Galileo had just seen it through his telescope.

The end of the walk

Philip Neri's pilgrims ended their day here, before Mary, with thanksgiving. If you started at dawn at the Chiesa Nuova, it is a short bus ride or a 2.5 km walk back. Many walkers end with Vespers.
