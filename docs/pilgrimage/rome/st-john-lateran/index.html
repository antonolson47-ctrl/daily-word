The Pope's own church is not St Peter's but St John Lateran, the cathedral of the Diocese of Rome. The inscription on its façade calls it omnium urbis et orbis ecclesiarum mater et caput: "mother and head of all the churches of the city and the world." Across the square is one of Rome's most moving devotions, the Scala Santa, the "Holy Stairs," which pilgrims climb on their knees.

Good to know

  • Entry to the basilica is free; the cloister has a small fee.
  • The Scala Santa is climbed only on the knees, praying; it's free, and side staircases let you walk up. The Sancta Sanctorum chapel at the top is visible through grilles, with a paid entry to go inside.
  • Getting there: Metro A to San Giovanni. On the walk it is about 4 km from San Sebastiano and 1 km from Santa Croce.
  • Feast: the Dedication of the Lateran Basilica, 9 November, is a feast for the whole Latin Church.

Deep Dive

History

History. The land belonged to the Laterani family and then to the imperial house. Around 312, after his victory at the Milvian Bridge, Constantine gave the palace to the bishop of Rome and built beside it the first great Christian basilica in the city, dedicated, by tradition, by Pope Sylvester I on 9 November 324, first to Christ the Saviour and later also to St John the Baptist and St John the Evangelist. For about a thousand years the Lateran Palace was the residence of the popes. Five ecumenical councils met here (1123–1517), including Lateran IV in 1215. In 1929 the Lateran Treaty, which created Vatican City, was signed in the palace.

The basilica was rebuilt after earthquake and fires; its present nave is Francesco Borromini's (1646–50), commissioned by Innocent X for the 1650 Jubilee, which encased the ancient columns in great piers. Alessandro Galilei's monumental façade, with Christ and the saints along its top, followed in 1735.

Altars and relics

  • The papal altar under a Gothic ciborium (1367) in whose upper level are reliquaries venerated as containing the heads of St Peter and St Paul.
  • The wooden altar table enclosed in the papal altar, which tradition says the early popes, even Peter, used to celebrate Mass in the time of persecution.
  • The Twelve Apostles: huge Baroque statues in Borromini's niches (1703–1718), by Rusconi, Le Gros, Monnot and others.
  • The apse mosaic of Christ and the jewelled cross, a 13th-century work by Jacopo Torriti and Jacopo da Camerino, reset in the 19th century, with Pope Nicholas IV kneeling.
  • Giotto's fresco fragment of Boniface VIII proclaiming the first Jubilee in 1300 (attribution debated), on a pier in the right aisle.

The Scala Santa and Sancta Sanctorum

Tradition. The 28 marble steps are venerated as the stairs of Pontius Pilate's praetorium in Jerusalem, which Jesus climbed on the day of his Passion, brought to Rome, by tradition, by St Helena in the 4th century. Historians cannot trace the steps back further than the medieval Lateran palace; the devotion is documented for centuries.

When Sixtus V demolished most of the old palace, Domenico Fontana moved the stairs to their present building in 1589, ending at the popes' old private chapel, the Sancta Sanctorum, which survived. It holds the Acheropita, an icon of Christ "not made by human hands," carried in procession through Rome in the Middle Ages. The steps have been covered with wood since the 18th century to protect them; in 2019 the wood was removed for restoration, and for a few weeks pilgrims could climb the bare marble for the first time in nearly 300 years. Martin Luther climbed them during his visit to Rome in 1510–11 as a young monk.

The baptistery and cloister

The octagonal Lateran Baptistery, founded under Constantine and rebuilt by Sixtus III (432–440), became the model for baptisteries across Italy; its side chapels keep 5th- and 7th-century mosaics. The cloister (about 1215–1232), by the Vassalletto family, is a jewel of Cosmatesque work, with twisted columns inlaid with mosaic.
