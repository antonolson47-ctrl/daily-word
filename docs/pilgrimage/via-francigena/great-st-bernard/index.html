"I lift up my eyes to the hills. From where does my help come? My help comes from the LORD, who made heaven and earth" (Psalm 121:1–2, ESV). The crossing of the Alps is the heart of the Via Francigena. After the vineyards of Lake Geneva and the ancient abbey of Saint-Maurice, the route climbs the Great St Bernard Pass (2,469 m), where canons regular have welcomed travellers for nearly a thousand years.

Good to know

  • Route: Jougne – Orbe – Lausanne – Vevey – Montreux – Aigle – Saint-Maurice – Martigny – Orsières – Bourg-Saint-Pierre – Great St Bernard Hospice – Saint-Rhémy – Aosta.
  • Snow: walking over the pass is only advisable from about mid-June to September. Outside that season the path is under snow and avalanche-prone; take the bus or train via the road tunnel, or go with a mountain guide.
  • The Hospice offers lodging and community meals all year (in summer also the Auberge de l'Hospice); book ahead. Visitors can join the canons' prayers; the church, the highest in Europe according to the Hospice, is free.
  • The dogs: the famous St Bernard dogs spend the summer at the col and can be visited (and even walked) at the museum and kennels.
  • Feast of St Bernard: 15 June, with a pilgrimage up from Bourg-Saint-Bernard and Mass at the Hospice.

Deep Dive

Saint-Maurice and the Theban Legion

History. At Agaunum, today's Saint-Maurice, the Burgundian king Sigismund founded an abbey in 515 at the tomb of St Maurice and his companions. Its monks kept up the laus perennis, unceasing praise in shifts day and night. The Abbey of Saint-Maurice has lived without interruption ever since, more than 1,500 years, and its treasury is one of the richest in Europe.

Legend. According to St Eucherius of Lyon (5th century), Maurice commanded the Theban Legion, Christian soldiers from Egypt, who were massacred near Agaunum around 286 for refusing to sacrifice to the gods or kill fellow Christians. Historians doubt that a whole legion was killed, but the cult of the martyrs at Agaunum is very old.

Bernard and the hospice

History. The pass was the Romans' Summus Poeninus. Around 1050, Bernard, archdeacon of Aosta, founded a hospice on the col to shelter and protect travellers, who were often robbed or lost in snow. The canons of the Great St Bernard, an Augustinian congregation, have kept it ever since. Bernard was canonized in 1681, and in 1923 Pope Pius XI declared him patron of the Alps and of mountaineers. In 800 Charlemagne, and in 1800 Napoleon, crossed here with armies.

Legend. The dogs became famous as rescuers in the 18th and 19th centuries. The most famous, Barry (died 1814), is said to have saved more than 40 lives; the barrel of brandy on the collar is a painter's invention. Legends also say Bernard drove a demon off the pass and broke a pagan idol of Jupiter there.

Down to Aosta

From the col the road descends steeply into Italy and the Aosta Valley. Aosta, Roman Augusta Praetoria, keeps its Arch of Augustus, Roman gates and the cathedral where Bernard served; St Anselm, later Archbishop of Canterbury, was born here in 1033, a link between the two ends of the road.
