The Via Francigena begins where English Christianity began. In 597 St Augustine, sent by Pope Gregory the Great, arrived in Kent and founded his cathedral in Canterbury, and Canterbury's archbishops have gone to Rome ever since, Sigeric among them. Pilgrims set out from the Kilometre Zero stone outside the cathedral, after a blessing and the first stamp in the credential.

Good to know

  • Credential and first stamp: Canterbury Cathedral sells the official Via Francigena pilgrim record and credential and stamps it. The cathedral welcomes pilgrims and can arrange a pilgrim blessing; ask at the welcome desk or contact the cathedral in advance.
  • Entry: Canterbury Cathedral charges admission for visitors; worshippers attending services enter free. Choral Evensong is sung most days. Check times on the cathedral's website.
  • Stages: Canterbury to Dover is about 33 km, usually walked in 1–2 days through Kentish villages (Patrixbourne, Shepherdswell).
  • The Channel: pilgrims cross from Dover to Calais by ferry. Not every crossing carries foot passengers, so check with the operator before you go. From Calais the route follows the coast to Wissant and then turns inland.
  • Northern France is quiet: pilgrim hostels are few, so plan beds ahead in Artois and Picardy.

Deep Dive

St Augustine and the mother church

History. King Æthelberht of Kent, whose Frankish queen Bertha was already Christian, received Augustine and his monks and was baptized. Augustine became the first Archbishop of Canterbury. The present cathedral was begun by Archbishop Lanfranc after 1070; the choir was rebuilt after a fire of 1174 by William of Sens, one of the first Gothic buildings in England. The cathedral, with St Augustine's Abbey and St Martin's Church, is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

The martyrdom of St Thomas

History. On 29 December 1170 four knights of King Henry II killed Archbishop Thomas Becket in the north-west transept of his cathedral, after years of conflict over the rights of the Church. Thomas was canonized by Pope Alexander III in 1173, and Henry did public penance at the tomb in 1174. Canterbury became one of the great shrines of Europe, the goal of Chaucer's pilgrims. In 1538 Henry VIII had the shrine destroyed and Thomas's bones disappeared.

Today a stone and a sculpture of swords mark the Martyrdom, the place where Thomas fell, and a candle burns in the Trinity Chapel where his shrine stood.

Legends. Collections of miracles at Becket's tomb were written within a few years of his death, and "Canterbury water", water mixed with a trace of the martyr's blood, was carried home in small ampullae by pilgrims all over Europe. Tradition says Thomas's last words were that he was ready to die for the name of Jesus and the protection of the Church.

From the white cliffs to Artois

After the North Downs, the road drops to Dover and the white cliffs, the last sight of England for medieval pilgrims too. Sigeric's list ends at Sumeran, usually identified with Sombre near Wissant, which was then the main crossing port. In France the way crosses the old battlefields of the First World War on its way to Arras, where war cemeteries line the route.
