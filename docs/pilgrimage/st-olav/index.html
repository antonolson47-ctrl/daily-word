Pilgrimage
St Olav's WayPilegrimsleden: the St Olav Ways to Nidaros Cathedral in Trondheim

Photo: Catedral de Nidaros, Trondheim, Noruega, 2019-09-06, DD 131 by Diego Delso, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons

For five hundred years, until the Reformation, Nidaros (today's Trondheim) was the greatest pilgrim goal of northern Europe. Pilgrims came over the mountains, along the fjords and from Sweden to the tomb of St Olav, the Viking king who brought Norway to Christ and died in battle at Stiklestad in 1030. Since 1994 the old roads have been marked again, and today about 5,000 km of trails in Norway, Sweden, Denmark and Finland form Pilegrimsleden, the St Olav Ways, a Council of Europe Cultural Route.

Good to know

  • Nine routes, one goal. The official Norwegian network has nine ways: Gudbrandsdalsleden (Oslo–Trondheim, 643 km, about 32 days), St. Olavsleden (Selånger in Sweden–Trondheim, 564 km, about 30 days), Østerdalsleden (Trysil/Rena–Trondheim, 375 km, about 21 days), the coastal Kystpilegrimsleia (Egersund–Trondheim, by boat, bus and foot), Borgleden, Tunsbergleden, Valldalsleden, Romboleden and Nordleden. In 2026 the National Pilgrim Centre advises against walking Nordleden until its marking is improved.
  • Season: the main season is 1 June to 1 September. Snow can block the mountain stretches into mid-June; don't cross Dovrefjell before about 10 June.
  • The Olav Letter (Olavsbrevet), the Norwegian Compostela, is given at the pilgrim reception by the cathedral if your pilgrim passport is stamped for at least the last 100 km on foot, the last 200 km by bike, or at least six key sites on the coastal route.
  • Pilgrims average about 20 km a day. Accommodation is in pilgrim hostels, farms, cabins and hotels, often small: book a few days ahead.
  • Olsok, 29 July, St Olav's day, is the great feast in Trondheim and at Stiklestad, with the St Olav Festival (Olavsfestdagene) around it.

Deep Dive

Olav Haraldsson: from Viking to saint

History. Olav was born around 995, a descendant of King Harald Fairhair, and went raiding as a teenager in the Baltic, England, France and Spain. According to the sagas he was baptized in Rouen in Normandy around 1013–14. In 1015 he returned to Norway, won the kingdom at the battle of Nesjar (1016), and set about making it Christian, by preaching and church-building, and also by force. At the assembly at Moster around 1024, with his English bishop Grimkell, he set down Norway's first Christian law. His harsh rule made enemies; with the support of King Cnut of Denmark and England they drove him out in 1028, and he fled to Yaroslav the Wise in Kievan Rus.

In 1030 he came back through Sweden and over the mountains of Jämtland with a small army, the route followed today by St. Olavsleden. On 29 July 1030, at Stiklestad near the Trondheimsfjord, he met a much larger army of farmers and chieftains and was killed. See Stiklestad.

From grave to shrine

History. Olav's body was secretly buried in a sandbank of the river Nid. A year later, on 3 August 1031, it was dug up and, the sources say, found incorrupt, with hair and nails grown. Bishop Grimkell declared him a saint, an early "local" canonization recognized by the whole Church. A church was built over the grave, and from about 1070 the great stone cathedral rose on the spot. In 1152–53 Nidaros became an archdiocese over Norway, Iceland, Greenland, the Faroes, Orkney, Shetland and the Isle of Man. Olav became Rex Perpetuus Norvegiae, Norway's eternal king.

Pilgrims came from all over the North. In 1537, with the Lutheran Reformation, the Danish king's men took Olav's silver shrine to Copenhagen to be melted down, and his remains were buried somewhere in the cathedral, where they have never been found. Pilgrimage was forbidden. The modern revival began in the 1990s: the restored Gudbrandsdalsleden was opened in 1997, and the St Olav Ways became a European Cultural Route in 2010.

Legends. Dozens of Olav's springs (Olavskilder) along the routes are said to have burst out where he stopped, and their water to heal. The sagas say the sun darkened over Stiklestad as he fell; there really was a total solar eclipse visible in Norway in 1030, but on 31 August, a month after the battle. Folk tales make Olav a troll-killer who turned giants to stone, an echo of the old pagan Thor stories transferred to the saint.

Who walks today

Thousands of pilgrims a year now reach Trondheim on foot, by bike or by boat, Norwegians and Germans in the largest numbers, and many walk only a stage or a week at a time. The Church of Norway (Lutheran) cares for the cathedral and the pilgrim centres, and Catholic pilgrims are welcomed both at Nidaros and at Trondheim's Catholic St Olav's Cathedral. St John Paul II prayed at Nidaros during his visit to Scandinavia in 1989.
