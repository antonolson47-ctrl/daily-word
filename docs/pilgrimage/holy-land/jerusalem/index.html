"He is not here, for he has risen, as he said" (Matthew 28:6, ESV). The Church of the Holy Sepulchre, the Christian heart of Jerusalem, covers both Golgotha, where Jesus was crucified, and the tomb where he was laid and rose. Around the Old City are the other places of Holy Week: the Upper Room on Mount Zion, the Garden of Gethsemane and the Mount of Olives.

Good to know

  • Holy Sepulchre hours (CIC, 15 September 2026): summer 5:00–20:45, October to 20:00, winter 4:00–19:00, March to 19:30. The Franciscans' daily procession is at 17:00 in summer, 16:00 in winter. Entry is free. An overnight vigil can be arranged with the sacristy.
  • Queues for the tomb (the Edicule) are longest mid-morning; come early or late.
  • Gethsemane: the Basilica of the Agony (Church of All Nations) is open 7:30–18:00 in April–September and 7:30–17:00 in October–March, closed at midday.
  • The Upper Room (Cenacle) on Mount Zion is administered by the Israeli Ministry of Tourism, open 8:00–18:00; Mass there is generally not allowed, so groups celebrate in the nearby Franciscan Ad Coenaculum chapel.
  • Mount of Olives: walk down from the Pater Noster church and Dominus Flevit to Gethsemane, the route of Palm Sunday.

Deep Dive

Is this the place?

History. The Gospels say Jesus was crucified outside the city wall and buried nearby (John 19:41–42; Hebrews 13:12). The site was an old quarry with rock-cut tombs, outside the walls in Jesus' day and enclosed by Herod Agrippa's wall in the 40s. Hadrian built a pagan temple over it after 135; when Constantine's builders demolished it around 326, they found a tomb they identified as Christ's. The early Christian community's memory, a quarry with 1st-century tombs and the location outside the wall all fit; most archaeologists consider it the most likely site. The Garden Tomb, near Damascus Gate, venerated by many Protestants since the 19th century, is an Iron Age tomb centuries older than Jesus.

The present church is mostly Crusader (consecrated 1149). The Edicule over the tomb was rebuilt in 1810 and restored in 2016–17, when the original limestone burial bench was briefly exposed for the first time in centuries. Restoration of the church floor has continued since 2022.

Legends and traditions. An old tradition places the burial of Adam beneath Golgotha, so that the blood of Christ ran down onto the first man: the chapel under Calvary is the Chapel of Adam, and the skull at the foot of crucifixes recalls it. Every Holy Saturday the Orthodox celebrate the Holy Fire, which the faithful believe kindles miraculously in the tomb.

Gethsemane

"My Father, if it be possible, let this cup pass from me" (Matthew 26:39, ESV). At the foot of the Mount of Olives, the Basilica of the Agony (1924, built with gifts from many countries, hence Church of All Nations) encloses the rock where Jesus is believed to have prayed. In the garden beside it stand olive trees that dating studies have shown to be at least 900 years old.

The Upper Room

History. The Cenacle that pilgrims see is a Gothic hall built by the Crusaders and restored by the Franciscans in the 14th century, on a site that Christians honoured as the place of the Last Supper and of Pentecost from the earliest centuries ("the church of the apostles" on Zion). The Franciscans were expelled in 1552, and the building passed into Muslim and later Israeli state hands. St John Paul II (2000), Benedict XVI (2009) and Pope Francis (2014) celebrated or prayed there.

Stop by stop

Church of the Holy Sepulchre

One church covers both Golgotha and the tomb. Constantine's builders found the site in about 326 under a Roman temple. The tomb shrine, the Aedicule, was restored in 2016–17, when the original limestone burial bed was briefly uncovered.

History. Archaeology confirms the site was a quarry and burial ground outside the walls in Jesus' time; Christians identified it by the 4th century.

Mount of Olives and Dominus Flevit

The tear-shaped chapel of Dominus Flevit (1955, Barluzzi) marks where Jesus wept over the city (Luke 19:41). Its west window frames the view of the Temple Mount and the Dome of the Rock.

St Anne's and the Pools of Bethesda

The Crusader church of St Anne, with perfect acoustics, is traditionally the home of Mary's parents. Beside it, excavations show the Pools of Bethesda with five porticoes, where Jesus healed the paralysed man (John 5).

History. The five porticoes John describes were long doubted until excavations in the 19th and 20th centuries found the double pool.

The Western Wall and the Davidson Center

The retaining wall of Herod's Temple platform. Just south, the Davidson Center excavations show the 1st-century street and steps up to the Temple that Jesus and the apostles walked (Acts 3).
