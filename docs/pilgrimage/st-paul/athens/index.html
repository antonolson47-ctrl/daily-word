Athens and the Areopagus"To the unknown god": Paul's speech on Mars Hill

Waiting alone in Athens for Silas and Timothy, Paul "was provoked within him as he saw that the city was full of idols" (Acts 17:16, ESV). He argued in the synagogue and daily in the Agora with Epicurean and Stoic philosophers, who brought him to the Areopagus, the ancient council named after the rocky "Hill of Ares" below the Acropolis. There he gave the most famous speech of his life.

Good to know

  • The Areopagus rock is free and open at all hours, a short walk below the Acropolis entrance; its polished marble steps are slippery, so take the modern metal stairs.
  • The bronze plaque with the full Greek text of Paul's speech (Acts 17:22–31) is fixed to the rock at the foot of the steps.
  • The Ancient Agora (paid entry) with the reconstructed Stoa of Attalos museum is just below; the Acropolis (paid, timed tickets in high season) is above.
  • Metro: Monastiraki or Thissio.

Deep Dive

The speech

"Men of Athens, I perceive that in every way you are very religious. For as I passed along and observed the objects of your worship, I found also an altar with this inscription: 'To the unknown god'" (Acts 17:22–23, ESV). Paul told them the God who made the world "does not live in temples made by man," quoted their own poets ("In him we live and move and have our being"; "For we are indeed his offspring," from Aratus, a poet of Paul's own Cilicia), and called them to repentance in view of the man God raised from the dead. At the word "resurrection" some mocked, but a few believed, including Dionysius the Areopagite and a woman named Damaris.

History. Ancient writers confirm that Athens had altars to "unknown gods": the 2nd-century traveller Pausanias saw them on the road from the harbour, and Diogenes Laertius tells how Epimenides of Crete had anonymous altars set up to end a plague. No altar with Paul's exact singular wording has been found. The Areopagus council still met in Paul's day, though whether he spoke on the hill itself or before the council in the Royal Stoa of the Agora is debated.

What to see

  • The Areopagus rock, with a view over the Agora where Paul debated.
  • The Ancient Agora: the market and civic centre, the temple of Hephaestus, and the Stoa of Attalos (rebuilt 1953–56) with finds from the excavations.
  • The Church of St Dionysius the Areopagite in Kolonaki, the Catholic cathedral of Athens, named after Paul's convert, who became, by tradition, the first bishop of Athens and is the city's patron.
  • The Acropolis, the "objects of worship" that so provoked Paul, still crowned by the Parthenon.

Legend. Writings that circulated from about AD 500 under the name of "Dionysius the Areopagite" became enormously influential in medieval theology; they are now known to be by a 5th–6th-century Syrian author. Medieval France also merged Dionysius with St Denis, the martyr bishop of Paris, a merger historians reject.
