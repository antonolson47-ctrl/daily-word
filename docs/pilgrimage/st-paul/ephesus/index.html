EphesusThree years in the great city of Asia, and the riot in the theatre

UNESCO2015

Ephesus was the capital of the Roman province of Asia, a port of perhaps 200,000 people, and home to one of the Seven Wonders of the World, the Temple of Artemis. Paul spent longer here than anywhere else on his journeys, "for three years" (Acts 20:31), and from here he wrote 1 Corinthians. Its marble streets are now the best-preserved Roman city in the eastern Mediterranean.

Good to know

  • Getting there: Selçuk is 75 km from İzmir airport by train or road; the cruise port of Kuşadası is 20 km away.
  • Entry: the site and the Terrace Houses have separate tickets (paid, sold at the gate and online through Türkiye's museum card system). Start at the upper (Magnesia) gate and walk downhill to finish at the theatre.
  • Go early: by mid-morning in summer the site is crowded and very hot; there's little shade.
  • Also in Selçuk: the Basilica of St John, the column of the Artemision, the Ephesus Museum, and the House of the Virgin Mary (Meryem Ana Evi) on the mountain above.

Deep Dive

What Acts tells

Paul first stopped here briefly with Priscilla and Aquila (Acts 18:19–21). Back on his third journey, he taught in the synagogue for three months, then daily for two years in the hall of Tyrannus, "so that all the residents of Asia heard the word of the Lord" (Acts 19:10). Luke tells of handkerchiefs touched to Paul curing the sick, the failed exorcism by the seven sons of Sceva, and converts burning magic books worth 50,000 pieces of silver.

Then came the riot. The silversmith Demetrius, who made silver shrines of Artemis, warned that Paul's preaching threatened their trade. The crowd rushed into the theatre, shouting for two hours, "Great is Artemis of the Ephesians!" (Acts 19:34, ESV), until the town clerk calmed them. Soon after, Paul left for Macedonia. On his way back to Jerusalem he called the Ephesian elders to Miletus for a farewell: they wept and embraced him, "sorrowing most of all because of the word he had spoken, that they would not see his face again" (Acts 20:38).

Archaeology and what to see

History. The Great Theatre, enlarged in the 1st century AD, seated around 24,000 people; it is the theatre of the riot. Inscriptions from Ephesus confirm many details of Acts 19: the title "temple warden" (neokoros) of Artemis, the town clerk (grammateus) as a leading official, and the Asiarchs, the city's leading men, who in Acts were friends of Paul.

  • Curetes Street and the Marble Street, lined with statues, fountains and shops, down to the Library of Celsus (built around AD 117, its façade re-erected in the 1970s).
  • The Terrace Houses: luxurious homes of the 1st century AD with wall paintings and mosaics, under a protective roof.
  • The Temple of Artemis (the Artemision): once 115 m long with 127 columns; today one re-erected column stands in a marshy field.
  • The Church of Mary (the Council Church), where the Council of Ephesus in 431 declared Mary Theotokos, Mother of God.
  • The Basilica of St John on Ayasuluk Hill in Selçuk: built by Justinian in the 6th century over the tomb venerated as that of John the Apostle, who, by early tradition, lived and died in Ephesus.

Tradition: Mary's house. On Bülbüldağı (Nightingale Mountain), a small stone house is venerated as the home where the Virgin Mary spent her last years in John's care. It was found in 1891 by Lazarist priests guided by the visions of the German mystic Anne Catherine Emmerich. Its foundations may go back to the 1st century, but the identification rests on tradition and private revelation; the Church has never ruled on it, though Paul VI (1967), John Paul II (1979) and Benedict XVI (2006) prayed there. There is also an old rival tradition that Mary died in Jerusalem.
