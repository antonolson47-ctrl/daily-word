ThessalonikiThessalonica: the city that heard the first letters, and the men who "turned the world upside down"

CountryGreece
Acts17:1–10
Letters1 & 2 Thessalonians
UNESCO1988 (monuments)

From Philippi, Paul and Silas followed the Via Egnatia 150 km west to Thessalonica, capital of the province of Macedonia and a busy port. For three Sabbaths Paul argued in the synagogue that the Messiah had to suffer and rise. Some believed, including "not a few of the leading women," but others gathered a mob and dragged Paul's host Jason before the city authorities, shouting, "These men who have turned the world upside down have come here also" (Acts 17:6, ESV). Paul slipped away by night to Berea.

Good to know

  • Getting there: Thessaloniki airport (SKG) has flights from across Europe. Most sites are walkable from the waterfront.
  • Most churches are free; the Rotunda and the Roman forum have small fees. Byzantine churches are active Orthodox churches: dress modestly and keep quiet during services.
  • Berea (Veria), 70 km west, has a striking Bema of St Paul, a mosaic-adorned monument at the traditional place where he preached (Acts 17:10–12).

Deep Dive

The earliest Christian writing

History. A few months after leaving, Paul wrote back from Corinth. 1 Thessalonians (c. AD 50–51) is very probably the oldest surviving Christian document, older than any of the Gospels. It shows a young church of former pagans who "turned to God from idols" (1 Thessalonians 1:9), suffering for their faith and worried about members who had died before Christ's return. Paul's advice is still beloved: "Rejoice always, pray without ceasing, give thanks in all circumstances; for this is the will of God in Christ Jesus for you" (1 Thessalonians 5:16–18, ESV).

An inscription once on the Vardar Gate (now in the British Museum) uses the title politarchs for the city's magistrates, the exact unusual word Luke uses in Acts 17:6–8, once doubted by critics and now confirmed by dozens of Macedonian inscriptions.

What to see

  • The Roman forum (Agora), of the 2nd–3rd century AD, with its odeon and a cryptoporticus, near the site of the market of Paul's day.
  • The Arch of Galerius (c. AD 300) and the Rotunda, built for Emperor Galerius, a persecutor of Christians, and turned into a church in the 4th or 5th century, with gold mosaics of martyrs.
  • Hagios Demetrios: the great basilica of the city's patron, the martyr Demetrius (d. c. 304), with the crypt of his martyrdom in a Roman bath. Rebuilt after the fire of 1917.
  • The Vlatadon Monastery in the upper city, built, by tradition, where Paul preached to the Thessalonians.
