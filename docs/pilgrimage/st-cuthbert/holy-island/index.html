Holy Island (Lindisfarne)The Pilgrims' Way over the sands, the priory and the Farne Islands

Photo: Lindisfarne Castle reflection - geograph.org.uk - 8163230 by Walter Baxter, CC BY-SA 2.0, via Wikimedia Commons

Fenwick–Holy Island10 km
Sands crossingabout 5 km, marked by poles
Founded635, by St Aidan
FeastSt Cuthbert, 20 March

The last stage ends on the sands. At low tide, pilgrims leave their boots on the shore and walk barefoot over the wet flats, following the line of tall posts of the Pilgrims' Way, toward the island where St Aidan founded his monastery in 635 and from which the Gospel spread across the North of England.

Good to know

  • Safe crossing times for the road causeway are published by Northumberland County Council for every day of the year. Never start when the tide is rising. The sands route is shorter in time: aim to finish before the mid-point of the safe period, in daylight; refuge boxes on posts are for emergencies. Every year cars and walkers are caught by the tide.
  • Lindisfarne Priory (English Heritage) has the ruins of the 12th-century Benedictine priory built in Cuthbert's memory, and a museum.
  • St Mary's Church, the parish church beside the priory, stands on the probable site of Aidan's first church; pilgrim groups use it and the island's retreat houses.
  • Lindisfarne Castle (National Trust), a Tudor fort turned Edwardian house by Lutyens.
  • St Cuthbert's Isle, a tiny tidal islet off the priory, where Cuthbert first sought solitude.
  • Inner Farne, Cuthbert's hermitage, is reached by boat from Seahouses in season (National Trust).

Deep Dive

Aidan's island

History. In 635 King Oswald of Northumbria, who had become a Christian in exile on Iona, asked for monks to preach to his people. Aidan came from Iona, chose this tidal island near the royal fortress of Bamburgh, and founded a monastery in the Irish tradition. With Oswald himself translating, he preached in the villages; Bede praises his gentleness and poverty. From Lindisfarne came Chad, Cedd and others who evangelized much of England. Cuthbert was prior here from about 665 and bishop from 685.

The Lindisfarne Gospels

History. Around 715–720, according to a later note in the book, Bishop Eadfrith of Lindisfarne wrote and painted the Lindisfarne Gospels "for God and St Cuthbert", one of the masterpieces of early medieval art. In the 10th century the priest Aldred added an Old English translation between the lines, the oldest surviving English version of the Gospels. The book is in the British Library in London.

Legends. Islanders still say the little fossils on the beach, called St Cuthbert's beads, were made by the saint on stormy nights on his rock. A legend says the Gospels were lost overboard during the monks' flight and washed up undamaged at Whithorn.
