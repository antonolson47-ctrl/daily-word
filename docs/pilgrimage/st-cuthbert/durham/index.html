Durham CathedralThe shrine of St Cuthbert and the tomb of St Bede

Since 995 St Cuthbert has rested in Durham, on a rocky peninsula in a loop of the River Wear. Around his tomb the Normans built from 1093 one of the greatest Romanesque churches in Europe. Many walkers of St Cuthbert's Way finish here, by train or on foot along the coastal routes, to pray at his shrine.

Good to know

  • Hours: the cathedral is open Monday–Saturday 8:00–18:00 (all areas 9:30–16:30) and Sunday 8:00–17:00 (all areas 12:30–15:00). Entry is free; a £5 donation is suggested. Parts can be closed for services and events; check the calendar.
  • The Shrine of St Cuthbert is in the Feretory behind the high altar, a simple slab inscribed Cuthbertus. The Venerable Bede lies in the Galilee Chapel at the west end.
  • Cuthbert Centre for Pilgrimage and Prayer: newly opened in the Chapel of the Holy Cross, it welcomes pilgrims and groups with hospitality and resources.
  • The Treasures: Cuthbert's oak coffin, his pectoral cross, embroidered stole and other relics found in the tomb are shown in the cathedral museum.
  • Evensong is sung most days; feasts of Cuthbert on 20 March and his translation on 4 September.

Deep Dive

The coming of Cuthbert

History. After more than a century at Chester-le-Street, the community of St Cuthbert moved again in 995 under threat of Danish raids. They built a "White Church" at Durham, which became the seat of the bishops. William of St Calais, the Norman bishop, began the present cathedral in 1093, replaced the community with Benedictine monks, and in 1104 Cuthbert's body was translated to the new shrine, found incorrupt again, according to the witnesses. The cathedral, with its ribbed vaults among the earliest in Europe, and the castle are a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

The Reformation and after

History. In 1539–40 Henry VIII's commissioners broke up the shrine, which was rich with gifts. The account written later by a former monk says they found the body still whole, and it was reburied behind the altar. The grave was opened in 1827, when only bones were found, together with the coffin, the gold-and-garnet pectoral cross, a comb and embroideries now in the museum. The St Cuthbert Gospel, found in his coffin in 1104 and the oldest intact European book, is in the British Library.

Legend. The site was chosen, it is said, when the monks' cart carrying the coffin stopped and could not be moved, and two milkmaids were overheard talking about a lost dun cow at "Dun Holm". The Dun Cow carved on the cathedral's north side remembers the story.
