Camino FrancésThe French Way: the classic, from the Pyrenees to Santiago

Photo: Saint-Jean-Pied-de-Port and the Nive by LPLT, CC BY-SA 3.0, via Wikimedia Commons

Distance~764–772 km
Stages33 · 5–6 weeks
StartSaint-Jean-Pied-de-Port

The most historic and most walked route, described in the 12th-century Codex Calixtinus. It crosses Navarra, La Rioja, Castilla y León and Galicia, through Pamplona, Burgos and León. Its biggest drawback is crowding, especially from Sarria onward.

Good to know

  • Day 1 is the hardest: about 1,200 m of climbing over the Pyrenees. In winter the Napoleon route is officially closed; take the Valcarlos road.
  • Short on time? Sarria to Santiago (~114 km, 5 days) earns the Compostela. 67% of Francés pilgrims start there, so it's busy.
  • Getting to the start: fly to Biarritz or Bordeaux → train to Bayonne → train/bus to Saint-Jean-Pied-de-Port; or Madrid → Pamplona → bus.

Deep Dive

History and origins

The cult of St James grew after a tomb near the Galician coast was identified, in the early 9th century, as that of the Apostle; the bishop Teodomiro of Iria Flavia is credited with the discovery and King Alfonso II of Asturias with the first church over it. That the tomb is truly St James's is a matter of faith and tradition; that pilgrims came in growing numbers is history. The earliest pilgrim known by name is Gotescalco (Godescalc), bishop of Le Puy, who travelled in 950–951.

The road we call the Francés took shape in the 11th century, when Sancho III of Navarre shifted it south onto the line through Puente la Reina, Estella, Logroño and Nájera, and Alfonso VI of León and Castile consolidated it with bridges, hospitals, toll exemptions and new towns for pilgrims and settlers, many of them "Franks" from beyond the Pyrenees. Around 1135–1145 the Codex Calixtinus (Liber Sancti Jacobi) was compiled; its fifth book, the so-called Pilgrim's Guide, describes this route stage by stage. In 1993 UNESCO inscribed the Camino Francés as a World Heritage Site, and the French approach routes followed in 1998.

Regions crossed

From the French Basque Country the road enters Navarra, where Basque (Euskara) is still heard in the Pyrenean valleys, then the wine country of La Rioja, the vast region of Castilla y León (the provinces of Burgos, Palencia and León) and finally Galicia (Lugo and A Coruña), where signs switch to Galician. The food changes with the land: Navarrese vegetables and pochas beans, Rioja wine and lamb chops grilled over vine cuttings, the roast suckling lamb and morcilla of Burgos, León's cecina (cured beef) and the botillo of El Bierzo, then Galicia's pulpo á feira, Arzúa-Ulloa cheese and tarta de Santiago.

Elevation and landscape

Day one is the big one: the Napoleon route climbs from under 200 m at Saint-Jean to about 1,430 m at the Col de Lepoeder before dropping to Roncesvalles. After Burgos comes the Meseta, a high plateau of wheat fields mostly between 750 and 900 m, open, windy and long. The route's highest point is the Cruz de Ferro in the Montes de León, at around 1,500 m, followed by a steep descent into the green basin of El Bierzo. The last great climb is up to O Cebreiro (about 1,300 m) and on past the Alto do Poio, after which Galicia is a rolling country of oak woods, granite villages and frequent rain. Guides disagree on total ascent because they measure differently, so we don't quote a single figure.

Notable past travellers

  • Gotescalco of Le Puy (950–951): the earliest pilgrim to Santiago known by name, documented in a manuscript copied for him at the monastery of Albelda.
  • The author of the Pilgrim's Guide (c. 1140): often attributed to the French cleric Aymeric Picaud, but the attribution is contested: the text names an "Aymericus", and Picaud appears only in later additions.
  • Hermann Künig von Vach (1495): a German Servite friar whose rhymed guidebook was printed for German pilgrims.
  • Domenico Laffi: a priest from Bologna who walked the route several times and published a vivid account, Viaggio in Ponente a S. Giacomo di Galitia e Finisterrae, first printed in Bologna in 1673.
  • Francis of Assisi (tradition): Franciscan tradition says he made the pilgrimage around 1213–1215, but there is no contemporary documentary proof.

Notable stops

  • Roncesvalles: Augustinian collegiate church and pilgrim hospital, tied by legend to Roland's last stand in the Song of Roland.
  • Pamplona: walled city of the San Fermín festival, entered over the medieval Magdalena bridge.
  • Puente la Reina: where the routes from Somport and Roncesvalles meet, crossing an 11th-century six-arched bridge.
  • Santo Domingo de la Calzada: the cathedral keeps a live hen and rooster in memory of the miracle of the hanged pilgrim, a famous legend.
  • Burgos: its Gothic cathedral is a World Heritage Site in its own right (since 1984).
  • León: the Cathedral's stained glass and the Royal Pantheon of San Isidoro, with its 12th-century frescoes.
  • Astorga: Roman walls and Gaudí's Episcopal Palace.
  • Cruz de Ferro: pilgrims leave a stone from home at the foot of an iron cross on a tall pole.
  • O Cebreiro: thatched pallozas and the church linked to a medieval Eucharistic miracle legend.
  • Sarria: the most popular start for those walking the last 100 km.

Stop by stop

The Camino Francés crosses the whole north of Spain: the Pyrenees, the vineyards of Navarre and La Rioja, the wheat plains of Castile and León, the mountains of El Bierzo and the green hills of Galicia. Below are the main stops from east to west.

Saint-Jean-Pied-de-Port

This walled Basque town on the Nive is the traditional start. Pilgrims come in through the Porte Saint-Jacques, register at the pilgrim office on the steep Rue de la Citadelle, and leave by the Porte d'Espagne across the old bridge. Red-shuttered houses lean over the river, and the green Pyrenean foothills rise on every side. Try Basque cake (gâteau basque), Bayonne ham, sheep's cheese (ossau-iraty) and Irouléguy wine.

Roncesvalles

The first day climbs about 1,250 m over the Col de Lepoeder (1,430 m) by the Route Napoléon, with huge views and griffon vultures circling overhead. It ends at Roncesvalles (Orreaga), a medieval hospital and collegiate church (13th century) where pilgrims have been given a bed and a blessing for 900 years. The evening pilgrim Mass ends with a blessing for those setting out.

Legend and history. The Song of Roland tells that Roland, Charlemagne's paladin, died here fighting the Saracens and blew his horn for help. History: in 778 it was Basque fighters who ambushed Charlemagne's rearguard in the pass. The chapel of Sancti Spiritus (the "Silo of Charlemagne") stands on the traditional burial place.

Pamplona

Navarre's capital is the first city on the way. Pilgrims enter over the Magdalena bridge and through the walls of the old town. The cathedral has a fine Gothic cloister, and the Plaza del Castillo is the city's living room, with Hemingway's Café Iruña. In July the San Fermín festival brings the running of the bulls (see St Fermín). Snack on pintxos in Calle Estafeta, and try chistorra sausage and Navarre asparagus.

Puente la Reina

Past the Alto del Perdón, with its iron pilgrim silhouettes and wind turbines, the way comes down to Puente la Reina, where the route from Somport (the Camino Aragonés) joins it. The town is named after the six-arched Romanesque bridge over the Arga, built in the 11th century by a queen of Navarre for pilgrims. In the Crucifix church hangs a Y-shaped German crucifix, said to have been brought by pilgrims.

Estella and the Irache wine fountain

Estella (Lizarra), "the beautiful" in Aymeric Picaud's 12th-century guide, has Romanesque treasures: the palace of the Kings of Navarre and the church of San Pedro de la Rúa, reached by a great stair. A few kilometres on, beside the Monastery of Irache, the Bodegas Irache winery has run a free wine fountain for pilgrims since 1991, with one tap for red wine and one for water. The sign asks pilgrims to drink in moderation. Local dishes include gorrín (roast piglet) and white beans.

Logroño

The capital of La Rioja is a wine city on the Ebro. Pilgrims enter over the stone bridge and pass the church of Santiago el Real, with its statue of St James on horseback over the door. The baroque twin towers of the co-cathedral of Santa María de la Redonda rise over the old town. In the evening, the bars of Calle Laurel each serve their own speciality: grilled mushrooms, patatas bravas, pinchos morunos and, of course, a glass of Rioja.

Santo Domingo de la Calzada

The town is named after St Dominic of the Causeway (d. 1109), a hermit who built a road, a bridge and a pilgrim hospital here.

Legend. A young German pilgrim was falsely accused of theft by an innkeeper's daughter and hanged. His parents found him still alive on the gallows, held up by the saint. They ran to the judge, who was sitting at dinner and scoffed that the boy was as alive as the roast hen and cock on his plate. At once the birds stood up and crowed. "Santo Domingo de la Calzada, where the hen sang after being roasted."

History. To this day a live white cock and hen are kept in a richly carved Gothic hen house inside the cathedral, and changed regularly. A crow heard during your visit is said to bring good luck on the way.

Burgos

Burgos Cathedral, begun in 1221, is one of the masterpieces of Gothic Europe and a UNESCO World Heritage site. Inside are the tomb of El Cid and his wife Jimena, the gilded Golden Staircase, and the Papamoscas, a clock figure that opens its mouth on the hour. The city sits on the river Arlanzón, with tree-lined promenades. Try morcilla de Burgos (blood sausage with rice), roast lamb and fresh Burgos cheese.

The Meseta and Frómista

After Burgos comes the Meseta, the high plateau of Castile: days of wheat fields, huge skies, adobe villages and long straight tracks. Many pilgrims dread it and later remember it as the most prayerful part of the Way. Castrojeriz, with its ruined castle, and the canal at Frómista break the horizon. At Frómista stands San Martín (1066), one of the purest Romanesque churches in Spain, with more than 300 carved corbels.

Sahagún

Sahagún marks the halfway point of the Francés. It was once the seat of a great Cluniac abbey. Its churches of San Tirso and San Lorenzo are the classic examples of Mudéjar Romanesque, built in brick by Muslim craftsmen working for Christian patrons. Pilgrims can get a certificate of having reached the halfway point at the Santuario de la Peregrina. Lamb and leeks are local specialities.

León

León Cathedral, the "House of Light", has 1,800 m² of medieval stained glass, which floods the nave with colour on sunny days. Nearby, the Basilica of San Isidoro holds the royal pantheon of the kings of León, whose Romanesque ceiling paintings are called the "Sistine Chapel of Romanesque". The former pilgrim hospital of San Marcos is now a parador. Try cecina (cured beef), and enjoy León's free tapa with every drink in the Barrio Húmedo.

Astorga

Roman Asturica Augusta is the place where the Vía de la Plata joins the Francés. Next to the Gothic cathedral stands the Episcopal Palace by Antoni Gaudí (1889–1915), a white granite fairy-tale castle that now houses the Museum of the Ways. Astorga is famous for chocolate and mantecadas (sponge cakes), and for the hearty cocido maragato, eaten in reverse order: meat first, soup last.

Cruz de Ferro

On the bare mountain pass of Monte Irago (about 1,500 m), the highest point of the Francés, stands a tall wooden pole topped with a small iron cross, on a great mound of stones.

Tradition. Pilgrims bring a stone from home and leave it at the foot of the cross, often with a prayer or the name of someone they carry in their heart, as a sign of laying down a burden. The custom may go back to Roman or earlier wayside cairns. The cross itself was reportedly set up by the hermit Gaucelmo in the 11th century.

The views from here stretch over the Bierzo valley, and the way passes the abandoned village of Foncebadón and the ruins of Manjarín.

Ponferrada

Down in the warm, wine-growing Bierzo valley, Ponferrada is named after the iron bridge (pons ferrata) built in the 11th century for pilgrims. The town is dominated by its huge Templar castle, given to the Order of the Temple in 1178. Bierzo specialities include botillo (spiced pork in casing), roasted peppers, chestnuts and Mencía wines.

Legend. The Templars are said to have found a statue of the Virgin in a holm oak (encina) while cutting wood for the castle; she is venerated as Our Lady of La Encina, patron of the Bierzo.

O Cebreiro

A hard climb brings pilgrims into Galicia at O Cebreiro (1,300 m), a hilltop village of round, thatched stone houses (pallozas), with views over the mountains of Lugo, often above a sea of cloud. The pre-Romanesque church of Santa María la Real is one of the oldest on the Way. Its priest, Elías Valiña (d. 1989), began painting the yellow arrows that now mark every Camino. Try caldo galego (cabbage soup) and the Cebreiro cheese, shaped like a chef's hat.

Legend. In the 14th century, a faithful peasant struggled through a snowstorm to Mass. The priest scorned him for coming so far for a bit of bread and wine. At that moment the host turned to flesh and the wine to blood. The chalice and paten of the miracle are kept in the church, and legend links them with the Holy Grail.

Samos

An optional variant goes down a green valley to the Benedictine Monastery of Samos, one of the oldest in Spain. It was founded in the 6th century and is still lived in by monks, who offer pilgrims a guided tour and a bed. Its huge cloisters are among the largest in the country.

Sarria

Sarria is the most popular starting point for pilgrims who walk only the last 100 km needed for the Compostela. The way climbs the old Rúa Maior to the ruined castle and the Monastery of La Magdalena, then crosses the medieval bridge over the Celeiro into countryside of oak woods, stone walls and dairy farms. Expect the Camino to become much busier from here.

Portomarín

Portomarín was drowned in 1962 by the Belesar reservoir on the Miño. Its main buildings were taken down and rebuilt stone by stone on the hill above, including the fortress church of San Nicolás (12th–13th century), a church built by the knights of St John. Pilgrims cross the reservoir on a high bridge and climb a steep stair into town. At low water the old houses reappear. The local speciality is aguardiente (grape spirit) and eels from the Miño.

Melide

Melide is where the Camino Primitivo joins the Francés, and it is the capital of pulpo á feira. Octopus is boiled in big copper pots, cut with scissors onto wooden plates, and dressed with olive oil, coarse salt and paprika, then eaten with bread and white Ribeiro wine. The pulperías Ezequiel and A Garnacha are pilgrim institutions. Afterwards, try the local almond cakes (melindres).

Monte do Gozo

Monte do Gozo, the "Mount of Joy", is the hill where medieval pilgrims first saw the spires of the cathedral, about 5 km from the end. Tradition says the first in a group to see them was called "king". A monument recalls Pope John Paul II's visit for World Youth Day in 1989, and nearby is a small chapel of St Mark. From here it is downhill to the city and the Praza do Obradoiro.
