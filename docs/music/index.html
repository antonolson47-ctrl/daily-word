Music of the Church YearBach and Handel, spirituals, holy blues, gospel and mountain hymns

Every season of the Church year has its music, and Christian music in America has come down several streams that keep meeting. Handel and Bach wrote for the liturgy of their own churches. African Americans made the spirituals from the Bible stories they heard, hiding hope of freedom inside the Exodus and the Cross. From the spirituals and the work songs came both the blues and gospel: the same flattened "blue" notes, the same call and response, the same moan, one sung on Saturday night and one on Sunday morning. Many blues singers were preachers' sons or preachers themselves, and the father of gospel, Thomas A. Dorsey, had been a blues pianist. Meanwhile, in the Appalachian hills, shape-note singing and mountain hymns became country gospel and bluegrass, and these streams crossed again and again, as when a Black guitarist named Lesley Riddle taught the Carter Family his songs. All of it, from Leipzig to Clarksdale to Clinch Mountain, sings the same Scriptures.

The classical masters

George Frideric Handel

Classical 1685–1759
German-born composer who made his career in London. He wrote *Messiah* in about 24 days in 1741 to a libretto by Charles Jennens drawn entirely from Scripture; it premiered in Dublin on 13 April 1742 as a charity concert for prisoners and hospitals. Handel conducted it yearly for London's Foundling Hospital.

Listen: 🎧 Hallelujah Chorus

Source: Library of Congress: Handel's Messiah

Photo: Attributed to Balthasar Denner · Public domain · Wikimedia Commons

Johann Sebastian Bach

Classical 1685–1750
Lutheran church musician, cantor of St Thomas, Leipzig, from 1723. He wrote some 200 surviving church cantatas for the Sundays of the year, the *St John* (1724) and *St Matthew* (1727) Passions for Good Friday, and the *Christmas Oratorio* (1734). He signed many scores "S.D.G.", *Soli Deo Gloria*, to God alone the glory.

Listen: 🎧 Erbarme dich St Matthew Passion

Source: Bach-Archiv Leipzig

Photo: Elias Gottlob Haussmann · Public domain · Wikimedia Commons

Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart

Classical 1756–1791
Austrian composer, raised in the Catholic cathedral music of Salzburg. His *Requiem* in D minor was unfinished at his death in December 1791 and completed by his pupil Franz Xaver Süssmayr. He also wrote the *Coronation Mass* and *Ave verum corpus* (1791) for Corpus Christi.

Listen: 🎧 Ave verum corpus K 618

Source: Internet Archive: Mozart Requiem

Photo: Johann Nepomuk della Croce · Public domain · Wikimedia Commons

Gabriel Fauré

Classical 1845–1924
French organist at the Madeleine in Paris and later director of the Paris Conservatoire. His gentle *Requiem* (1887–1890) leaves out the terrifying *Dies irae* and ends *In paradisum*, the prayer that angels lead the soul into paradise. He called it a lullaby of death.

Listen: 🎧 Requiem Pie Jesu

From the spirituals to gospel

1. Spirituals (18th–19th centuries). Sung in slavery and carried to the world by the Fisk Jubilee Singers from 1871. Songs like Go Down, Moses and Steal Away were prayers and, sometimes, coded escape plans.

Fisk Jubilee Singers

Spirituals founded 1871
A choir of students, most of them formerly enslaved, from Fisk University in Nashville. They toured the US and Europe from 1871 to save their school from bankruptcy, sang for Queen Victoria, and brought spirituals like *Swing Low, Sweet Chariot* to concert halls for the first time. Their earnings built Jubilee Hall.

Listen: 🎧 Swing Low Sweet Chariot

Source: Library of Congress: Fisk Jubilee Singers

Photo: Unidentified artist · Public domain · Wikimedia Commons

Marian Anderson

Spirituals 1897–1993
Contralto from Philadelphia. Barred from Constitution Hall in 1939, she sang on Easter Sunday at the Lincoln Memorial for 75,000 people, opening with *My Country, 'Tis of Thee* and closing with spirituals. In 1955 she became the first Black soloist at the Metropolitan Opera.

Listen: 🎧 Deep River

Source: Library of Congress: Marian Anderson

Photo: Carl Van Vechten · Public domain · Wikimedia Commons

2. Holy blues (1920s–30s). Street evangelists with guitars took the blues form and filled it with Scripture. Record companies sold them on the same "race records" as secular blues.

Blind Willie Johnson

Holy blues 1897–1945
Texas street evangelist with slide guitar and a rasping bass voice, who recorded 30 sides for Columbia from 1927 to 1930, all religious. His wordless *Dark Was the Night, Cold Was the Ground*, a meditation on the Crucifixion, was sent into space on the Voyager Golden Record in 1977.

Listen: 🎧 Dark Was the Night Cold Was the Ground

Source: Internet Archive: Blind Willie Johnson

Photo: Columbia Records "race series" · Public domain · Wikimedia Commons

Rev. Gary Davis

Holy blues 1896–1972
South Carolina-born guitarist and ordained Baptist minister who preached and sang on the streets of Durham and later Harlem. A master of ragtime-style fingerpicking, he refused for years to play "devil's music" and sang gospel blues: *Death Don't Have No Mercy*, *Samson and Delilah*, *I Am the Light of This World*.

Listen: 🎧 Samson and Delilah

Source: Library of Congress: Rev. Gary Davis

Photo: Unknown photographer · Public domain · Wikimedia Commons

Son House

Holy blues 1902–1988
Mississippi Delta bluesman who was a preacher before he was a blues singer, and spent his life torn between the two. His *John the Revelator* (1965) sings the Book of Revelation over handclaps alone, and his *Preachin' the Blues* is a sermon about a preacher who could not stop singing the blues.

Listen: 🎧 John the Revelator

Source: Library of Congress: Son House

Photo: Unknown photographer · Public domain · Wikimedia Commons

3. The birth of gospel (1930s). Thomas A. Dorsey brought blues piano and feeling into the Black church in Chicago and organised the first gospel choirs and conventions. Sister Rosetta Tharpe took it onto the radio and into the nightclubs; Mahalia Jackson made it the voice of a nation.

Thomas A. Dorsey

Gospel 1899–1993
"The father of gospel music." As "Georgia Tom" he was a blues pianist for Ma Rainey. After a religious conversion he joined blues feeling to church song at Pilgrim Baptist Church in Chicago. When his wife Nettie died in childbirth in 1932, and their baby son soon after, he wrote *Take My Hand, Precious Lord*, later sung by Mahalia Jackson at Martin Luther King Jr.'s funeral.

Listen: 🎧 Take My Hand Precious Lord

Source: Library of Congress: Thomas A. Dorsey

Sister Rosetta Tharpe

Gospel 1915–1973
Arkansas-born singer and electric guitarist who grew up in the Church of God in Christ and took gospel into nightclubs and the pop charts with *Rock Me* and *This Train* (1938). Her guitar playing on *Strange Things Happening Every Day* (1944) is often called the first rock and roll. She was inducted into the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame in 2018.

Mahalia Jackson

Gospel 1911–1972
New Orleans-born contralto, "the Queen of Gospel", who worked with Thomas A. Dorsey in Chicago. Her *Move On Up a Little Higher* (1947) sold millions. She refused to sing secular music, sang before the "I Have a Dream" speech at the March on Washington in 1963, and is said to have called out to King, "Tell them about the dream, Martin."

4. The quartets (1930s–50s). Four or five men in sharp suits, unaccompanied or with one guitar, singing "hard gospel" on Sunday afternoon programs across the South.

The Dixie Hummingbirds

Gospel quartet founded 1928
Founded by James B. Davis in Greenville, South Carolina, with Ira Tucker as lead singer from 1938. Among the most influential of the "hard gospel" quartets, they won a Grammy for *Loves Me Like a Rock* with Paul Simon (1973) and sang for over 75 years.

5. Gospel into soul (1950s–70s). Church-raised singers carried gospel into soul music and the civil-rights movement, and some came back to make the greatest gospel records of all.

Daughter of the Detroit preacher C. L. Franklin, raised singing in New Bethel Baptist Church. *Amazing Grace* (1972), recorded live in a Los Angeles church with Rev. James Cleveland, is the best-selling live gospel album of all time.

Appalachian, bluegrass and early country gospel

In the mountains of Virginia, Kentucky, Tennessee and North Carolina, Scots-Irish ballads met Baptist and Methodist hymnody. Shape-note tunebooks taught whole congregations to sing in rough four-part harmony; Primitive Baptists "lined out" psalms, a leader singing each line for the church to echo, a practice also kept in Black churches. When the radio and record business arrived in the 1920s, these songs became country and later bluegrass, and every major act kept a gospel set.

Where the streams crossed: A. P. Carter collected songs with Lesley Riddle, a Black guitarist who remembered the tunes and taught Maybelle his picking. Bill Monroe learned the blues from Arnold Shultz, a Black fiddler in Kentucky. Hank Williams credited Rufus "Tee-Tot" Payne. The "high lonesome" sound and the blues moan answer each other.

Sacred Harp singing

Appalachian / shape-note from 1844
A tradition named for *The Sacred Harp*, a tunebook compiled by B. F. White and E. J. King in Georgia in 1844, using four shaped noteheads so anyone could sight-read. Singers sit in a hollow square facing each other and sing full-voiced, with no instruments and no audience. Hymns like *Idumea* and *Wondrous Love* came through it.

Listen: 🎧 Wondrous Love

Source: Library of Congress: Sacred Harp

The Carter Family

A. P. Carter, his wife Sara and his sister-in-law Maybelle of Maces Spring, Virginia, recorded at the Bristol Sessions in 1927, often called the "big bang" of country music. A. P. collected songs across the mountains, and Maybelle's "Carter scratch" guitar became the model for country guitar. *Can the Circle Be Unbroken* (1935) reworks a 1907 hymn by Ada Habershon and Charles Gabriel.

Lesley Riddle

Blues 1905–1979
A Black guitarist from Kingsport, Tennessee, who travelled with A. P. Carter on song-collecting trips around 1928–1931, memorising tunes while Carter wrote down the words. He taught Maybelle Carter his finger-picking and slide style. The Carter Family's *Cannonball Blues* and *Hello Stranger* came through him: a meeting point of blues and Appalachian gospel.

Bill Monroe

Bluegrass 1911–1996
Kentucky mandolinist, "the father of bluegrass", who named the music after his band, the Blue Grass Boys. He learned from his uncle Pendleton Vandiver and from Arnold Shultz, a Black fiddler and guitarist; that blues feeling runs through his "high lonesome" sound. His gospel quartets (*Wicked Path of Sin*, *Precious Memories*) were a set piece of every show.

Why gospel and blues share the same roots

  • Same source. Both grew from the spirituals, field hollers and ring shouts of enslaved Africans in the American South, with their call and response, bent "blue" notes and rhythmic drive.
  • Same musicians. Thomas A. Dorsey played blues as "Georgia Tom" before writing Precious Lord. Son House preached before he sang blues; Rev. Gary Davis sang blues before he preached. Sister Rosetta Tharpe played the same guitar in church and in clubs.
  • Same subject, different answer. The blues names suffering honestly, with no resolution; gospel names the same suffering and answers it with the Cross and the Resurrection. Dorsey said gospel was "good news" sung with the feeling of the blues.
  • Same Scriptures. The Psalms of lament (Psalm 22, 88, 130) sit in the Bible alongside the Psalms of praise. Lent and Holy Week give the Church its "blues"; Easter its gospel.

Mahalia Jackson put it plainly: "Blues are the songs of despair, but gospel songs are the songs of hope." Both are honest prayer.
