## The story
Wilgefortis (Uncumber in England, Kümmernis in Germany, Librada in Spain) is the most instructive saint on this site: a bearded woman on a cross, in a dress, venerated for centuries by women seeking to be 'disencumbered' of husbands — and almost certainly born from a misread work of art.

## The full story

### The princess and the beard
The story told to explain the image: a Christian king's daughter of Portugal, vowed to virginity, was promised to a pagan king. She prayed to be made repulsive; a beard grew overnight; her furious father crucified her. Dying, she promised help to all who remembered her.

### The Volto Santo explanation
Scholars trace the cult to the Volto Santo of Lucca — a famous robed, crucified Christ wearing a long tunic and crown. Northern pilgrims unfamiliar with the Eastern-style robed crucifix read the figure as a woman, and a legend grew to fit the picture. Churches acquired bearded crucifixes; offerings of oats (in England, to 'uncumber' oneself) followed. Rome quietly let the cult lapse; images remain in museums and old churches as evidence of how devotion invents biography.

<div class=legend markdown=1>**Legend — and a caution.** There was no historical Wilgefortis as told. We include her, clearly labelled, because her cult is real religious history: it shows medieval people — especially women with few legal exits — praying with the images they had. The Church never canonised her and removed her feast in the 1969 calendar reform.</div>

## Path to sainthood
Never canonised; widespread late-medieval popular cult, tolerated locally, later discouraged and removed from the General Roman Calendar (1969).

## Patronages
Historically: women seeking relief from abusive marriages ('St Uncumber'), tribulation ('Kümmernis' = sorrow).

## Trivia
- English wives offered oats at her image in old St Paul's, London, to be 'uncumbered' of husbands — Chaucer-era London knew the joke and the prayer.
- Her story is the classic case study in how a misunderstood image can create a saint's Life.
