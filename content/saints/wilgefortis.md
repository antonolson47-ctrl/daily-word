## The story
Wilgefortis (Uncumber in England, Kümmernis in Germany, Librada in Spain) is a bearded woman on a cross, in a dress. For centuries women prayed to her to be set free, especially from husbands who harmed them, when the law gave them few ways out.

## The full story

### The princess and the beard
The story told to explain the image: a Christian king's daughter of Portugal, vowed to virginity, was promised to a pagan king. She prayed to be made repulsive; a beard grew overnight; her furious father crucified her. Dying, she promised help to all who remembered her.

### The Volto Santo explanation
The cult is traced to the Volto Santo of Lucca, a famous crucifix of Christ in a long robe and a crown. Northern pilgrims, unused to that Eastern form, took the figure for a woman, and the story of the Portuguese princess grew to fit the picture. Churches acquired bearded crucifixes. In England women offered oats so as to be 'uncumbered'. There is no historical princess behind the tale as it is told. The Church never canonised her, and the 1969 reform took the feast off many calendars. The images, and the prayers of the women who used them, remain.

## Path to sainthood
Never canonised; widespread late-medieval popular cult, tolerated locally, later discouraged and removed from the General Roman Calendar (1969).

## Patronages
Historically: women seeking relief from abusive marriages ('St Uncumber'), tribulation ('Kümmernis' = sorrow).

## Trivia
- English wives offered oats at her image in old St Paul's, London, to be 'uncumbered' of husbands. The prayer was known in Chaucer's London.
- The Volto Santo is how her image is usually explained.
