## The story
Zita went into domestic service at twelve in the Fatinelli household in Lucca and stayed more than forty years — first bullied and overworked, then so trusted she ran the house. Her sanctity is deliberately unglamorous: early Mass before work, scraps saved for the poor, and a temper mastered by prayer in a kitchen.

## The full story

### Bread in the apron
The best-loved story: Zita was carrying bread to the poor hidden in her apron when her mistress, suspecting theft, demanded she open it. Out fell — in the telling — flowers, or roses out of season. Variants attach the same miracle to Elizabeth of Hungary and others; for Zita it crystallises a real pattern of almsgiving from the household stores, eventually with her employers' blessing.

### Incorrupt in Lucca
She died in 1272 and was buried in San Frediano, Lucca. Her body, found incorrupt at exhumations, is displayed in a glass reliquary there. Dante already mentions 'Santa Zita' in the *Inferno* as shorthand for Lucca itself — fame within a generation.

<div class=legend markdown=1>**Legend.** Dante's reference proves an early cult, not the apron miracle. The flower transformation is a travelling medieval motif; what is historical is a servant-saint venerated by her own city almost immediately after death.</div>

## Path to sainthood
Popular cult from 1272; formally confirmed by Innocent XII in 1696. 

## Patronages
Domestic workers, housekeepers, waiters and waitresses, lost keys (popular).

## Trivia
- She is widely invoked as patron of domestic workers and of people who have lost their keys.
- Her feast is kept with special devotion by bakery and service workers in Lucca, where her body still rests in San Frediano.
