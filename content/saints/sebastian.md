## His story
Sebastian was a Christian martyr of Rome, buried on the Appian Way where the Basilica of San Sebastiano now stands; St Ambrose mentions him in the 4th century. The famous story comes from a later *Passio* (5th century) and is legend: an officer in the imperial guard, he was shot with arrows on the emperor's orders, nursed back to health by a widow named Irene, and then confronted the emperor and was clubbed to death.

## The arrows and the recovery: the full story

<div class=warn markdown=1>**History or legend?** Only three things are early and solid. The **Depositio Martyrum** (a list of Roman martyrs' burial feasts in the Chronograph of 354) records: "XIII Kal. Feb. … Sebastiani in Catacumbas", meaning Sebastian was honoured on 20 January at the cemetery "ad Catacumbas" on the Appian Way. **St Ambrose** (d. 397), commenting on Psalm 118, says Sebastian was a native of **Milan** who was martyred in Rome. And a basilica stood over his tomb by the 4th century. Everything else below, the soldier, the arrows, Irene and the club, comes from the ***Passio Sancti Sebastiani***, a long, dramatic text written in the early **5th century**, about 120 years later, and once wrongly credited to Ambrose. The 1912 *Catholic Encyclopedia* calls its stories "unhistorical". We tell the Passio's story in full because it shaped 1,500 years of devotion and art, and we flag the legend as we go.</div>

### An officer with a secret
In the Passio, Sebastian is a young man of Narbonne in Gaul, raised in Milan and brought up a Christian. He goes to Rome and joins the army, not out of love of soldiering (the author insists) but so he can stand close to Christians in danger. He rises to captain in the **imperial guard**, a trusted officer whom Diocletian and his co-emperor Maximian keep near them, with no idea of his faith. The Vatican's saint-of-the-day text sums up the tradition: his position allowed him "to help Christians in prison in secret".

The Passio then tells the story that fills most of its pages. Two twin brothers of a noble family, **Marcus and Marcellian**, are in prison awaiting execution for their faith. Their parents, **Tranquillinus** and Marcia, come with the brothers' wives and small children, weeping and begging them to offer incense to the gods and save their lives. The brothers begin to waver. Sebastian, in his officer's cloak, steps in and gives a long speech: the suffering of this world is short, and the crown of the martyr lasts forever. The brothers stand firm.

### Zoe's voice, and a household converted
The speech takes place in the house of **Nicostratus**, the official in charge of prisoners. His wife **Zoe** has been unable to speak for six years. Moved by Sebastian's words, she falls at his feet and asks by signs for help. He makes the sign of the cross over her, and she speaks, praising God. Nicostratus converts on the spot. So do the twins' parents; old Tranquillinus is cured of a crippling illness when he is baptised. The priest **Polycarp** baptises the converts, and even the city prefect, **Chromatius**, is won over and resigns his office.

The Passio then turns into a roll-call of martyrs. Zoe is arrested while praying at the tomb of St Peter and dies for the faith; Tranquillinus is stoned at the tomb of St Paul; Nicostratus and his companions are drowned; and **Marcus and Marcellian** are finally executed. One by one the little circle around Sebastian is destroyed. Some of these names do appear in old Roman calendars, which suggests the author wove real local martyrs into his story, but the plot that ties them together is his own.

### Exposed before the emperor
In the end Sebastian himself is denounced. In the Passio, Diocletian summons him and speaks words the Vatican News Italian page quotes: "I placed you among the great, giving you free access to my palace, and you plot against my safety and insult the gods of the state?" Sebastian answers that he has always prayed to Christ for the emperor's health and the empire's safety, but worships only the God in heaven.

The emperor gives a terrible order. Sebastian is to be taken to a field, stripped, tied to a stake, and **shot by archers**. Later retellings, followed by the Catholic Encyclopedia, make them **Mauretanian** archers, North African bowmen known in the Roman army. The Passio says they filled him with arrows until he looked like a hedgehog (*ericius*), and left him for dead.

### Irene comes in the night
That night a Christian widow named **Irene**, the widow of the martyr Castulus, comes with her servants to take down the body and bury it. She finds that Sebastian is still breathing. Instead of a burial, she carries him secretly to her own house, where she cleans and dresses his wounds and nurses him back to health. This quiet scene became one of the most-painted moments in Christian art: a woman with a lamp, bending over a wounded young man in the dark.

### Back to the palace steps
Friends urge the recovered Sebastian to flee Rome. He refuses. In the Passio's most dramatic scene, he goes and stands on the **steps of Heliogabalus** (*gradus Heliogabali*), a staircase on the Palatine Hill by the imperial palace, and waits for the emperor to pass. When Diocletian appears, Sebastian confronts him face to face, condemning the persecution of Christians. The emperor, stunned to see a man he thought was dead, asks if this is the Sebastian he ordered shot. Sebastian answers that the Lord brought him back to life so that he could bear witness to the emperor's cruelty.

### The club and the Cloaca Maxima
This time Diocletian makes sure. Sebastian is taken to the **hippodrome of the palace** and beaten to death with clubs. So that Christians cannot honour his body as a martyr's, it is thrown into the **Cloaca Maxima**, Rome's great sewer.

### Lucina's dream
That night, the Passio says, Sebastian appears in a dream to a Christian noblewoman named **Lucina** and tells her where his body is hanging, caught on a hook in the sewer. He asks to be buried "ad Catacumbas", at the entrance of the crypt close to the resting place of the apostles. She recovers the body and buries it there, on the **Appian Way**. Here legend reconnects with history: the burial place in the Passio is the same cemetery the Depositio of 354 already named.

### The basilica on the Appian Way
A large basilica was built over the cemetery in the 4th century. It was first linked to the apostles Peter and Paul, whose memory was honoured there, and later dedicated to Sebastian. It became one of the traditional **Seven Pilgrim Churches** of Rome. Cardinal Scipione Borghese rebuilt it in its present form, finished in 1611: **San Sebastiano fuori le mura**, still served by Franciscan friars, with the catacombs of St Sebastian beneath it. In 826 part of his relics was taken to the abbey of St Médard at Soissons in France.

### Patron against the plague
Sebastian's greatest popularity came from the plague. The Lombard historian **Paul the Deacon** (*History of the Lombards* VI.5, late 8th century) describes the plague of **680** that struck Rome and then **Pavia** (Ticinum). In Pavia people fled to the mountains, and grass grew in the market square. People saw a good angel and a bad angel walking the city at night; each time the bad angel struck a door with his spear, someone in that house died the next day. Then it was revealed that the plague would not stop until an **altar to St Sebastian** was set up in the church of St Peter in Chains. Relics were brought from Rome, the altar was built, and, Paul writes, the plague stopped.

The arrows helped too. In the ancient and biblical imagination, disease fell like arrows shot from heaven, and a saint who had survived arrows was the natural protector against it. In the Black Death and later outbreaks, towns across Europe built Sebastian chapels and processed with his image. He is also a patron of soldiers, archers and athletes.

### From bearded courtier to arrow-pierced youth
The oldest images look nothing like the familiar one. A 7th-century mosaic in San Pietro in Vincoli in Rome (traditionally dated to around 680, after the plague) shows a **grey-bearded man in court dress** holding a martyr's crown, with no arrows at all. The young, half-naked figure tied to a column is a creation of the **Renaissance**, when painters seized on Sebastian as a chance to paint the classical nude within a sacred subject:

- **Andrea Mantegna** painted him at least three times: the small Vienna panel (c. 1457–59, shown at the top of this page, with the artist's signature in Greek on the column), the life-size Louvre canvas (c. 1480), and the late Ca' d'Oro picture in Venice (c. 1490).
- **Antonello da Messina** set him against a bright Venetian square with people going about their day (c. 1478, Dresden).
- **Guido Reni** returned to him again and again in the 17th century; his versions in Genoa, Rome and the Prado made Sebastian's upturned, suffering face famous.
- **Georges de La Tour** painted not the shooting but the aftermath: Irene and her servant by candle or torchlight, in several versions (one in the Louvre, another, *Saint Sebastian Tended by Irene*, in the Kimbell Art Museum, Texas).

### What to take away
Historically, we can say this much: a Christian named Sebastian, connected with Milan, died as a martyr in Rome, probably during the persecutions of the late 3rd or early 4th century, and was buried and venerated on the Appian Way. The officer, the twins, Zoe, the arrows, Irene's nursing, the palace steps and Lucina's dream belong to the 5th-century Passio. They are less a biography than a sermon in story form: a man who could not be killed by the first attempt, and who walked back to face the emperor.

## Path to sainthood
Pre-Congregation saint, venerated since the 4th century.

## Patronages
Soldiers, athletes, archers, against plague.

## Trivia
- He became a major **plague saint** in the Middle Ages, perhaps because arrows were a symbol of sudden disease.
- The arrow-pierced Sebastian was one of Renaissance painters' favourite subjects (Mantegna painted him three times).
- He is one of the patrons of athletes and of the city of Rome.
