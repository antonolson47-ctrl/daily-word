## His story
Basil was born around 330 at Caesarea in Cappadocia, in a family that already had a name for holiness. His grandmother Macrina the Elder had lived through the last persecution. His sister, Macrina the Younger, and his brothers Gregory of Nyssa and Peter of Sebaste are all remembered as saints. He studied at Caesarea, Constantinople, and Athens, where his closest friend was [Gregory Nazianzen](/saints/gregory-nazianzen/). Like many Christians of that century he put off baptism. Around 357, under his sister's influence, he was baptized, gave away much of his property, and went to look at the monks of Egypt, Syria, and Mesopotamia.

What he brought home was a rule for living together. At Annesi, on the river Iris in Pontus, he settled a community and wrote the *Asketikon*: the Longer Rules and the Shorter Rules, questions and answers about prayer, work, obedience, and the common table. Eastern monasticism still lives from that book. Benedict, two centuries later in Italy, knew Basil's teaching and named him in his own Rule. Basil did not want hermits competing to be harsher than each other. He wanted brothers who prayed, worked, and ate in one house, and who kept the poor in view.

In 370 he became bishop of Caesarea. The emperor Valens favoured the Arians, who denied that the Son is of one being with the Father. Basil spent the decade arguing, in letters, in *Against Eunomius*, and in *On the Holy Spirit*, that the Spirit too is to be worshipped and glorified with the Father and the Son. He was not a comfortable court bishop. Outside the city he built the Basiliad, a whole settlement of care: hospital, hospice, and workshops for the sick and the strangers. Gregory of Nazianzus, preaching after Basil's death, described it as a new city. Basil died on **1 January 379**, worn out, not yet fifty.

The letters, the rules, and the treatise on the Holy Spirit are his own books, and they are why he is called Great. The friendship with Gregory, the quarrel over the little see of Sasima, and the long fight with Valens are told by people who knew him. The Basiliad is what his city remembered him for. The Liturgy of St Basil, still used in the East on some great feasts, bears his name. The prayers grew over the centuries around his teaching. Herrera's painting, above, is how his admirers pictured him: the bishop speaking doctrine while a scribe writes.

## Path to sainthood
Pre-Congregation. Honoured in East and West from his own century. He is a Doctor of the Church. The East keeps his feast on **1 January**, the day he died, and remembers him again on **30 January** with Gregory and [John Chrysostom](/saints/john-chrysostom/) as the Three Holy Hierarchs. The Roman calendar since 1969 remembers Basil and Gregory together on **2 January**. Older Western books kept Basil on 14 June.

## Patronages
Cappadocia, monks, hospital work, and the reform of church life. Invoked by people who have to lead a community and still keep a rule.

## Trivia
- His family produced so many saints that Gregory of Nyssa wrote lives of both Macrina and their brother.
- He and Gregory Nazianzen compiled the *Philokalia* of Origen, a selection, not the later monastic anthology of the same name.
- The Basiliad was so large that Gregory compared it to the pyramids, except that it was built for the living poor.
