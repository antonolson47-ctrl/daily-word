## Her story
The Gospels never name a Veronica. On the way to the cross they name Simon of Cyrene, the women of Jerusalem, and the soldiers. They do not name a woman who wiped Christ's face.

The story as Christians came to tell it is this. In the press of the crowd on the road to Calvary, a woman pushed forward with a linen cloth and wiped the sweat and blood from his face. When she drew the cloth back, his image was on it. The cloth became the **Veronica**, or the *vernicle*, and she became Veronica.

The name itself is part of the devotion. *Veronica* was explained in the Middle Ages as *vera icon*, "true image", a title of the cloth that was then given to the woman. An older telling had already called the woman healed of a haemorrhage (Mark 5:25–34) **Berenice**, and later writers folded that woman, the veil, and the road to Calvary into one person. The Gospels do not name her. The **Sixth Station** of the Cross, "Veronica wipes the face of Jesus", belongs to the Way of the Cross as it settled in the later Middle Ages and with the Franciscans.

What is historical is the relic and the crowd it drew. From the twelfth century a cloth called the Veronica was one of the great treasures of **St Peter's** in Rome. In jubilee years it was shown from the loggia, and pilgrims measured their journey by whether they had seen "the holy face". The present cloth is rarely shown, and its history is tangled. The woman of the legend was never given a feast on the universal calendar.

## Path to sainthood
She is a figure of devotion rather than a saint with a documented life. She is not on the General Roman Calendar and is not in the Roman Martyrology as a person whose acts are known. The Stations of the Cross keep her story in front of anyone who prays them.

## Patronages
Laundry workers, photographers, and people who perform a hidden act of mercy. These patronages grew out of the legend of the cloth.

## Trivia
- Hans Memling's painting, now in Washington, shows her holding the veil up like a processional banner, with the face of Christ painted on it. That is how the late Middle Ages imagined her.
- Dante, in *Paradiso*, remembers the old custom of pilgrims who travelled to Rome for the Veronica and went home talking of nothing else.
- Several towns claim a *Volto Santo* or a face-cloth. The Roman Veronica is the one the medieval West meant.
