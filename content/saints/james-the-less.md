## His story
One of the Twelve is **James the son of Alphaeus** (Matthew 10:3, Mark 3:18, Luke 6:15, Acts 1:13). Mark also mentions a **James the younger** (*ho mikros*, "the small" or "the less"), son of a Mary who stood near the cross, whose brother was Joses (Mark 15:40). Paul and Acts speak of another James again: **James the brother of the Lord**, the leader of the church in Jerusalem, whom Paul met (Galatians 1:19; 2:9), who spoke at the council in Acts 15, and who is the traditional author of the Epistle of James.

From early centuries many Western writers folded these into one man, and called him James the Less to distinguish him from [James the Greater](/saints/james-the-greater/), the son of Zebedee, whose feast is 25 July and whose tomb is the goal of the Camino. Other readers, including many modern scholars and a strong Eastern tradition, keep them apart: the apostle son of Alphaeus on one side, and James of Jerusalem, the Lord's brother, on the other. This page will not pretend the question is settled.

What is firm is James of Jerusalem. Paul knew him. Josephus, a Jewish historian who was not writing hagiography, says that the high priest Ananus had "the brother of Jesus, who was called Christ, whose name was James" stoned, around the year 62 (*Antiquities* 20.9). Hegesippus, a second-century Christian whom Eusebius quotes, tells a longer death: James the Just, thrown from the pinnacle of the Temple and clubbed. The club became the attribute in art. Rubens puts a wooden club in this apostle's hand. If James the son of Alphaeus is not James of Jerusalem, then the club belongs to the Jerusalem martyr and has been lent to the apostle by the old identification. The painting is an early modern opinion, not a photograph.

<div class=history markdown=1>

**History.** A James son of Alphaeus is in every list of the Twelve. A James, brother of the Lord, led the Jerusalem church and died a violent death under Ananus, which Josephus records. Those are two facts. That they are one biography is a tradition, ancient and widespread, and still argued.

</div>

<div class=legend markdown=1>

**If the tradition is followed.** Then James the Less is the bishop of Jerusalem, author of the epistle, and the martyr of the pinnacle, and his feast with [Philip](/saints/philip-apostle/) is the feast of that man. **If the names are kept separate,** the apostle son of Alphaeus has almost no story beyond the list, and the rich narrative belongs to James of Jerusalem, who may not have been one of the Twelve. Either way, he is not James the Greater, and he is not the James of Compostela.

</div>

## The calendar
Catholics keep Philip and James on **3 May**. Lutherans and the Church of England keep them on **1 May**, which was also the Roman date until 1955, when Pius XII moved the apostles to make room for [Joseph the Worker](/saints/joseph/#worker). From 1955 to 1969 the Roman date was 11 May. The James of this feast is James the Less, not the pilgrim of Compostela.

## Path to sainthood
An apostle, or, on the other reading, the first bishop of Jerusalem named in the New Testament. Pre-Congregation.

## Patronages
Fullers and hatters, from the club of the martyrdom story; pharmacists in some local lists; the dying, because of the manner of James of Jerusalem's death. Those patronages sit on the traditional identification.

## Trivia
- "The Less" translates *minor* or *mikros*. It may mean younger, or shorter, or simply "the other James".
- The Eastern churches keep James the brother of the Lord on 23 October, and do not automatically make him the son of Alphaeus.
- Rubens's apostle series gives each man his attribute. The club is the legend's prop.
