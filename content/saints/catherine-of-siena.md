## Her story
The 24th of 25 children of a Sienese dyer, Catherine refused marriage and became a Dominican tertiary, living a life of prayer and fasting at home before turning to care for the sick and poor. Though she learned to write only late, she dictated hundreds of letters to popes, kings and ordinary people, and a book of mystical theology, *The Dialogue*. She urged **Pope Gregory XI** to return the papacy from Avignon to Rome, which he did in 1377, and she worked for peace in the Church during the Western Schism. She died in Rome aged 33.

## The full story: dyer's daughter, mystic and Doctor of the Church

<div class=warn markdown=1>**History or legend?** Catherine is one of the best-documented women of the Middle Ages. We have **about 380 of her letters**, her book, the *Dialogue*, and her prayers, along with the detailed *Legenda Maior* written by her confessor, **Raymond of Capua**, and testimony collected after her death. Her public role (Avignon, Florence, the Schism) is solid history. Her **mystical experiences** (the espousal, the exchange of hearts, the stigmata) are known almost entirely from Raymond's hagiography and her own reports. We tell them as he tells them, and mark them as such.</div>

### The 24th child of a dyer
Caterina Benincasa was born in 1347 in the Fontebranda district of **Siena**, the 24th of the 25 children of **Giacomo Benincasa**, a wool dyer, and his wife Lapa; she was a twin, but her sister Giovanna died in infancy. According to Raymond she had her first vision of Christ at about six, and at seven privately vowed her virginity to God. When her parents tried to arrange a marriage for her in her teens, she cut off her hair in protest. The family eventually gave in and let her have a small room of her own, where she lived in silence, prayer and severe fasting for about three years.

Around 1364–65 she was accepted among the **Mantellate**, the Dominican "Third Order" women of Siena, mostly widows, who wore the black mantle over the white habit but lived at home, not in a convent. It was unusual for a young unmarried woman. She learned to read, though she seems to have learned to write only near the end of her life.

### The espousal and the exchange of hearts (hagiography)
Raymond describes how, during the Carnival of about 1366–67, while Siena celebrated outside, Christ appeared to Catherine in her room with his mother and saints and **espoused her** with a ring that only she could see. This "mystical marriage" became one of the most-painted scenes in Sienese art. A few years later, Raymond writes, Christ took her heart and, days afterwards, gave her his own in its place. These are hagiographic accounts, reported by Raymond from Catherine's words; the Church honours them as part of her spiritual biography but has never required belief in them. After the espousal, she left her seclusion to nurse the sick in Siena's hospitals, care for the poor and visit prisoners.

### The "Caterinati" and the Florence examination
A circle of followers gathered around her: friars, priests, noblewomen, artists and young patricians. They called her ***mamma*** and were known as the **Caterinati** (the "Catherine people"). Her growing fame, and her extreme fasting, made some suspicious. In **May 1374** she was summoned to **Florence**, where the Dominicans were holding their **general chapter**, apparently to examine her orthodoxy and way of life. She was cleared, and the order assigned her a new director, the learned friar **Raymond of Capua**, who became her confessor, companion and, later, biographer and master general of the Dominicans.

### Letters dictated to secretaries
Most of Catherine's letters were **dictated** to secretaries from among her followers, sometimes to two or three at once on different subjects, as witnesses recalled. She wrote to popes and cardinals, kings and queens, condottieri and prisoners, nuns, merchants and her own mother. Almost all begin the same way: "*I, Caterina, servant and slave of the servants of Jesus Christ, write to you in his precious blood…*" They are among the classics of early Italian prose.

### The stigmata at Pisa (hagiography)
On **1 April 1375**, the fourth Sunday of Lent (*Laetare* Sunday), Catherine was in **Pisa**, praying after Communion in the little church of **Santa Cristina** on the Arno. Raymond, who was present, says her body rose to her knees, her arms stretched out and her face shone, and then she collapsed. She told him afterwards that she had seen Christ crucified coming towards her in a great light, and five rays, red as blood, coming from his wounds towards her hands, feet and heart. She begged him that the scars should not appear on her body, and the rays turned to pure light as they reached her. The wounds stayed **invisible** during her life, with only their pain, and, Raymond adds, they became visible only after her death. The Catholic Encyclopedia records the same tradition.

**The controversy:** The Franciscans held that their founder, Francis of Assisi, was the unique stigmatic, and objected to a Dominican woman being shown with the wounds. In a bull of **25 July 1475**, *Licet dum militans*, the Franciscan **Pope Sixtus IV** forbade depicting Catherine (and any saint but Francis) with the stigmata. The dispute ran for generations. Dominican writers argued that invisible wounds could still be painted, and Siena's artists often showed the scene anyway, with the rays reaching her but no bleeding wounds. The devotion to her stigmata was formally recognised by the Holy See in the early 18th century.

### Peacemaker: Florence and Avignon
The popes had lived at **Avignon**, in what is now southern France, since 1309. In 1375 papal misrule in central Italy drove **Florence** into the "War of the Eight Saints" against Pope **Gregory XI**, who placed the city under interdict. Catherine poured out letters to the Pope urging three things: make peace, reform the Church, and come back to Rome. Her letters to him, kept on Wikisource, use a startling tone for a laywoman writing to a pope. She asks him to be "*uomo virile e non timoroso*" (a manly man, not a fearful one), which is the source of the popular paraphrase "Be a man!". She tells him: "*venite, venite, e non tardate più*" ("come, come, and delay no longer"), and to come "*con la croce in mano come agnello mansueto*" ("with the cross in your hand like a gentle lamb"), not with soldiers.

In **June 1376** she travelled to **Avignon** herself, with Raymond and some followers, hoping to make peace for Florence. The Florentines undercut her, but she met Gregory XI several times and pressed him to return. He left Avignon in September 1376 and entered **Rome on 17 January 1377**. Historians debate how much Catherine's pleading decided him (he had been planning the move for years), but Paul VI called the Pope's return "truly the masterpiece of her activity". In 1378 she went back to Florence as a peace envoy and was nearly killed in a riot; peace was signed with the new Pope that summer.

### Loyal to Urban VI in the Great Schism
In April 1378 the cardinals elected **Urban VI**; within months, several of them declared the election invalid and chose a rival, Clement VII, who returned to Avignon. This began the **Western Schism**. Catherine backed Urban without hesitation, as the true pope. He called her to Rome in November 1378, where she spent her last months writing to rulers and cardinals on his behalf, while also rebuking Urban himself for his harshness. In Siena that year she had dictated the ***Dialogue***.

### Death in Rome
Worn out by fasting and illness, she suffered a stroke-like collapse early in 1380. Every day, as long as she could, she walked to St Peter's to pray. She died on **29 April 1380** in her house near **Santa Maria sopra Minerva**, aged 33, offering her life, in her own words, "for this mystical body of holy Church".

### Why she is a Doctor of the Church
The title **Doctor of the Church** ("teacher") is given to a saint whose writings the Church recognises as a lasting guide for all believers. It requires three things: **eminent doctrine** (*eminens doctrina*), **outstanding holiness** (*insignis vitae sanctitas*) and a **formal declaration** by the pope. For centuries it was given only to men. On **27 September 1970** Pope Paul VI declared **Teresa of Ávila** a Doctor, and a week later he did the same for Catherine. The date is usually given as **4 October 1970**, though vatican.va heads his homily "Sunday, 3 October 1970". They were the first two women Doctors.

**Her writings:** the ***Dialogue*** (also called *The Book of Divine Providence* or *of Divine Doctrine*), a conversation between God the Father and a soul; about **380 letters**; and some **26 prayers** taken down by her disciples while she prayed aloud.

**Her teaching:** At the heart of the *Dialogue* is the image of **Christ as a bridge** joining heaven and earth over the flooded river of sin, a bridge with three "stairs" (the feet, the open side and the mouth of the crucified Christ), which the soul climbs from fear to love to union. Another key idea is the "**cell of self-knowledge**": knowing our own nothingness and God's goodness at the same time, so that humility and love grow together. She speaks constantly of **truth**, of the **Blood of Christ** poured out for all, and of the **Church** as Christ's bride, a Church to be reformed "not with war but with peace and quiet, with humble and continuous prayers, sweat and tears". She called the pope "**the sweet Christ on earth**", and wrote that the Church "is nothing other than Christ himself".

**Why, with so little schooling?** Paul VI faced this directly. In his homily he admits that one will not find in her "the apologetic vigour and theological daring" of the great ancient Doctors, nor "the high speculations of systematic theology" from the "unlettered virgin of Fontebranda". What strikes him instead is her "**infused wisdom**": a deep grasp of the truths of faith, which he calls a charism of the Holy Spirit. He quotes Pius II's bull of canonisation: "*Her doctrine was not acquired; she was a teacher before she was a pupil.*"

### Patroness of Italy and Europe
**Pope Pius XII** named Catherine and Francis of Assisi principal **patrons of Italy** in **1939**, and **Pope John Paul II** made her one of the three **co-patronesses of Europe** in **1999**, with St Bridget of Sweden and St Teresa Benedicta of the Cross (Edith Stein). Her **head** is venerated in San Domenico, Siena, near the spot where she joined the Mantellate; her body lies under the high altar of Santa Maria sopra Minerva in Rome.

## Path to sainthood
Canonised by **Pope Pius II** in **1461**. Declared a **Doctor of the Church** by **Pope Paul VI** in October 1970 (with Teresa of Ávila, the first women so named), and **co-patroness of Europe** by Pope John Paul II in 1999.

## Patronages
Italy (with Francis of Assisi), Europe, nurses.

## Trivia
- Her **head** is venerated in the Basilica of San Domenico in Siena; most of her body lies in Santa Maria sopra Minerva in Rome.
- She reported receiving the **stigmata**, invisible to others during her life.
- She wrote to the pope with remarkable frankness, telling him to "be a man" and return to Rome.
