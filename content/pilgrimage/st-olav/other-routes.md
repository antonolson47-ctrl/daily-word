In the Middle Ages most pilgrims to Nidaros probably came **by sea**. The coastal route, **Kystpilegrimsleia**, revives that journey: about 1,080 km from **Egersund** to **Trondheim**, combining public boats, buses and walking between **25 key sites** of churches, monasteries and saints. Five more land routes lead in from the south and west, and two from the far north.

## Good to know
- **Kystpilegrimsleia** is open all year, though some lodgings close in winter. Plan 3–4 weeks to visit all 25 key sites; most people do a few days at a time. Use [Entur](https://entur.no/) to plan boats and buses.
- **The Olav Letter** on the coast route needs stamps from at least **six key sites** plus Nidaros Cathedral. The pilgrim passport costs NOK 50.
- **By your own boat:** take great care rounding **Stad**, an exposed headland.
- **Nordleden:** in 2026 the National Pilgrim Centre advises against walking it until marking and infrastructure are improved.

## Deep Dive

### Kystpilegrimsleia, the sea road to Nidaros
<div class=history markdown=1>
**History.** The earliest description of pilgrim travel in Norway, by **Adam of Bremen** in about **1075**, describes the sea route: from Denmark a day's sail to the Vik (Oslofjord), then along the coast, reaching Trondheim on the fifth day. The overland way over the mountains, he says, takes longer and is dangerous. Kings built *sælehus* (travellers' shelters) along the coast too; the place-name **Salhus** north of Bergen remembers one. The modern route was approved as a St Olav Way in **December 2017** and opened with a voyage of the ship MS *Risør* from Egersund to Trondheim in **June 2018**.
</div>

**Some key sites, south to north:** **Stavanger Cathedral** (begun around 1125, dedicated to St Swithun of Winchester); **Utstein Abbey**, Norway's best-preserved medieval monastery; **Avaldsnes** with St Olav's church; **Moster**, where Olav's Christian law was proclaimed around 1024, with Moster old church; **Bergen**, with St Sunniva, St Mary's Church and Lyse Abbey; and **Selja**, the island of **St Sunniva**.

<div class=legend markdown=1>
**Legend: St Sunniva and the Seljemenn.** An Irish princess named **Sunniva** fled a pagan suitor by sea with her followers and came ashore on the island of **Selja**. When the local chieftain came to attack them, they prayed in a cave that the rocks would cover them, and the cave collapsed. Later a light was seen over the island and their bodies were found incorrupt. In **996** King Olav Tryggvason (not St Olav) had a church built there, and Selja became western Norway's first bishopric. In **1170** Sunniva's relics were moved to Bergen, where she is patron. The ruins of the monastery and the cave chapel on Selja can be visited by boat from Selje.
</div>

### The southern routes
- **Borgleden** (Halden–Sarpsborg–Ås–Oslo): through the farmland of south-east Norway to Oslo, named after **Borg**, the medieval Sarpsborg, which Olav founded around 1016. The **Borgarsyssel Museum** there holds the ruins of St Nicholas's church.
- **Tunsbergleden** (Larvik–Tønsberg–Bærum): along the Oslofjord, named after **Túnsberg**, Old Norse for Tønsberg, often called Norway's oldest town.

### The mountain and northern routes
- **Valldalsleden** (Valldal–Lesja–Dovre): from the fjord to the mountains in the footsteps of St Olav, who by tradition came this way in 1028. Spectacular but challenging, and only for experienced mountain hikers. It joins Gudbrandsdalsleden on Dovrefjell.
- **Romboleden**: described by the National Pilgrim Centre as Norway's oldest pilgrim path, linking St Olav and **St Birgitta** of Sweden, for experienced hikers.
- **Nordleden**: from the north towards Trondheim; currently not recommended (see above).
