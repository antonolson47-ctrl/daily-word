"I was glad when they said to me, 'Let us go to the house of the LORD!'" (Psalm 122:1, ESV). For a thousand years Christians of the North walked to Rome to pray at the tombs of the Apostles **Peter and Paul**. Their road, the **Via Francigena** ("the road that comes from France"), runs about **2,000 km** from **Canterbury Cathedral**, across the English Channel, through northern and eastern France, over the Alps at the **Great St Bernard Pass** and through Tuscany and Lazio to **St Peter's Basilica**. Its backbone is the diary of one man: **Sigeric**, Archbishop of Canterbury, who recorded his journey home from Rome in **990**.

## Good to know
- **Distance and time:** Canterbury to Rome is about 2,000 km, roughly **90–100 days** on foot. Most pilgrims walk it in sections over several years. The most popular stretch by far is the Italian part, especially **Lucca or Siena to Rome** (about 3 weeks from Siena).
- **Credential:** carry the official pilgrim credential of the **European Association of the Via Francigena Ways (EAVF)**, sold by the Association, by Canterbury Cathedral and at points along the route. Stamp it at churches, hostels and town halls each day.
- **The Testimonium:** St Peter's Basilica issues the *Testimonium*, the pilgrim's certificate, to those who have travelled **at least the last 100 km on foot, or 200 km by bicycle**, shown by the stamps in the credential. Enter by the signed *Percorso Oranti* (Prayer Path) from the obelisk in St Peter's Square and ask at the Basilica's reception desk.
- **Waymarking:** the route is well marked in Italy (white-and-red signs and the little brown pilgrim figure), reasonably marked in Switzerland and in most of France. Use the EAVF's official stages and GPS tracks.
- **Season:** spring and autumn are best. The **Great St Bernard Pass** (2,469 m) is snowbound from about October to June; the road over the col opens in early June. Tuscany and Lazio are very hot in July and August.
- **Accommodation:** church and pilgrim hostels (*ospitali*, often donation-based) are common in Italy, rarer in France, where pilgrims mix gîtes, B&Bs and hotels.

## Deep Dive

### Sigeric's itinerary
<div class=history markdown=1>
**History.** In **990** **Sigeric the Serious**, newly made Archbishop of Canterbury, went to Rome to receive the **pallium** from Pope **John XV**. A short manuscript now in the **British Library** (Cotton MS Tiberius B V) lists the churches he visited in Rome in two days, and then about **80 stopping places** (*submansiones*) on the way home, from Rome to the Channel. It is the oldest detailed itinerary of the route, and modern planners have used it to set the official line of the Via Francigena. The road itself is older: Lombard and Frankish travellers used it from the 7th and 8th centuries, keeping inland, away from the Byzantine-held coast.
</div>

In the Middle Ages the three great pilgrimages of Christendom were **Jerusalem**, **Santiago** and **Rome**. Rome pilgrims were called *romei*, and Dante speaks of them in the *Vita Nuova*. Kings, bishops and ordinary people walked south; hospices for them were founded at the passes and river crossings, many by the Church and by monastic orders. The first **Holy Year**, proclaimed by Pope **Boniface VIII** in **1300**, brought huge crowds along the road.

### The revival
<div class=history markdown=1>
**History.** After the Reformation and the coming of carriages and railways, the walking road was forgotten. In **1994** the Council of Europe named the Via Francigena a **European Cultural Route**, and in **2004** a *Major* Cultural Route. The **EAVF**, founded in **2001**, now coordinates the route in England, France, Switzerland and Italy; it also promotes the southern extension, the *Vie Francigene del Sud*, from Rome to the ports of Apulia, where medieval pilgrims took ship for the Holy Land.
</div>

<div class=legend markdown=1>
**Legends and traditions.** The name *Francigena* has nothing to do with St Francis; it means "born in" or "coming from the land of the Franks". Pilgrims' tradition has it that the first glimpse of the dome of St Peter's from **Monte Mario**, the medieval *Mons Gaudii* ("Mount of Joy"), was the moment pilgrims knelt and sang. Medieval pilgrims returning from Rome wore badges of the **keys of St Peter** or the **Veronica**, just as Santiago pilgrims wore the scallop shell.
</div>

### Why walk to Rome
The goal is not a city but a confession of faith: the tomb of St Peter, on whom Christ built his Church (Matthew 16:18), and the tomb of St Paul. Along the way stand the shrines of saints who also walked: **Thomas Becket** in Canterbury, **Bernard of Aosta** at the pass, the **Volto Santo** in Lucca, **St Catherine of Siena**, the Eucharistic miracle of **Bolsena**. The letter to the Hebrews says of the patriarchs that "they were strangers and exiles on the earth" (Hebrews 11:13, ESV); the pilgrim road teaches that again, one day at a time.
