<div class=history markdown=1>
**History.** The main pilgrimage site of the Dodecanese, set on a quiet bay in the south of Symi. The monastery is documented in the 15th century. It was destroyed in the mid-18th century and rebuilt in **1783**, when the present church (*katholikon*) and its frescoes were made. It is famous for its huge icon of the Archangel Michael, covered in silver in **1724** and honoured by sailors. Who painted the icon, and when, is unknown ([Visit Greece](https://www.visitgreece.gr/en/experiences/religious-tourism/monasteries/monastery-of-the-archangel-michael-panormitis)).
</div>

<div class=legend markdown=1>
**Legend.** The icon was found here and, the story goes, kept returning to this spot whenever it was moved, so the monastery was built where it chose to stay. Sailors once sent offerings floating in bottles that the current carried to the monastery.
</div>

<div class=warn markdown=1>Note: Panormitis is an Orthodox monastery. Visitors should dress modestly (covered shoulders and knees).</div>


## Panormitis in depth

### Founding stories: legend and history

<div class=warn markdown=1>**History or legend?** The story of the icon that kept going back to the bay is a folk tradition. The monastery itself calls it "the popular account" ([official history](https://panormitisymis.gr/index.php/el/istoria/i-istoria-tis-ieras-monis)). Documented history begins with a manuscript hymn to the Archangels written at the monastery in **1460**. It survived there until 1862. The silver cover of the great icon carries an inscription dated **19 March 1724**, and the monastery was completely rebuilt in **1783**.</div>

**The icon that kept returning.** This is the version the monastery tells today. A pious housewife, **Mario tou Protenios**, was digging on her land at Panormos ("the all-sheltered bay") when she found a small old icon of the Archangel Michael under a mastic bush. She took it secretly to her house in Symi town and lit a lamp in front of it. The next morning the icon was gone. When she went back to her field, she found it again under the bush. Three times she took it home, and three times it returned. Then the Archangel appeared to her in a dream, shining, and told her he wished to stay at Panormos for ever. She told her neighbours, and together they built a little chapel at the place. That chapel grew into the monastery. A popular version has fishermen, not a housewife, finding the icon, and it is often retold this way for visitors.

**What the history suggests.** The monastery's own historians think the story describes the **re-founding** of an older shrine, not the very first one. There was probably an early church of the Archangel on the bay, destroyed by the pirate raids that plagued the Aegean. Symi has several other monasteries dedicated to the angels, and an old tradition says there were nine, one for each order of angels. In the 1350s Symi's monks paid tribute to the **Knights of St John** in Rhodes. A wall painting with an inscription of **1760** survives from the older church. The monastery was in ruins when the hieromonk **Neophytos** arrived in **1775**. He made the rebuilding his life's work, and the present complex dates from **1783**.

- **The bell tower.** It is tall and **Baroque**, and stands above the harbour like a wedding cake. It is the first thing pilgrims see as the boat rounds the headland. It is Symi's most famous landmark. It is newer than it looks: building began in **1905** and ended in **1911**.
- **The katholikon (main church).** It stands in the middle of the courtyard. The church is dark inside, lit by many silver lamps, and covered with **frescoes**, painted in **1792** by the local painters Hieromonk Neophytos of Symi and Kyriakos Karakostis. The pebble mosaic floor of the courtyard (*chochlakia*), in black and white patterns, is a Dodecanese speciality.
- **The iconostasis.** This is the screen in front of the sanctuary. It is a large, finely **carved and gilded wooden** screen, completed in **1788** by master craftsman Drakos Taliadouros of Kos. The great icons of Christ and the Virgin on it are signed by the Chiot painter Stavrianos and probably date from 1727 ([Metropolis of Symi](https://imsymis.gr/index.php/el/ieres-mones/iera-moni-panormitou)).
- **The guest rooms.** The long ranges around the courtyard and harbour have rooms for pilgrims. You can stay overnight in simple rooms, as Greek pilgrims have done for centuries.
- **Chapels, the bakery and the refectory.** Smaller chapels to St John the Baptist, the Annunciation and other saints stand around the complex.

### Relics and sacred objects

**The great icon.** The miraculous icon of Michael the **Taxiarch** ("commander of the angelic hosts") is set into the right-hand wall of the katholikon, near the south door of the sanctuary, in a carved gilded frame. It is large, **2.20 × 1.30 m**, painted on wood. Michael stands full-length, holding a sword in his right hand. At his feet lies a dead man, and Michael holds his soul as a tiny child, showing his role as guide of souls to judgement. Nobody knows who painted it or when. The [monastery's own study](https://panormitisymis.gr/index.php/el/istoria/i-iera-eikona) suggests it may be the work of the Cretan icon-painter **Stelianos Genitis**, who worked on Symi after 1618.

- **The silver cover (1724).** It was made by the master silversmith **Ioannis Peloponnisios** and paid for by the whole community of Symi: "captains, priests and the commune". An inscription carved on the Archangel's sword gives the date and the names.
- **The face.** Over time, pilgrims stuck gold and silver coins to the Archangel's face, damaging it. Later, an unnamed painter painted a new face in oil on canvas, in a Western Renaissance style, and fitted it over the original. The face you see today is that later work.

**The votive offerings.** Panormitis is famous for its **votive offerings** (*tamata*), especially from sailors. The ecclesiastical museum is full of them: **silver model ships**, silver arms, legs and hearts, jewellery and money. The most unusual custom is the offering **sent by sea**: sailors anywhere in the Aegean put money or a written prayer in a bottle and throw it into the sea, trusting the Archangel to bring it to Panormitis. There are many stories of such bottles washing up in the bay. Islanders also say that if a promised offering is never delivered, the Archangel comes to collect it himself, which is why they jokingly call him "the Thief" ([Visit Symi](https://visitsymi.gr/en/activities/religious-tourism/)).

### Notable pilgrims through history

- **Sailors and sponge-divers** of the Dodecanese, for whom Michael Panormitis was the protector at sea. Captains would sound their horns as they passed the bay.
- **Hieromonk Neophytos**, who rebuilt the monastery in 1775–83.
- **The wartime abbot.** In 1944 the monastery's secret radio became a centre of Allied counter-intelligence. On **11 February 1944** the Italian occupiers executed the abbot, **Archimandrite Chrysanthos Maroulakis**, together with the monastery steward Michael Lambrou and the Greek army radio operator Floros Zouganelis. A large bronze memorial on the quay by the Symiot sculptor Kostas Valsamis shows the execution. The three men's remains rest there ([monastery notice](https://panormitisymis.gr/index.php/el/anakoinoseis/ta-nea-tis-ieras-monis/i-80i-epeteios-tis-thysias-tou-arxim-xrysanthou-maroulaki-igoumenou-tis-i-monis-panormitou-kai-ton-mixail-lamprou-kai-florou-zouganeli)).
- **Today's pilgrims.** Every year at the feasts of the Archangel, crowds from Rhodes, the other Dodecanese islands and the Greek diaspora fill the guest rooms and courtyard.

### The surrounding area

**The island.** **Symi** is a small, rocky island just off the Turkish coast, about 25 km north of Rhodes. Its main town is a horseshoe of **neoclassical houses** in ochre, pink and blue around the harbour of **Gialos**. A long flight of steps, the **Kali Strata**, climbs to the upper town, **Chorio**. The houses were built with wealth from shipbuilding and sponge fishing in the 19th century. Panormitis is in the south, at the end of a road across the hills and pine forest.

**Local food.** The famous dish is **Symi shrimp** (*Symiako garidaki*): tiny red shrimp fried whole and eaten shell and all. You will also find fresh fish, octopus, Dodecanese cheeses, honey and the island's spoon sweets. At Panormitis there is a taverna by the water and a bakery selling bread made by the monastery.

**Getting there.** Ferries and fast boats run from **Rhodes** to Symi town in about an hour. Some boats and day cruises go directly to Panormitis. A bus from Gialos to the monastery takes about 40 minutes over 20 km. A taxi or water taxi is the other option. Rhodes has a large international airport.

**Best time to visit.** May–June and September–October bring warm sea and fewer crowds. The big feast is **8 November** (the Synaxis of the Archangels), when pilgrims sleep in the courtyard. The feast of the **Miracle at Chonae** (6 September) also draws crowds. In July and August the day-cruise crowds peak in the late morning.

**Nearby sights.**
- **The Roukouniotis monastery**, another monastery of the Archangel, with a matching great icon.
- **The Monastery of Megalos Sotiris**.
- The coast at **Nimborios**.
- The beach of **Agios Georgios Dysalona**, under a huge cliff.
- The **Symi Museum** in Chorio.
- **Rhodes old town** (a UNESCO site).

## Visiting
- **From Rhodes:** a direct ferry to Panormitis (~1h25, a limited weekly schedule ⚠), or a ferry to Symi town and then the monastery bus or a taxi (~20 km, 40 minutes). Day cruises from Rhodes often stop here ([Visit Symi](https://visitsymi.gr/en/activities/religious-tourism/)).
- Simple guest rooms are available for pilgrims ⚠ (contact the monastery).
