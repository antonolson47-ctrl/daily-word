<div class=history markdown=1>
**History.** A jagged sea rock off the Iveragh Peninsula holds one of the best-preserved early Christian monasteries in Europe: drystone beehive huts and oratories built by monks who lived there for centuries in the early Middle Ages. It became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1996 ([UNESCO](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/757)).
</div>

<div class=legend markdown=1>
**Legend.** In one tradition, St Michael appeared here to help St Patrick drive the serpents and demons out of Ireland.
</div>


## The rock in depth

### Founding stories: legend and history

<div class=warn markdown=1>**History or legend?** Nobody knows when the monastery was founded or by whom. UNESCO says plainly that "the date of the foundation of the monastery on this island is not known", and it puts the dedication to St Michael somewhere between 950 and 1050. The stories of a 6th-century founder and of a drowned prince are tradition, not record.</div>

**The legend.** Local tradition names **St Fionán** as the founder, a 6th-century monk said to have rowed out from the Iveragh coast with a few companions. There are several saints called Fionán in Irish tradition, and none of them can be firmly tied to Skellig. An older story, from the medieval *Lebor Gabála Érenn* ("Book of Invasions"), says the rock was the grave of **Ir**, a son of Míl, drowned when the sons of Míl invaded Ireland. That is myth, but it shows that the rock was already a storied place in the Irish imagination.

**The history.** The Irish annals are the first real evidence. For **824** the *Annals of Inisfallen* record that "Scelec was plundered by heathens", and the *Annals of Ulster* add that "Étgal of Scelec was carried off by the heathens, and died shortly afterwards of hunger and thirst". The "heathens" were Viking raiders, and Étgal was probably the abbot or an anchorite of the rock. The raids did not end the community. The annals go on recording deaths of Skellig clergy into the 11th century, and the later dedication to Michael fits a wider Irish devotion to the Archangel in that period. Sometime around the 12th or 13th century the community moved to the mainland at **Ballinskelligs** ("the town of the Skelligs"), where an Augustinian priory took up its role. The rock went on as a place of penitential pilgrimage. (See the [UNESCO listing](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/757) and [Heritage Ireland](https://heritageireland.ie/places-to-visit/sceilg-mhichil/).)

### Architecture: how the monks built on a cliff

The island is two peaks of Devonian sandstone, 218 m and 185 m high, joined by a dip called **Christ's Saddle** (about 130 m up). Everything the monks built is **drystone**, laid without mortar, and almost all of it has survived more than a thousand years of Atlantic storms.

- **The beehive cells (clocháns).** Six cells survive in the main enclosure. Each is round outside and square-ish inside, made by **corbelling**: every course of stone slightly overlaps the one below until the walls meet at the top. The stones slope outward so rain runs off. The largest cell is about 4.5 m across inside, with cupboard-like niches and projecting pegs that may have held shelves or hangings. These were the monks' houses. Food and fire would have been shared.
- **The oratories.** Two small boat-shaped oratories (the Large and Small Oratory) served for prayer. A ruined medieval church, **St Michael's Church**, was built later with mortar, which marks it as a newer layer.
- **The cemetery and crosses.** In front of the church is a small graveyard with rough cross-slabs and standing crosses. Some are carved, many simply shaped.
- **Terraces and gardens.** The monks built terraces held up by retaining walls to make level ground for buildings and small garden plots. Cisterns caught rainwater.

**The South Peak Hermitage** is the most extreme part of the site. It is a set of tiny terraces, an oratory and walls perched just below the summit of the higher peak, reached by a climb that even modern experts do with ropes. It is closed to visitors. UNESCO calls it "the most spectacularly situated" part of all early medieval island monastic sites.

**The steps.** Three landing places link to the monastery by flights of stone steps. The one used today has **over 600** uneven, unrailed steps. They are the main reason the OPW sets strict rules for visits.

### Relics and sacred objects

There are no known relics on Skellig today, and no early manuscripts survive from the rock. The "sacred objects" here are the **cross-slabs** in the cemetery, the stone **altar** traces in the oratories, and the cells themselves. On the South Peak, later pilgrims followed a set of "stations": a climb through a rock chimney called the Needle's Eye, and a stone projecting over the sea called the **Spit** or Eagle's Nest. There they kissed a cross carved at the very end. Writers in the 18th century described this as a penance. Today it is forbidden and dangerous.

### Notable pilgrims and visitors

- **Étgal** (d. 824), the monk carried off by Viking raiders. He is the first named person tied to the rock in a contemporary record.
- **Medieval and early modern penitents** who made the Good Friday and summer pilgrimages from the mainland, following the "stations" to the top.
- **The lighthouse keepers and their families**, who lived on the island from the 1820s until the lights were automated in 1987. Their road and walls are the only major later changes on the island.
- **Film crews.** In 2014–15 the island was a filming location for the *Star Wars* sequel films. This raised visitor demand sharply and led to tighter limits on landings.

### The surrounding area

**The landscape.** The rock rises out of the Atlantic about 12 km off the Iveragh Peninsula in County Kerry. Next to it is **Little Skellig**, white with one of the world's largest **northern gannet** colonies (tens of thousands of pairs). It cannot be landed on, but boats circle it. Skellig Michael itself has puffins (roughly May to early August), storm petrels, Manx shearwaters, fulmars and kittiwakes.

**The town.** Most boats leave from **Portmagee**, a one-street harbour village facing **Valentia Island** across a narrow channel and a bridge. It has pubs with traditional music and a small fishing fleet. Ballinskelligs, Waterville and Caherdaniel are nearby.

**Local food.** Order seafood chowder and brown soda bread, crab claws, and fresh hake or mackerel off the Portmagee boats. Kerry lamb is another local dish. On Valentia, the farmhouse dairy makes ice cream from its own herd.

**Getting there.** Fly into Kerry Airport (Farranfore), or take the train to Killarney. From there it is about 1.5–2 hours' drive to Portmagee. Buses on the Ring of Kerry are limited, so a car or tour is the easiest option. Only licensed boats may land passengers, in a season the OPW sets each year (mid-May to September is typical, but always subject to weather). The crossing takes about 45–60 minutes and can be rough. Book early and expect cancellations. Eco-tours that circle the islands without landing run more often.

**Best time to visit.** Late May to July is best for calmer seas, long light and puffins. Even in summer, plan a spare day for the weather.

**Nearby sights.**
- **The Skellig Experience** visitor centre on Valentia, useful if your landing is cancelled.
- The **Valentia tetrapod trackway**, footprints about 350–385 million years old.
- **Geokaun Mountain** views.
- The ruined **Ballinskelligs priory** and castle on the shore.
- The **Kerry Cliffs** near Portmagee.
- The **Skellig Ring** drive.
- **Derrynane** House and beach.
- The **Ring of Kerry** itself.

## Visiting
- **Landing only by licensed boat**, mostly from Portmagee, in a short summer season run by the Office of Public Works. In 2026 the season opened 9 May and closed early, on **21 September**, because of the weather ([OPW](https://www.gov.ie/en/office-of-public-works/press-releases/closure-of-visitor-season-at-sceilg-mhich%C3%ADl/)).
- Book months ahead. Landings are often cancelled by swell.
- The climb is hundreds of steep, uneven stone steps with no handrails. Not for anyone unsteady on their feet.
- Can't land? "Eco tours" circle the island without landing, and the Skellig Experience on Valentia Island tells the story.
