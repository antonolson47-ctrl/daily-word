Roman Corinth was a boom town: refounded by Julius Caesar in 44 BC, capital of the province of Achaia, controlling the isthmus between two seas and two harbours. Paul arrived from Athens around AD 50, worked as a tentmaker with **Priscilla and Aquila**, and stayed **a year and six months** (Acts 18:11), longer than anywhere except Ephesus. His letters to this lively, quarrelsome church give us some of the most famous words in the Bible.

## Good to know
- **Getting there:** Ancient Corinth (*Archaia Korinthos*) is 80 km west of Athens, about an hour by car; trains (Proastiakos) and buses run to the modern city 7 km away.
- **The site and museum** (paid, one ticket) take 2–3 hours. **Acrocorinth**, the fortress on the mountain above, is free and worth the drive for the view.
- **The Corinth Canal** (1893) is on the way from Athens.
- **Cenchreae** (Kechries), the eastern harbour, has partly submerged ruins you can see from the shore.

## Deep Dive

### What the Bible says
Paul preached first in the synagogue, then next door in the house of **Titius Justus**; **Crispus**, the synagogue ruler, believed with his household. One night the Lord told Paul in a vision, "Do not be afraid, but go on speaking and do not be silent, for I am with you" (Acts 18:9–10). When the Jews brought Paul before the proconsul **Gallio** at the **bema** (tribunal), he threw the case out as a dispute about words and names (Acts 18:12–17).

To this church, after he left, Paul wrote about divisions, food offered to idols, the Lord's Supper, spiritual gifts and the resurrection, and the hymn to love: "So now faith, hope, and love abide, these three; but the greatest of these is love" (1 Corinthians 13:13, ESV). He also wrote **Romans** from Corinth, around AD 57, commending "our sister **Phoebe**, a servant of the church at Cenchreae" (Romans 16:1).

### Archaeology and what to see
<div class=history markdown=1>
**History.** Three finds tie Corinth to the New Testament:
- **The Gallio inscription**, found not at Corinth but at **Delphi**: a letter of Emperor Claudius naming Gallio as proconsul, datable to AD 51–52. It is the single most important anchor for Paul's chronology.
- **The bema**: a raised speakers' platform in the middle of the forum, faced with marble, identified with the tribunal of Acts 18:12. A Byzantine church was later built on it.
- **The Erastus inscription**: a paving stone near the theatre reading that **Erastus**, in return for his aedileship, laid the pavement at his own expense. Paul sends greetings from "Erastus, the city treasurer" (Romans 16:23). Whether they are the same man is debated, but the name and office fit.
</div>

- **The Temple of Apollo** (6th century BC), seven of whose Doric columns still stand, already ancient in Paul's day.
- **The Lechaion Road**, the marble-paved street to the northern harbour, and the **Peirene fountain**.
- **The meat market** (*macellum*) area, relevant to Paul's advice to "eat whatever is sold in the meat market" (1 Corinthians 10:25).
- **The diolkos**, the stone trackway on which ships were dragged across the isthmus.
- **Isthmia**, 10 km east, where the Isthmian Games were held, background to Paul's images of runners and the "imperishable" wreath (1 Corinthians 9:24–27).
