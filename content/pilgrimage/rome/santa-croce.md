The name is not a mistake. This church was meant to be **a piece of Jerusalem in Rome**: built in the palace of **St Helena**, Constantine's mother, to hold the relics of Christ's Passion she brought back from the Holy Land, its floor was laid, so the story goes, on earth from Calvary.

<div class=warn markdown=1>
**Relics chapel closed.** As of October 2026 the basilica's official site says the **Chapel of the Relics** is closed for urgent building works by order of the Vicariate of Rome and the Ministry of Culture, with the reopening date to be announced on the site. The basilica is open. [Check before you go](https://www.santacroceroma.it/en/opening-hours.html).
</div>

## Good to know
- **Hours (official site):** basilica 7:30–19:00; museum 10:00–12:00 and 16:00–17:30, entered from the sacristy. Entry is free.
- **The relics chapel**, when open, is free; it's closed to visits during Masses.
- **Getting there:** tram 3 or 8, or a 10-minute walk from Metro A *San Giovanni*.

## Deep Dive

### History
<div class=history markdown=1>
**History.** In the early 4th century **Helena** lived in the **Sessorian Palace**, an imperial residence on this spot. Part of a great hall of the palace was turned into a church, the *Basilica Heleniana* or *Sessoriana*, to keep the relics of the Cross. **Lucius II** rebuilt it in **1144**, with the Romanesque bell tower that survives. **Benedict XIV** gave it its curving Baroque façade and oval atrium in **1743** (architects **Domenico Gregorini** and **Pietro Passalacqua**). The basilica sits right against the Aurelian Walls and the remains of the **Castrense amphitheatre**.
</div>

### The relics of the Passion
<div class=legend markdown=1>
**Tradition.** Around **326–328**, Helena travelled to the Holy Land, and later accounts (from the end of the 4th century, such as Ambrose and Rufinus) tell how she found the **True Cross** on Golgotha. She left part in Jerusalem, sent part to Constantinople, and brought part to Rome.
</div>

The present chapel (1930s), reached by a corridor with the Stations of the Cross, displays in reliquaries:
- **three fragments of the True Cross**;
- **a nail** of the Crucifixion;
- **two thorns** from the Crown of Thorns;
- **the *Titulus Crucis***, a fragment of a wooden board with the charge against Jesus in Hebrew, Greek and Latin, written right to left: *I. NAZARINVS RE[X IVDAEORVM]*. It was found walled up in the church in **1492**. Radiocarbon dating published in 2002 placed the wood between the 10th and 12th centuries, while some scholars argue from the lettering for an older original; the debate continues;
- a large fragment of **the cross of the Good Thief**;
- **a finger bone of St Thomas**, venerated as the finger he put in the risen Christ's wounds (John 20);
- fragments from the **Grotto of the Nativity**, the **Holy Sepulchre** and the **pillar of the Scourging**.

### Altars, art and architecture
- **The Chapel of St Helena**, below the apse, whose floor, by tradition, was laid over earth from Calvary. Women were long admitted only once a year. Its vault mosaic, designed around 1484 and attributed to Melozzo da Forlì's circle, was redone in the 16th century, and its statue of Helena is an ancient Roman statue (of Juno or an empress) reworked with a cross.
- **The apse fresco *Legend of the True Cross*** (about 1492), attributed to **Antoniazzo Romano** and workshop: Helena finding the Cross, and Heraclius bringing it back to Jerusalem.
- **The Cosmatesque floor** and the **tomb of Venerable Antonietta Meo** ("Nennolina," 1930–1937), a Roman child whose letters to Jesus written during her illness are much loved.
