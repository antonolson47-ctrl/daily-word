## Setting the scene

Still on the hillside above the Sea of Galilee near Capernaum, around AD 28–30, at the close of the prayer Jesus is teaching in the Sermon on the Mount. His listeners knew what a time of trial looked like. When they were children or their parents were young, after Herod the Great died in 4 BC, a revolt broke out at Sepphoris, just a few miles from Nazareth. The Roman governor Varus burned the city and, according to the historian Josephus, crucified about two thousand rebels. Around AD 6, Judas the Galilean led an uprising over Roman taxes, and the pull toward armed rebellion never fully went away. Many Jews also expected a great final testing before God set the world right. Into that world Jesus gives a last request: do not bring us into the test, and pull us out of the grip of evil. Months later, in the garden of Gethsemane, he would tell his sleepy disciples the same thing: "Pray that you may not enter into temptation" (Matthew 26:41). He speaks as a man in the flesh to people who did not yet know who was standing in front of them.

## Key words

- **eisenenkēs → inducas → lead (bring) into.** Eispherō means "carry in, bring into." It is the same verb used when friends carry a paralyzed man into a house to reach Jesus (Luke 5:18–19). Jerome's inducas means "lead in, bring in." The request is not that God would push anyone toward sin; James 1:13 says plainly that God tempts no one. It is a plea not to be brought into a situation that would break us.
- **peirasmon → tentationem → temptation.** Peirasmos covers both "temptation" to do wrong and "testing" or "trial" of faith. It is the word family used when the devil tempts Jesus in the wilderness (Matthew 4:1). Latin tentatio has the same double sense of a trial and a temptation, and so did older English "temptation."
- **rhysai → libera → deliver.** Rhyomai means "rescue, drag out of danger," the word for pulling someone from a fire or a flood. Jerome's libera means "set free."
- **tou ponērou → a malo → evil / the evil one.** The Greek genitive can be neuter ("evil") or masculine ("the evil one"), and Latin malo is just as ambiguous. The KJV and ESV text say "evil," the ESV footnote and many modern versions say "the evil one," and Eastern Christian tradition has long read it as the devil.

## Alignment

Close in the prayer itself, with one big textual difference. Greek, Latin and English carry the same three ideas: bring in, testing, and rescue from evil, and all three keep the evil/evil-one ambiguity. The difference is the ending. The KJV adds the doxology "For thine is the kingdom, and the power, and the glory, for ever. Amen," which comes from later Greek manuscripts and the Textus Receptus. The SBLGNT and ESV stop at "evil." The Vulgate has no doxology but closes with "Amen."

## The story

The famous ending "For thine is the kingdom" is missing from the oldest manuscripts of Matthew, including Codex Sinaiticus and Codex Vaticanus from the 300s, and from early Latin writers like Tertullian and Cyprian. Yet a version of it appears very early in worship: the Didache, a church manual from around AD 100, ends the Lord's Prayer with "for thine is the power and the glory forever." So it seems the church sang it as a response long before scribes wrote it into Matthew. Catholics still say it at Mass, just after the priest's prayer that follows the Our Father, while Protestants say it as part of the prayer. And the line before it is still debated. After Pope Francis said in 2017 that a father does not lead his children into temptation, French Catholics changed their wording to "do not let us enter into temptation" (2017), and Italian Catholics changed theirs to "do not abandon us to temptation" (in Mass from 2020). English stayed with "lead us not."
