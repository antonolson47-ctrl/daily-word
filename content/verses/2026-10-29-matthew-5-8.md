## Setting the scene

Still the hillside above the Sea of Galilee near Capernaum, around AD 28. Jesus has just blessed the merciful, and now he goes beneath behavior to its source: the heart. His listeners lived by careful purity rules. Hands were washed before meals, vessels were scrubbed, and anyone going up to the Temple in Jerusalem had to be ritually clean first. Pilgrims climbing toward the Temple sang Psalm 24: "Who shall ascend into the hill of the LORD? He that hath clean hands, and a pure heart." Into that world Jesus says the truly blessed are the clean on the inside, and he promises them the greatest reward of all: they will see God.

## Key words

- **makarioi → Beati → Blessed.** The sixth verbless declaration in a row: "Blessed, the pure in heart." It announces how God sees them.
- **katharoi → mundo → pure.** Katharos means clean: washed, unmixed, free of dirt or stain. It is related to English "catharsis," a cleansing. Latin mundus as an adjective also means clean or neat (a different word from mundus, "world"); Jerome writes mundo corde, "clean of heart."
- **tē kardia → corde → in heart.** In Hebrew and Greek thought the heart is not mainly the feelings but the center of the whole person: mind, will, and desire. Greek kardia gives "cardiac"; Latin cor gives "cordial" and "core."
- **autoi → ipsi → they.** Emphatic: they themselves will see him.
- **ton Theon opsontai → Deum videbunt → shall see God.** Opsontai is the future of horaō, "to see," and gives English "optic." The promise is staggering in a Bible where Moses was told, "no man shall see me, and live" (Exodus 33:20).

## The story

Jesus keeps coming back to the heart in Matthew. When critics complained that his disciples ate without washing their hands, he answered that what defiles a person comes "out of the heart" (Matthew 15:18-20). He scolded those who "make clean the outside of the cup" but leave the inside full (Matthew 23:25-26). The rest of the New Testament carries the promise forward: "without holiness no man shall see the Lord" (Hebrews 12:14), "we shall see him as he is" (1 John 3:2), and in the last chapter of the Bible, "they shall see his face" (Revelation 22:4).

## Dig Deeper: Roots in Hebrew & Jewish Sources

This Beatitude is built almost word for word from the Psalms Jesus' listeners sang. Psalm 24:3-4 asks who may climb the Lord's hill and stand in his holy place, and answers, "He that hath clean hands, and a pure heart." The Hebrew there is bar levav, "pure of heart," and the Greek Old Testament (the Septuagint, Psalm 23:4 in its numbering) translates it katharos tē kardia, the very phrase Matthew uses. Psalm 51 adds David's prayer, "Create in me a clean heart, O God" (lev tahor), and Psalm 17:15 hopes, "I will behold thy face in righteousness." Against Exodus 33:20, where God tells Moses no one can see his face and live, Jesus' promise that the pure in heart "shall see God" lands with real force. These links are background study tools, not Scripture, and including them is not an endorsement of any later theology. Keep the dates straight: Psalms and Exodus are Hebrew Bible (Tanakh), written centuries before Christ, and the Septuagint is a Jewish Greek translation from before Jesus' time. No Dead Sea Scroll or later rabbinic text offers a parallel strong enough to list here, so none is included.
