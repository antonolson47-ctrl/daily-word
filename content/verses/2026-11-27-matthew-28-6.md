## Setting the scene

It is the first day of the week, just as dawn begins to break over Jerusalem. Mary Magdalene and "the other Mary" are walking to the tomb in Joseph of Arimathea's garden (Matthew 28:1), the same women who had watched from a distance as Jesus died and had sat over against the sepulchre as he was buried (27:55-61). They expect a sealed stone and a Roman watch, because the chief priests had remembered something the disciples had forgotten: "that deceiver said, while he was yet alive, After three days I will rise again" (27:63). So the stone was sealed and soldiers were posted. But heaven does not wait for permission. "Behold, there was a great earthquake: for the angel of the Lord descended from heaven, and came and rolled back the stone from the door, and sat upon it" (28:2). His face is like lightning, his clothing white as snow, and the hardened guards shake and become "as dead men" (28:3-4). Then the angel turns to the trembling women with the gentlest words of the morning: "Fear not ye: for I know that ye seek Jesus, which was crucified" (28:5). And then come the words that changed the world. He is not here. He is risen, just as he said. Come and see. The stone was not rolled away to let Jesus out; it was rolled away to let the witnesses in.

## Key words

- **ouk estin hōde → non est hic → he is not here.** Four short words that no grave robber, no guard and no grieving friend could have expected. The tomb was not lost, not mistaken, not looted: it was simply empty of the One it had held. Every Easter sermon since begins with this absence.
- **ēgerthē → surrexit → he is risen.** Ēgerthē comes from egeirō, "to wake, to raise up" (Strong's G1453), the word used for waking a sleeper. In Greek it is a passive form, literally "he was raised," and many readers have long heard in it the Father's mighty hand: God raised him up (Acts 2:24; Romans 6:4). Jerome chose the active surrexit, "he rose," which honors the other side of the same truth, that Jesus has power to take up his life again (John 10:18). The Father raised the Son; the Son rose. The church sings both.
- **kathōs eipen → sicut dixit → as he said.** The angel points the women back to Jesus' own words. He had told them again and again: after Peter's confession (16:21), after the Transfiguration (17:9, 23), on the road up to Jerusalem (20:19), and at the Last Supper, "after I am risen again, I will go before you into Galilee" (26:32). The empty tomb is a promise kept, and so it is a pledge that every other promise he made will be kept too.
- **deute idete → venite, et videte → come, see.** Deute is a warm summons, "come here!", the very word Jesus used when he called fishermen, "Come ye after me" (4:19), and when he invited the weary, "Come unto me" (11:28). The angel does not ask the women to believe on hearsay. He invites them to look. Christian faith begins with an invitation to see the evidence.
- **ekeito → positus erat → where he lay.** Ekeito, from keimai, "to lie, to be laid" (Strong's G2749), is the imperfect tense, "where he was lying." Jerome renders it positus erat, "where he had been placed," a quiet echo of Joseph laying the body in his own new tomb (27:59-60). The place is real, the grave clothes real, the stone real, and now the space is empty.

## Alignment

Very close. Every Greek text on Bible Hub agrees on the heart of the verse, "ouk estin hōde; ēgerthē gar kathōs eipen; deute idete ton topon hopou ekeito." The one meaningful difference is at the end. Nestle 1904, Westcott–Hort, NA27/UBS4 and Tischendorf stop at ἔκειτο, "where he lay," which is the ESV's reading (with a footnote, "Some manuscripts the Lord"). The RP Byzantine Majority Text, the Greek Orthodox text and both Textus Receptus editions add ὁ κύριος, "the Lord," which the KJV follows: "where the Lord lay." The Clementine Vulgate also has Dominus: "ubi positus erat Dominus." Small punctuation differences aside (Westcott–Hort prints a comma after ὧδε), the wording is identical. The Vulgate's surrexit ("he rose") renders the Greek passive ēgerthē ("he was raised") with an active verb, and the Aramaic Peshitta likewise says qām leh, "he has risen." The KJV's "is risen" and the ESV's "has risen" both carry the sense well.

## The story

For many centuries the church has acted out this very verse at Easter. In the tenth century, English monasteries following the Regularis Concordia (about AD 970) staged a short sung dialogue during the night office (Matins) of Easter morning. A monk vested in white sat at a model of the tomb, like the angel on the stone, while three others came slowly toward him "as if seeking something," like the women with their spices. He sang, "Quem quaeritis in sepulchro, o Christicolae?" "Whom do you seek in the tomb, O followers of Christ?" They answered, "Jesum Nazarenum crucifixum, o caelicolae," "Jesus of Nazareth, who was crucified, O heavenly one." And he replied with the words of our verse: "Non est hic; surrexit sicut praedixerat," "He is not here; he is risen as he foretold." Then he lifted the veil to show the place empty, with only the linen cloths left behind, and the whole church burst into the Te Deum. This little scene, the Quem quaeritis, is often called the seed of Western drama. And the place itself was never forgotten: when Constantine's builders cleared away a pagan temple in Jerusalem around AD 326, Eusebius writes that "contrary to all expectation, the venerable and hallowed monument of our Saviour's resurrection was discovered" (Life of Constantine 3.28). Pilgrims still kneel there today in the Church of the Holy Sepulchre, at the spot Christians have honored for seventeen centuries as "the place where the Lord lay."

## Dig Deeper: Roots in Hebrew & Jewish Sources

When the angel said "He is risen," the women did not hear a foreign idea. The hope that God would one day raise the dead was already rooted in the Hebrew Scriptures: "many of them that sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake" (Daniel 12:2), "thy dead men shall live" (Isaiah 26:19), "thou wilt not leave my soul in hell" (Psalm 16:10), and "on the third day he will raise us up" (Hosea 6:2, ESV). Among the Dead Sea Scrolls, copied before the time of Christ, a fragment called 4Q521 looks forward to a day when the Lord "will heal the wounded, and revive the dead and bring good news to the poor" (as Geza Vermes translates it), strikingly close to Jesus' own answer to John the Baptist (Matthew 11:5). Much later, around AD 200, the Mishnah treats belief in the resurrection of the dead as a core teaching, showing how deep this hope ran in Jewish life. What was new on Easter morning was not the hope of resurrection but that it had begun, in one Man, ahead of the last day. These are background study, not Scripture, and not an endorsement of any later theology.
