## Setting the scene

Still the hillside above the Sea of Galilee near Capernaum, around AD 28. Jesus has blessed the pure in heart, and now he turns from the inner life to what it produces among people. His listeners lived under the Pax Romana, a "peace" kept by Roman soldiers, tax collectors and crosses along the roads. Some in Galilee dreamed of winning freedom by the sword. Into that world Jesus blesses neither the conquerors nor the rebels, but the ones who make peace, and he gives them a family name: children of God.

## Key words

- **makarioi → Beati → Blessed.** The seventh verbless declaration in a row: "Blessed, the peacemakers." It announces how God sees them.
- **eirēnopoioi → pacifici → peacemakers.** A compound of eirēnē, "peace," and poieō, "to make, to do." This noun appears only here in the New Testament. Jerome's pacifici is the same build in Latin (pax plus facio, "peace-making") and gives English "pacific" and "pacify." Not peace-lovers or peace-keepers, but people who actively make peace.
- **autoi → ipsi (not translated in Latin here) → they.** Emphatic in Greek: they themselves. Jerome leaves it unspoken.
- **huioi Theou → filii Dei → sons of God / children of God.** Greek huioi and Latin filii mean "sons," the word for heirs who share a father's character. The KJV says "children," the ESV keeps "sons." Both are faithful; the point is family likeness, and it includes daughters too.
- **klēthēsontai → vocabuntur → shall be called.** Future passive: God himself will give them the name. Latin vocare gives "vocation."

## Alignment

Very close. The Greek editions on Bible Hub (Nestle 1904, Westcott–Hort, the Byzantine Majority Text and both Textus Receptus editions) agree word for word; Tischendorf's edition alone omits autoi, "they." The Vulgate follows the Greek closely but drops the emphatic "they." The KJV and ESV differ only in "children" versus "sons" of God.

## The story

Paul uses the verb form of this word for what Christ did on the cross: God was pleased "having made peace through the blood of his cross" (Colossians 1:20, eirēnopoiēsas). Ephesians 2:14 says of Christ, "he is our peace," and James 3:18 picks up the Beatitude almost directly: "the fruit of righteousness is sown in peace of them that make peace." So the peacemakers are called God's children because they are doing what the Son of God came to do.

## Dig Deeper: Roots in Hebrew & Jewish Sources

Peace in Jesus' world meant the Hebrew word shalom: not just the absence of fighting but wholeness, things set right between people and with God. The Psalms told his listeners to "seek peace, and pursue it" (Psalm 34:14), so making peace was something you go after, not something you wait for. The title he promises also has Hebrew roots: Hosea foresaw a day when Israel would be called "the sons of the living God" (Hosea 1:10, verse 2:1 in Hebrew Bibles). Later Jewish teaching kept the same priority. The Mishnah, written down around AD 200, remembers the sage Hillel (traditionally said to have died around AD 10) saying, "be of the disciples of Aaron, loving peace and pursuing peace," and it lists "bringing peace between a person and his fellow" among deeds whose reward lasts into the world to come (Mishnah Peah 1:1). These links are background study tools, not Scripture, and including them is not an endorsement of any later theology. Keep the dates straight: Psalms and Hosea are Hebrew Bible (Tanakh), written centuries before Christ, while the Mishnah was compiled about 170 years after Jesus, though it preserves sayings from teachers of his era. No Dead Sea Scroll offers a parallel strong enough to list here, so none is included.
