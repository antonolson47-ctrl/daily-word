## Setting the scene

Caesarea Philippi, about 25 miles north of the Sea of Galilee, at the foot of Mount Hermon, around AD 29. It is the farthest north Jesus is recorded taking his disciples, and it is pagan ground. A huge wall of rock rises over a spring that feeds the Jordan River, and in the cliff face is a dark cave the locals treated as a gateway to the underworld. Shrines to the Greek god Pan were cut into the rock, Herod the Great had built a white marble temple to Caesar Augustus nearby, and Herod's son Philip renamed the town Caesarea in honor of the emperor. Here, surrounded by rival gods and emperors, Jesus asks, "Who do people say that the Son of Man is?" Then, more pointedly: "But who do you say that I am?" Simon answers, "You are the Christ, the Son of the living God" (16:16). Jesus calls him blessed, because this was revealed to him by the Father, not by flesh and blood. Then he speaks to Simon in front of that wall of stone.

## Key words

- **Petros → Petrus → Peter.** A new name, given by Jesus. John 1:42 preserves the Aramaic Jesus most likely spoke: Kēphas (Cephas), from kêphāʾ, "rock."
- **petra → petram → rock.** Petra is the ordinary Greek word for bedrock or a great mass of rock, the same word as the wise man's house built "upon a rock" (Matthew 7:24). Petra is feminine, so a man's name needed the masculine form Petros. In Aramaic there would have been no gap at all: kêphāʾ ... kêphāʾ.
- **oikodomēsō → ædificabo → I will build.** Future tense, and Jesus says "my" church. It is his building project, not Peter's.
- **ekklēsian → Ecclesiam → church.** This is the first time ekklēsia appears in the Gospels; it shows up again only in Matthew 18:17. In the Greek Old Testament it often stands for the Hebrew qāhāl, the assembly of Israel gathered before God at Sinai (Deuteronomy 4:10; 9:10). Latin simply borrowed the Greek word, which is why the church is église in French, iglesia in Spanish and chiesa in Italian.
- **pylai Hadou → portæ inferi → the gates of hell / Hades.** Hades is the realm of the dead, the Greek name for the Hebrew Sheol, rather than Gehenna, the place of final punishment. Both the KJV and the ESV say "hell" here; many other translations say "Hades." Latin inferi means "those below," the underworld. Standing near a cave people called a gate to the underworld, the image would have landed hard.
- **katischysousin → prævalebunt → prevail.** To overpower or be too strong for. Death itself will not overcome what Christ builds.

## Alignment

Very close, almost word for word in every version. The one real choice is "hell" (KJV, ESV) versus "Hades" for pylai Hadou. The wordplay Petros/petra survives in Latin (Petrus/petram) but disappears in English, where "Peter" and "rock" sound nothing alike.

## The story

French keeps the pun perfectly: "tu es Pierre, et sur cette pierre je bâtirai mon Église," because pierre means both Peter and stone. Jerome's Latin of this verse and the next runs around the inside of the dome of St Peter's Basilica in Rome in letters taller than a person: TU ES PETRUS ET SUPER HANC PETRAM AEDIFICABO ECCLESIAM MEAM. Christians have read "this rock" in more than one way since the early centuries. Catholic teaching sees Peter himself as the rock, many Protestant readers see Peter's confession of faith, and Augustine at times read the rock as Christ. Every reading agrees on the center of the scene: Jesus is the Christ, the Son of the living God, and he is the one who builds.

## Dig Deeper: Roots in Hebrew & Jewish Sources

Matthew wrote in Greek, but this scene is full of Hebrew and Aramaic roots. Jesus most likely gave Simon the Aramaic name Kêphāʾ, "rock," which John 1:42 preserves as Cephas, so in his own language the wordplay was exact. The image of a rock as a foundation for God's people is old: Isaiah tells Israel to "look to the rock from which you were hewn," meaning Abraham, from whom God built a whole nation (Isaiah 51:1–2). "Church," ekklēsia, is the word the Greek Old Testament uses for the qāhāl, the assembly of Israel gathered before God. And "the gates of Sheol" is a Hebrew way of speaking about death itself (Isaiah 38:10, which the Greek Old Testament renders with the very words pylai Hadou). Among the Dead Sea Scrolls, the Thanksgiving Hymns (Hodayot) from Qumran picture God's people as a fortified city founded on rock, holding firm while the gates of death and Sheol open against it; scholars often point to these hymns as a close Jewish parallel to Jesus' words. A much later rabbinic collection, the Yalkut Shimoni (compiled around the 13th century from older midrash), even pictures God finding Abraham as a petra, a rock, on which to build the world. These sources are background study, not Scripture, and including them is not an endorsement of any later theology. Keep the dates straight: Isaiah is Hebrew Bible (Tanakh); the Greek Old Testament (Septuagint) is a Jewish translation made before Christ; the Thanksgiving Hymns were composed in the 2nd–1st century BC; the Mishnah (about AD 200), Talmud (about AD 400–600) and medieval midrash collections come later and are not sources Matthew used.

**A quick timeline, so the sources don't blur together**

| Source | Date | What it is |
|---|---|---|
