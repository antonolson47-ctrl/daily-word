## Setting the scene

Jesus is still teaching on the mountain in Galilee, and the Sermon on the Mount is nearing its close. He has just promised, "Ask, and it shall be given you," and reminded his hearers that if earthly fathers give good gifts to their children, "how much more shall your Father which is in heaven give good things to them that ask him?" (7:7-11). Then comes a small but mighty word: "Therefore." Because the Father is so generous to us, we are to be generous to others. In one sentence Jesus gathers up everything he has said since 5:17, where he declared, "Think not that I am come to destroy the law, or the prophets." Now he closes the circle: treating others the way you long to be treated "is the law and the prophets." Right after this, he turns to the narrow gate (7:13-14). Luke records the same saying in the Sermon on the Plain, beside "love your enemies" (Luke 6:31).

## Key words

- **oun → ergo → therefore.** The hinge of the verse. The Golden Rule is not a free-floating moral slogan; it flows out of the Father's goodness just described in 7:7-11. We give because we have been given to.
- **panta hosa ean → omnia quæcumque → all things whatsoever.** Nothing is left out. Jesus does not limit the rule to friends, family or fellow believers. Every kind of good you would want done for you is in scope.
- **thelēte → vultis → ye would.** Thelō means to wish, want, or will. Jesus starts with the desires of your own heart: think honestly about what you long for from others (patience, mercy, kindness, a second chance), and then give exactly that.
- **poieite → facite → do.** A present imperative, so an ongoing habit: "keep on doing." Notice the rule is active. It is not only "don't harm," but "go and do the good."
- **ho nomos kai hoi prophētai → lex et prophetæ → the law and the prophets.** Jewish shorthand for the whole Hebrew Scriptures. Jesus uses the same phrase in 5:17 and again in 22:40, where the two great commandments, love God and love your neighbour, are what "all the law and the prophets" hang on.

## Alignment

Very close, with one small Latin compression. Every Greek text on Bible Hub (Nestle 1904, Westcott–Hort with NA27/UBS4, the Byzantine Majority Text, the Greek Orthodox text, Tischendorf and both Textus Receptus editions) has the same words; the only differences are spelling choices like ean or an, and houtōs or houtō, which do not change the meaning. The Vulgate follows the Greek closely but renders houtōs kai hymeis ("even so you also") simply as et vos ("you also"), dropping the "so." The KJV keeps it ("do ye even so"), while the ESV ("do also to them") and the Berean ("do to others as you would have them do to you") smooth it out. The Greek anthrōpoi ("men, people") becomes "men" in the KJV and "others" in the ESV.

## The story

The name "Golden Rule" is not in the Bible. English preachers and writers began calling this verse "the golden rule" in the 1600s, meaning a rule more precious than gold, the one that sums up all the others. But Christians had treasured it long before it had a nickname. The Didache, a church manual from around the late first or early second century, opens its "Way of Life" with the two great commandments and then the rule in its "do not" form: whatever you would not want done to you, do not do to another. Jesus' own wording is famous for going further. Many before him taught "don't do what you hate"; Jesus said "do," turning a fence against harm into an open road of active love.

## Dig Deeper: Roots in Hebrew & Jewish Sources

When Jesus says this "is the law and the prophets," he is pointing his Jewish hearers straight back to their own Scriptures. The heart of it is Leviticus 19:18, "thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself: I am the LORD" (Hebrew ve'ahavta lere'acha kamocha, וְאָהַבְתָּ לְרֵעֲךָ כָּמוֹךָ), which Jesus later names as the second great commandment (Matthew 22:39). Other Jewish writers had also summed up the Law in a single sentence, usually in a negative form. The Book of Tobit, written a couple of centuries before Christ and kept in the Apocrypha (fragments of Tobit in Aramaic and Hebrew were found among the Dead Sea Scrolls), has the elder Tobit tell his son, "Do that to no man which thou hatest" (Tobit 4:15). The best-known parallel is the story of Hillel, a great sage who lived shortly before and around the time of Jesus' birth. A Gentile asked to be taught the whole Torah while standing on one foot, and Hillel answered in Aramaic: "That which is hateful to you do not do to another; that is the entire Torah, and the rest is its interpretation. Go study." That story is recorded in the Babylonian Talmud, which was compiled centuries after Christ (around AD 500), so it is a parallel and a window into Jewish teaching of the period, not a source the Gospel copied. Set side by side, they show how fresh Jesus' wording is: he states the rule positively, as something to do, and grounds it in the generosity of the Father. Dig Deeper is background study, not new Scripture, and not an endorsement of any later theology.

**A quick timeline, so the sources don't blur together**

|---|---|---|
