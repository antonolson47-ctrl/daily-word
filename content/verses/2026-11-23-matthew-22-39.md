## Setting the scene

The Temple courts are still crackling with tension. It is the last week before the cross, and Jesus has just answered the lawyer's test question with the greatest words in Israel's prayer book: "Thou shalt love the Lord thy God with all thy heart, and with all thy soul, and with all thy mind. This is the first and great commandment" (22:37-38). The lawyer asked for one commandment. Jesus could have stopped there, and no one in the crowd could have faulted him. But he does not stop. In the same breath he reaches for a second verse, tucked into the middle of Leviticus 19 among laws about harvest gleanings, honest scales and fair wages, and he lifts it up beside the first: "And the second is like unto it." In a single moment, two commandments that lay far apart in the Torah are joined like the two beams of a cross, one reaching up to God and one reaching out to the person standing next to you. Then comes the line that silences the debate: "On these two commandments hang all the law and the prophets" (22:40). Within days, the One who spoke these words would live them out completely, loving the Father to the end and laying down his life for his neighbours, even the ones who nailed him there.

## Key words

- **deutera → secundum → the second.** Second in order, not second-rate. Jesus was asked for one commandment and gave two, because for him they cannot be pulled apart. As St. John would later write, "he that loveth not his brother whom he hath seen, how can he love God whom he hath not seen?" (1 John 4:20).
- **homoia autē → simile est huic → is like unto it.** Homoios means "like, of the same kind, resembling." The second commandment is cut from the same cloth as the first. Love of neighbour is not a separate project from love of God; it is the same love, flowing outward.
- **agapēseis → diliges → thou shalt love.** The very same verb, in the very same future-tense covenant form, as in verse 37. Word for word, the Greek is identical to the ancient Greek Septuagint of Leviticus 19:18. Jerome uses the same diliges in both verses, so the Latin reader hears the echo too.
- **ton plēsion → proximum → thy neighbour.** Plēsion literally means "the one near," "the one close by." Jerome's proximus means exactly the same, "the nearest," the root of our word "proximity." Your neighbour, in the Bible's own language, is simply whoever God has put near you. When a lawyer in Luke's Gospel asks, "And who is my neighbour?", Jesus answers with the parable of the Good Samaritan (Luke 10:29-37).
- **hōs seauton → sicut teipsum → as thyself.** The measure of the love is the care we already give ourselves without thinking: food when hungry, rest when weary, help when hurting. Jesus raises the bar even higher on the night before he dies: "love one another; as I have loved you" (John 13:34).

## Alignment

Very close. The Greek texts on Bible Hub agree on the commandment itself, Ἀγαπήσεις τὸν πλησίον σου ὡς σεαυτόν, letter for letter. The only differences are in the little introduction: the Byzantine Majority Text, the Greek Orthodox text and both Textus Receptus editions add δέ ("and" or "but"), which NA27/UBS4 print in brackets; and Westcott–Hort read αὕτη ("the second, like it, is this") instead of αὐτῇ ("like it"). The KJV's "And the second is like unto it" follows the Textus Receptus; the ESV's "And a second is like it" follows the critical text, keeping "And" for smooth English. The Vulgate is word for word: secundum (second), autem (and), simile est huic (is like this one), diliges proximum tuum sicut teipsum. The Aramaic Peshitta says, beautifully, "love your near one as your own soul" (naphshakh).

## The story

St. Jerome, the very scholar who gave the Church the Latin of today's verse, preserved one of the most beloved stories in all of Christian tradition. In his Commentary on Galatians (on 6:10), he tells how "the blessed John the Evangelist," the beloved disciple who leaned on Jesus' breast at the Last Supper, lived on at Ephesus into extreme old age. He was so frail that his disciples had to carry him into the church in their arms, and at every gathering he could say only one thing: "Little children, love one another." At last the brothers grew weary of hearing the same words and asked, "Master, why do you always say this?" And John gave an answer, Jerome says, worthy of John: "Because it is the Lord's command, and if this alone is done, it is enough." The last living apostle, who had stood at the foot of the cross, spent his final breaths preaching the second great commandment. For centuries the Church read this story aloud at Matins on his feast day, December 27.

## Dig Deeper: Roots in Hebrew & Jewish Sources

Jesus did not coin the words "thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself"; he quoted them, and every listener in the Temple knew where they came from. They stand in Leviticus 19:18, in the heart of the "Holiness Code," where God says, "Ye shall be holy: for I the LORD your God am holy" (19:2). In Hebrew the command is short and strong: ve-ahavta le-re'akha kamokha, "and you shall love your neighbour as yourself," followed by the divine signature, ani YHWH, "I am the LORD." The same chapter stretches the circle wider still: "The stranger that dwelleth with you ... thou shalt love him as thyself" (19:34). Long before Jesus, a Jewish community connected with the Dead Sea Scrolls wrote this command into its rule of life: the Damascus Document, known from copies found at Qumran (4Q266-273) and from two medieval copies discovered in the Cairo Genizah, lists among the covenant duties "to love each man his brother as himself." Later rabbinic tradition prized the verse just as highly. In the Sifra, a rabbinic commentary on Leviticus compiled in the centuries after the Gospels, Rabbi Akiva (died around AD 135) calls it "a great principle of the Torah." Those later writings are not where Matthew got his words; they show how deeply Jesus' own people treasured this command. What is breathtaking is what Jesus does with it: he sets it right beside the Shema, declares it "like" the first, and then, in the parable of the Good Samaritan and on the cross, shows how far "neighbour" truly reaches. Dig Deeper is background study, not new Scripture, and not an endorsement of any later theology.
