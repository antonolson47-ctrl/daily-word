## Setting the scene

It is autumn in Jerusalem, around the Feast of Tabernacles. Jesus has just given sight to a man born blind (John 9). Instead of rejoicing, some of the Pharisees question the man, mock him and throw him out of the synagogue. Jesus goes and finds him, and the man worships him (John 9:35-38). Then, speaking to those same leaders, Jesus begins to talk about sheep, thieves, hired hands and shepherds. Every listener raised on the Scriptures knew this picture. David had sung "The LORD is my shepherd" (Psalm 23:1), and through Ezekiel God had rebuked the shepherds of Israel who fed themselves and left the flock to scatter, promising, "I myself will search for my sheep" (Ezekiel 34:2, 11). Against that background, and right after a lost sheep has been cast out and found, Jesus says the words of today's verse. He is not one more shepherd among many; he is the Good Shepherd, and the proof will be that he gives his own life for the flock.

## Key words

- **egō eimi → ego sum → I am.** One of the seven great "I am" sayings in John's Gospel (bread, light, door, shepherd, resurrection, way, vine). The emphatic egō, "I myself," echoes the way God speaks of himself in the Old Testament, and in this same chapter Jesus says, "I and my Father are one" (John 10:30).
- **poimēn → pastor → shepherd.** Jerome's Latin word gives English its word "pastor." Every pastor's title is borrowed from Christ, the one Shepherd (John 10:16), whom Peter calls "the chief Shepherd" (1 Peter 5:4).
- **kalos → bonus → good.** Greek has two common words for good: agathos (morally good) and kalos, which also means noble, fine, beautiful, the real thing. Jesus is the true and beautiful Shepherd, the model every other shepherd falls short of. Latin bonus and English "good" carry the sense well, though "noble" is in there too.
- **psychēn → animam → life (soul).** The same word as "soul." He does not give part of himself; he gives his very life.
- **tithēsin → dat → lays down / giveth.** Literally "places, sets down," the way one lays aside a garment (John 13:4 uses the same verb when Jesus lays aside his robe to wash feet). Only John uses "lay down one's life" this way (John 10:15, 17-18; 15:13; 1 John 3:16). Jerome chose dat, "gives," and the KJV followed with "giveth"; the ESV keeps the picture of laying down. Jesus adds, "No man taketh it from me, but I lay it down of myself" (10:18). It is a free gift, not a defeat.
- **hyper → pro → for.** "On behalf of, in place of." The shepherd stands between the wolf and the sheep and takes the blow himself.

## Alignment

Very close across Greek, Latin and English. Nestle 1904, Westcott–Hort, Byzantine Majority, Tischendorf, the Greek Orthodox text and both Textus Receptus editions read the same words, differing only in capital letters and punctuation. Jerome follows the Greek phrase by phrase: Ego sum pastor bonus for egō eimi ho poimēn ho kalos. Two small Latin touches: dat ("gives") for tithēsin ("lays down"), and suis ("his own") added to "for the sheep," which the Douay-Rheims follows ("for his sheep"). The KJV's "giveth his life" stays close to the Latin sense, while the ESV, NASB, NIV and NRSV give the literal "lays down his life." Both say the same thing: he gives himself, freely and completely.

## The story

Some of the oldest Christian pictures in the world are not of the cross but of the Good Shepherd. On the walls and tombs of the Roman catacombs, such as Priscilla and Callixtus, from the 200s, believers painted and carved a young shepherd carrying a lamb across his shoulders, the lost sheep of Luke 15 brought home. In a time of persecution, when an open cross could be dangerous to display, this was how the early Church said, "Christ died for us and carries us." The Church still keeps a Good Shepherd Sunday each Easter season, when John 10 is read. And the same gentle picture is the one a frightened child or a dying saint still reaches for: "The LORD is my shepherd; I shall not want."

## Dig Deeper: Roots in Hebrew & Jewish Sources

When Jesus called himself the Good Shepherd, he was speaking straight out of the Hebrew Scriptures. "The LORD is my shepherd" (Psalm 23:1) is David's song, and in Ezekiel 34 God condemns the false shepherds of Israel who fed themselves instead of the flock, then promises, "I myself will search for my sheep," and "I will set up one shepherd over them... even my servant David" (Ezekiel 34:11, 23). Jesus' listeners had just watched leaders cast out a healed man (John 9), so the echo was hard to miss: the promised Shepherd had come. Among the Dead Sea Scrolls, copied in the last centuries before Christ, the Damascus Document asks the community's overseer to care for his members "as a shepherd his flock," which shows the shepherd picture was a living ideal for Jewish leaders in Jesus' day; it is background, and the Scrolls are not Scripture. Much later, the rabbinic midrash Exodus Rabbah (compiled centuries after the New Testament) tells a lovely story: when a young goat ran from Moses' flock, he found it drinking, carried it home on his shoulder, and God said that a man so tender with sheep was fit to shepherd Israel. It is a later Jewish tradition, not Scripture, but it shows how Israel prized a shepherd's compassion. Jesus goes further than any of them: the Good Shepherd does not only carry the sheep, he dies for them. Dig Deeper here is background study, not new Scripture, and not an endorsement of any later theology.
