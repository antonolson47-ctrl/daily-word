## Setting the scene

It is Thursday night in Jerusalem, the evening before the crucifixion. In the upper room Jesus has risen from supper, tied a towel around his waist and washed the disciples' feet, then told them, "I have given you an example, that ye should do as I have done to you" (John 13:15). He has foretold his betrayal, and Judas has taken the morsel and gone out: "and it was night" (13:30). With the betrayer gone, Jesus speaks tenderly to the eleven: "Little children, yet a little while I am with you … whither I go, ye cannot come" (13:33). Before he leaves them, he gives them one thing to hold on to. Peter will interrupt in the next breath, "Lord, whither goest thou?" (13:36).

## Key words

- **entolēn → mandatum → commandment.** A charge or order, the same word used for the commandments of the Law. Jesus does not offer a suggestion; he gives an instruction as Lord and Teacher (13:13).
- **kainēn → novum → new.** Greek has two words for "new": neos, new in time, and kainos, new in kind or quality. John uses kainos, the word of the "new covenant" (Luke 22:20) and the "new creation" (2 Corinthians 5:17). The command to love is old (Leviticus 19:18); what is new is its measure and source.
- **didōmi → do → I give.** A present-tense gift. The commandment comes as something handed over, like the bread he has just shared.
- **agapate → diligatis → love.** A present subjunctive in Greek, "that you keep on loving." Jerome again chose diligo, the verb of choosing and prizing, for agapaō.
- **allēlous → invicem → one another.** A reciprocal word: love that moves both ways among the disciples. It appears three times in 13:34-35.
- **kathōs ēgapēsa hymas → sicut dilexi vos → as I have loved you.** The aorist "I loved" looks at the whole of his love, just shown in the foot-washing (13:1, 13:15). Kathōs can mean both "in the same way as" and "because": his love is both the pattern and the ground of theirs.

## Alignment

Very close across Greek, Latin and English. Nestle 1904, Westcott–Hort, the Byzantine Majority Text, the Greek Orthodox text, Tischendorf and both Textus Receptus editions have identical words and differ only in punctuation (where to put a comma or a raised dot). Jerome renders the Greek word for word, keeping the repeated ut … ut for the repeated hina … hina. The KJV keeps that second "that" ("that ye also love one another"), while the ESV smooths it into a statement of duty, "you also are to love one another." The NIV's "so you must love one another" takes the same approach.

## The story

Holy Thursday is called Maundy Thursday because of this verse. In the old Latin liturgy, the washing of feet on that night was sung with the antiphon "Mandatum novum do vobis," and the rite itself came to be called the Mandatum. Through Old French, mandatum became the English "maundy." English monarchs kept the custom for centuries: medieval kings washed the feet of poor men on Maundy Thursday, and to this day the British sovereign gives specially minted Maundy money to elderly recipients, one man and one woman for each year of the monarch's age. Around AD 197 the North African writer Tertullian reported what pagans said about Christians in his day: "See, they say, how they love one another" (Apology 39). That is the very sign Jesus promised in the next verse: "By this shall all men know that ye are my disciples, if ye have love one to another" (13:35).

## Dig Deeper: Roots in Hebrew & Jewish Sources

Jesus calls this commandment "new," which only makes sense against an old one. The Torah already commanded love: "thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself" (Leviticus 19:18), and the same chapter extends it to the stranger living among Israel (19:34). Jesus himself names Leviticus 19:18 as the second great commandment (Matthew 22:39). Jewish teachers in his own era also prized that verse. Hillel the Elder, who died around AD 10, is remembered for summing up the Torah to a would-be convert: "What is hateful to you, do not do to your fellow; that is the whole Torah, and the rest is its commentary; go and learn it." That saying is preserved in the Babylonian Talmud (Shabbat 31a), which was compiled about AD 500, centuries after Hillel lived. Among the Dead Sea Scrolls, written before and around the time of Jesus, the Damascus Document tells members "to love each man his brother as himself," while the Community Rule from Qumran asks them to "love all the sons of light" but "hate all the sons of darkness." Against that background the newness of John 13:34 is not love itself but its measure: "as I have loved you." The standard is no longer "as yourself" but Jesus, who has just washed their feet and will lay down his life the next day (John 15:13). Dig Deeper here is background study, not new Scripture, and not an endorsement of any later theology.
