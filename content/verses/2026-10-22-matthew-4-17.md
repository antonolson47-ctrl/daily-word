## Setting the scene

Galilee, around AD 28. John the Baptist has just been arrested by Herod Antipas, and Jesus, freshly back from forty days of testing in the wilderness, leaves his hometown of Nazareth and settles in Capernaum, a fishing town on the north shore of the Sea of Galilee. Matthew sees in this move the fulfillment of Isaiah: "the people dwelling in darkness have seen a great light" (Matthew 4:15–16; Isaiah 9:1–2). Galilee was a crossroads region, mixed Jewish and Gentile, looked down on by Jerusalem. That is where the light shows up first. With John silenced, Jesus picks up the very message John had preached in the desert (Matthew 3:2) and begins his own public ministry. The next thing he does is call Peter, Andrew, James and John from their fishing nets.

## Key words

- **Apo tote → Exinde → From that time.** A hinge phrase. Matthew uses it here to open Jesus' public ministry, and again at 16:21 when Jesus begins to speak of the cross. These are the two great turning points of the Gospel.
- **kēryssein → prædicare → to preach.** To proclaim like a herald (kēryx) announcing a king's decree in the town square. It is news, not advice.
- **Metanoeite → Pœnitentiam agite → Repent.** From meta (change, after) and noeō (to think, perceive): literally, change your mind, think again from the ground up. The Hebrew idea behind it is shûv, to turn around, and the Aramaic Peshitta has ܬܘܒܘ (tubu), from the same root. The present tense suggests an ongoing turning, not a one-time event.
- **ēngiken → appropinquavit → is at hand / has come near.** Perfect tense: the kingdom has drawn near and is now standing right here. In Jesus' person it has arrived at the door.
- **hē basileia tōn ouranōn → regnum cælorum → the kingdom of heaven.** Matthew's own phrase, used about 32 times, where Mark and Luke say "kingdom of God." Devout Jews often said "heaven" out of reverence for God's name, and "heavens" is plural in Greek, echoing the Hebrew shāmayim. Basileia means reign and rule as much as territory: God acting as king.

## Alignment

Very close in meaning, with one famous exception. Greek, KJV and ESV all say "repent." Jerome's Latin, pœnitentiam agite, can be read "do penance," and that single phrase became one of the most argued-over translations in Christian history. A tidbit In 1516 the scholar Erasmus published his Greek New Testament with a fresh Latin translation and rendered metanoeite as resipiscite, "come to your senses," arguing it was closer to the Greek than "do penance." A year later, on October 31, 1517, Martin Luther opened his 95 Theses with this very verse: "When our Lord and Master Jesus Christ said 'Repent' (Pœnitentiam agite), he willed the entire life of believers to be one of repentance." Catholic and Protestant readers came to stress different sides of the word, the inner turning of the heart and its outward fruit, but both agree it is the first word of Jesus' preaching in Matthew. The Gospel begins not with a rule but with an announcement: the King is here, so turn around and come home.

## Dig Deeper: Roots in Hebrew & Jewish Sources

Matthew wrote in Greek, but Jesus' first sermon is soaked in Hebrew thought. "Repent" stands for the great prophetic call shûv, "turn back," heard in Hosea's "Return, O Israel, to the LORD your God" (Hosea 14:1, numbered 14:2 in Hebrew Bibles) and all through Isaiah and Jeremiah; the Aramaic Peshitta's tubu comes from the same root. Matthew frames the moment with Isaiah's promise of light dawning on Galilee (Isaiah 9:1–2, numbered 8:23–9:1 in Hebrew Bibles). Among the Dead Sea Scrolls, the Qumran community called itself "the returners of Israel" (shāvê Yisrāʾēl) in the Damascus Document, a pre-Christian witness that "turning" was the watchword of people waiting for God to act. And "kingdom of heaven" (malkhût shāmayim) is a Jewish idiom: the Mishnah, written down around AD 200, speaks of reciting the Shema as taking on "the yoke of the kingdom of heaven." These sources are background study, not Scripture, and including them is not an endorsement of any later theology. Keep the dates straight: Hosea and Isaiah are Hebrew Bible (Tanakh); the Damascus Document is pre-Christian (2nd–1st century BC); the Mishnah (about AD 200) and Talmud (about AD 400–600) come later and are not sources Matthew used, though they preserve older Jewish ways of speaking.
