## Setting the scene

It is early morning on the shore of the Sea of Tiberias, the Sea of Galilee. Peter had said, "I go a fishing," and six other disciples went with him; they toiled all night and caught nothing. At daybreak a stranger on the beach told them to cast the net on the right side of the ship, and the net filled with "an hundred and fifty and three" great fish. "It is the Lord," the beloved disciple said, and Peter threw on his coat and leapt into the sea (John 21:1-8). Ashore they found "a fire of coals there, and fish laid thereon, and bread," and Jesus said, "Come and dine" (21:9-12). Only a few weeks earlier Peter had stood warming himself at another fire of coals in the high priest's courtyard and denied his Lord three times (18:18, 25-27). Now, after breakfast, Jesus turns to him: "Simon, son of Jonas, lovest thou me more than these?" He asks a second time, "lovest thou me?" Then comes the third question.

## Key words

- **agapaō / phileō → diligo / amo → love.** In the first two questions Jesus asks, "agapas me?" (Latin diligis me?). Each time Peter answers, "philō se," "I love you" (amo te). The third time Jesus takes up Peter's own word: "phileis me?" (amas me?). English has only one word, "love," so the KJV and ESV cannot show the shift. Many teachers, from the Fathers to modern pulpits, hear in it the Lord stooping to meet Peter where he is; others, noting that John often uses the two verbs side by side (both are used of the Father's love for the Son, 3:35 and 5:20), read them as near synonyms varied for style. Either way, the heart of the scene is the same: three questions for three denials, and three times the same answer.
- **to triton → tertio → the third time.** John repeats it twice in one verse. The third question is what grieves Peter. He hears the echo of the third denial, spoken beside a fire of coals just like this one; the Greek word for that charcoal fire (anthrakia) appears in the New Testament only in John 18:18 and 21:9.
- **elypēthē → contristatus est → was grieved.** A passive verb of real sorrow, the same root Jesus used at the Last Supper: "ye shall be sorrowful, but your sorrow shall be turned into joy" (16:20). Peter's grief here is the sorrow that heals.
- **oidas … ginōskeis → nosti … scis → thou knowest … thou knowest.** Peter no longer boasts, as he did at the supper ("I will lay down my life for thy sake," 13:37). He appeals to Christ's own knowledge: "Lord, thou knowest all things." He who knew Peter would deny him also knows that Peter loves him.
- **boske ta probatia mou → pasce oves meas → feed my sheep.** Boskō is the shepherd's word for grazing and feeding a flock; in verse 16 Jesus used poimaine, "tend, shepherd" (Latin pasce in all three). The sheep remain "my sheep." Jesus, the Good Shepherd who lays down his life for the sheep (10:11), entrusts his own flock to the one who failed him.

## Alignment

Close across Greek, Latin and English, with one real loss in English. Jerome kept the change of verbs, rendering agapaō as diligo in verses 15-16 and phileō as amo here, so the Latin preserves what the KJV and ESV flatten into "love." Nestle 1904 and Westcott–Hort read "Simon son of John" (Iōanou) and the diminutive probatia, "little sheep" or "dear sheep"; the Byzantine Majority Text, the Greek Orthodox text and both Textus Receptus editions read "son of Jonas" (Iōna) and the ordinary probata, which is why the KJV has "son of Jonas" and the ESV "son of John." The Vulgate's Simon Joannis follows the "John" reading, and its oves, "sheep," matches probata. The Aramaic Peshitta uses a single verb of love, rḥem, in all three questions and answers.

## The story

St Augustine, preaching on this verse, said that "to the threefold denial there is now appended a threefold confession, that his tongue may not yield a feebler service to love than to fear" (Tractates on the Gospel of John 123.5). He added that "Do you love me? Feed my sheep" means: if you love me, think not of feeding yourself, but feed my sheep. In the very next verses Jesus foretells "by what death he should glorify God" (21:18-19), and ancient tradition, recorded by Origen and the historian Eusebius, says Peter was crucified in Rome under Nero, head downward at his own request, judging himself unworthy to die as his Lord had died. The Church remembers this charge to feed the flock in Peter's feasts and in the Chair of St Peter (February 22), and at St Peter's Basilica the words of Christ to Peter run in great letters around the base of Michelangelo's dome.

## Dig Deeper: Roots in Hebrew & Jewish Sources

When Jesus tells Peter to feed his sheep, he speaks the language of Israel's prophets. In Ezekiel 34 the Lord rebukes the shepherds of Israel, its rulers, who fed themselves and not the flock: "Should not the shepherds feed the flocks? … ye feed not the flock" (34:2-3). The Hebrew verb is ra'ah (רָעָה), "to shepherd, to feed," and the Greek Septuagint renders the end of verse 3 ta probata mou ou boskete, "my sheep you do not feed," the very words, reversed into a command, that Jesus gives Peter: boske ta probata mou. In the same chapter God promises, "I will feed my flock" (34:15) and "I will set up one shepherd over them, … my servant David" (34:23). David himself was called from the sheepfold with the words "Thou shalt feed my people Israel" (2 Samuel 5:2). So the risen Christ, the promised Shepherd, now hands his flock to Peter, not to rule it for himself as the false shepherds did, but to feed it out of love. Hebrew also has one main word for love, ahav (as in Deuteronomy 6:5, "thou shalt love the LORD thy God"), and Franz Delitzsch's 19th-century Hebrew New Testament uses it for all of Jesus's questions, much as the Aramaic Peshitta uses one verb throughout. Among the Dead Sea Scrolls, the Damascus Document describes the community's overseer as one who should care for his members "as a shepherd his flock," a pre-Christian Jewish picture of a leader as shepherd. Dig Deeper is background study, not new Scripture, and not an endorsement of any later theology.
