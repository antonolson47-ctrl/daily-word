## Setting the scene

Outside Jerusalem, at the place called The Skull (Greek Kranion, Latin Calvariae), on the day of the crucifixion. Pilate has handed Jesus over; Simon of Cyrene has been forced to carry the cross; and "when they were come to the place... they crucified him there, and the malefactors, one on the right hand, and the other on the left" (Luke 23:33). This verse is the first of Jesus' sayings from the cross in Luke. While the nails are driven or just after, he does not cry for himself. He prays for the men who are killing him. The second half of the verse then shows the soldiers, already treating him as dead property, dividing his clothes by casting lots. Luke alone records this prayer among the four Gospels; Matthew, Mark and John all note the casting of lots for the garments (Matthew 27:35; Mark 15:24; John 19:23-24), but only Luke preserves "Father, forgive them."

## Key words

- **aphes → dimitte → forgive.** Aorist imperative of aphiēmi, literally "send away" or "let go": release the debt, dismiss the charge. The same verb stands behind "forgive us our debts" in the Lord's Prayer (Matthew 6:12). Jerome chose dimitte, "dismiss / send away," which the Douay-Rheims keeps as "forgive."
- **Pater → Pater → Father.** Vocative: Jesus addresses God as Father even from the cross, as he will again with "Father, into thy hands I commend my spirit" (Luke 23:46). Luke loves this note of prayer; his Gospel alone records Jesus praying at his baptism, before choosing the Twelve, and here for his killers.
- **oidasin → sciunt → they know.** Perfect of oida, "to know," with the force of a settled state: they do not grasp what they are doing. Peter later uses the same idea in Acts 3:17, "I wot that through ignorance ye did it." Ignorance does not erase guilt, but Jesus names it as grounds for mercy.
- **diamerizomenoi → Dividentes → dividing.** Present participle of diamerizō, "to divide thoroughly / distribute." The soldiers are in the middle of splitting up his clothes while he prays.
- **klērous → sortes → lots.** From klēros, a lot cast to decide a share (the English "clergy" and "clerk" come from the same root, via the "portion" assigned). Nestle 1904 and Tischendorf have the plural klērous; Westcott–Hort and the Byzantine Majority have the singular klēron. Either way the picture is the same: they gamble for his garments, fulfilling Psalm 22:18.

## Alignment

Close on wording; disputed on whether the prayer belongs in the text at all. Nestle 1904, both Textus Receptus editions, the Byzantine Majority Text, the Greek Orthodox text, Tischendorf and Jerome's Vulgate all include "Father, forgive them." So do the KJV, ESV, NIV and most English Bibles. But some of the earliest Greek witnesses omit the prayer: Papyrus 75, Codex Vaticanus (B), Codex Bezae (D) and a few others. Modern critical editions (NA27/UBS4) print the prayer in double brackets, signalling that the editors think it was added early rather than written by Luke; the Tyndale House Greek New Testament keeps it in the main text with a diamond mark. Within the prayer itself the Greek, Latin and English line up tightly: aphes / dimitte / forgive, oidasin / sciunt / know, poiousin / faciunt / do. The only small Greek difference in the second half is singular "lot" versus plural "lots."

## The story

This prayer is one of the most famous disputed lines in the New Testament, and also one of the earliest attested. Tatian's Diatessaron (late 2nd century) already had it, and Irenaeus and others quote it, which means the saying was circulating well before our oldest papyrus that omits it. Stephen, the first Christian martyr, echoes it almost word for word as he dies: "Lord, lay not this sin to their charge" (Acts 7:60). And Isaiah had already said of the Suffering Servant that "he bare the sin of many, and made intercession for the transgressors" (Isaiah 53:12). Whether a scribe added a known saying of Jesus into Luke, or an early copyist dropped a hard prayer from the Gospel, the Church has prayed these words for nearly two thousand years. Meanwhile the soldiers below, deaf to the prayer above them, cast lots for a seamless coat and fulfill a psalm written a thousand years earlier.
