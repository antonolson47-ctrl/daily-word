## Setting the scene

It is Thursday evening of the last week, and the city of Jerusalem is crowded with pilgrims for Passover, the great feast that remembers how God brought Israel out of Egypt. Jesus has sent Peter and John ahead to prepare the meal, and now he reclines at table with the Twelve in a borrowed upper room (26:17-20). The mood is heavy. Jesus has just said, "One of you shall betray me," and Judas has asked, "Master, is it I?" and heard the answer, "Thou hast said" (26:21-25). Outside, the plot is already in motion. And yet, in that very night, the Lord does something astonishing. In the middle of the meal that celebrated Israel's freedom, he takes an ordinary loaf, gives thanks to his Father, breaks it, and hands it to his friends with words no rabbi had ever spoken over Passover bread: "This is my body." Within hours that body would be arrested in Gethsemane; within a day it would hang on the cross. Before the world could take his life, Jesus gave it, freely, into the hands of his disciples. St. Paul, writing only about twenty-five years later, says he "received of the Lord" this very tradition and passed it on to the church (1 Corinthians 11:23-24), and the followers of Jesus have gathered around these words ever since.

## Key words

- **esthiontōn autōn → cœnantibus eis → as they were eating.** This happens during the Passover supper itself, not after it. Jerome's cœnantibus comes from cena, "supper," the word that gives us the Latin name for this night, the Cena Domini, the Lord's Supper.
- **labōn arton → accepit panem → took bread.** Artos is the everyday word for bread, but at Passover the only bread on the table was unleavened, the matzah that Moses called "the bread of affliction" (Deuteronomy 16:3). Jesus takes the bread of Israel's deliverance and gives it a new and deeper meaning.
- **eulogēsas → benedixit → blessed.** To "bless" here means to praise God over the food, the way every faithful Jew did before eating. Here the Byzantine Majority Text reads eucharistēsas, "having given thanks," the same verb Luke and Paul use (Luke 22:19; 1 Corinthians 11:24). From that Greek word the church took its oldest name for this meal: the Eucharist, the Thanksgiving.
- **eklasen → fregit → brake, broke.** One loaf, broken and shared. "The breaking of bread" became one of the first descriptions of Christian worship (Acts 2:42), and the risen Jesus would be "known of them in breaking of bread" at Emmaus (Luke 24:35).
- **labete phagete → accipite, et comedite → take, eat.** Two short commands, a gift held out in an open hand. Jesus does not ask the disciples to earn it, only to receive it.
- **touto estin to sōma mou → hoc est corpus meum → this is my body.** Sōma means the living body, the whole person; Jerome's corpus carries it straight into Latin, and the Aramaic Peshitta reads hanaw pagri, "this is my body." The earliest Christians received these words with awe. Around AD 110, St. Ignatius of Antioch, on his way to martyrdom, called the Eucharist "the flesh of our Saviour Jesus Christ" (To the Smyrnaeans 7), and St. Justin Martyr, about AD 150, wrote that this food is not received "as common bread" (First Apology 66). Believers in every tradition treasure this verse as the Lord's own gift of himself.

## Alignment

Very close. Every Greek text on Bible Hub agrees on the heart of the verse, Λάβετε φάγετε· τοῦτό ἐστιν τὸ σῶμά μου. The differences are small and lovely. Nestle 1904, Westcott–Hort, NA27/UBS4 and Tischendorf read ἄρτον ("bread") and δοὺς ("having given"), while the Byzantine Majority Text, the Greek Orthodox text and both Textus Receptus editions read τὸν ἄρτον ("the bread") and ἐδίδου ... καὶ εἶπεν ("was giving ... and said"). The Byzantine Majority Text alone has εὐχαριστήσας ("gave thanks") where the others have εὐλογήσας ("blessed"). The KJV's "blessed it ... and gave it ... and said" follows the Textus Receptus; the ESV's "after blessing it broke it and gave it" follows the shorter text. The Vulgate is word for word and adds only suis ("his" disciples): accepit Jesus panem, et benedixit, ac fregit ... hoc est corpus meum. The Aramaic Peshitta likewise says Jesus "blessed" (barekh) and "broke" the bread.

## The story

One of the oldest Christian prayers outside the New Testament is a thanksgiving over this broken bread. The Didache, a little church manual from around AD 100, teaches believers to pray at the Eucharist: "Even as this broken bread was scattered over the hills, and was gathered together and became one, so let Your Church be gathered together from the ends of the earth into Your kingdom" (Didache 9:4). Picture it: wheat scattered across the hills of Galilee and Judea, gathered, ground, and baked into one loaf, and that loaf broken in Jesus' hands so it could be shared. The first Christians saw in every loaf a picture of the church itself, many scattered people made one in the body of Christ. St. Paul said the same thing: "For we being many are one bread, and one body: for we are all partakers of that one bread" (1 Corinthians 10:17). The bread Jesus broke that night is still being shared at tables on every continent.

## Dig Deeper: Roots in Hebrew & Jewish Sources

Today's verse happens at a Jewish table, during a Jewish feast, with a Jewish blessing on the lips of Jesus, so the Hebrew roots shine a bright light on it. The Torah commanded Israel to eat unleavened bread at Passover, "the bread of affliction," so that every generation would remember the night God brought them out of Egypt (Deuteronomy 16:3). Faithful Jews also blessed God before eating bread; the Mishnah, compiled around AD 200, records the words still said today, "Blessed ... who brings forth bread from the earth" (Berakhot 6:1). The Gospels do not tell us the exact words of Jesus' blessing, but it was a prayer of praise to the Father in this same spirit. The Mishnah's description of the Passover meal (Pesachim 10) is also later than the Gospels in its written form, yet it preserves a striking custom: Rabban Gamaliel taught that the Passover lamb, the matzah and the bitter herbs must each be explained at the meal, and the Mishnah adds, "In each and every generation a person must view himself as though he personally left Egypt." Into that very custom of explaining the bread, Jesus spoke his new words, "This is my body." Older still are the Dead Sea Scrolls, copied before the time of Christ: the Rule of the Congregation (1QSa) pictures a future meal where the priest blesses the bread and wine first and the Messiah of Israel then stretches out his hand to the bread, a sign of how deeply Jews of that era linked a sacred meal with the hope of God's anointed King. These are background study, not Scripture, and not an endorsement of any later theology.
