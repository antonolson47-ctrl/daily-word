## Setting the scene

It has been a heavy chapter. John the Baptist, the fiery prophet of the Jordan, now sits in Herod's prison and sends his disciples to ask Jesus, "Art thou he that should come?" (11:2-3). Jesus answers with the signs of the Kingdom, honors John as more than a prophet, and then grieves over "this generation," which danced to no song and mourned to no lament (11:16-19). He pronounces solemn woes on Chorazin, Bethsaida and even his own base at Capernaum, towns that saw his mighty works and did not repent (11:20-24). Then, in a sudden turn, he lifts his eyes in prayer: "I thank thee, O Father, Lord of heaven and earth, because thou hast hid these things from the wise and prudent, and hast revealed them unto babes" (11:25). He declares that all things have been delivered to him by the Father, and that no one knows the Father except the Son and those to whom the Son reveals him (11:27). And then, to everyone listening, the tired fishermen, the farmers, the people crushed by debts, sickness and religious burdens, he opens his arms: "Come unto me." Only Matthew records these words. The very next scene (12:1-14) is a fight over the Sabbath, the day of rest, and suddenly we see what Jesus means: the true rest God promised is found in a Person.

## Key words

- **deute → venite → come.** An urgent, warm call, almost "Come here!" It is the same word Jesus used when he called the fishermen by the sea, "Come ye after me, and I will make you fishers of men" (Matthew 4:19), and the word the risen Lord used on the shore, "Come and dine" (John 21:12). The Latin venite became the name of a beloved prayer: the "Venite," Psalm 95, "O come, let us sing unto the Lord," sung at dawn by monks and congregations for centuries.
- **pros me → ad me → unto me.** Not to a program, a teacher's system or a set of rules, but to Jesus himself. In the verse just before, he said no one knows the Father except through the Son. Now the Son says: come to me.
- **hoi kopiōntes → qui laboratis → ye that labour.** Kopiaō means to toil to the point of exhaustion, to be worn out. It is the word for the fishermen who "toiled all the night" and caught nothing (Luke 5:5). The present tense pictures people still laboring, still worn down.
- **pephortismenoi → onerati → heavy laden.** From phortion, a load or cargo, the kind of pack strapped onto a donkey. The perfect passive means a burden that has been loaded onto you by someone else and is still there. Jesus uses the same image when he warns about those who "bind heavy burdens and grievous to be borne, and lay them on men's shoulders" (Matthew 23:4).
- **kagō anapausō → et ego reficiam → and I will give you rest.** Kagō is emphatic: "and I, I myself." Anapauō means to give rest, to refresh, to let someone stop and breathe again. Jerome chose reficiam, from reficio, "to remake, restore, refresh," the root of our word "refection," a restoring meal. Jesus does not just lighten the load; he renews the person carrying it.

## Alignment

Very close. Every Greek text on Bible Hub (Nestle 1904, Westcott–Hort with NA27/UBS4, the Byzantine Majority Text, the Greek Orthodox text, Tischendorf and both Textus Receptus editions) has exactly the same words; the only differences are a lowercase first letter in Scrivener and missing punctuation in Stephanus. The Vulgate follows word for word, with one lovely nuance: anapausō ("I will give rest") becomes reficiam ("I will refresh, restore"). The KJV and ESV are nearly identical; the ESV simply says "all who labor" for "all ye that labour," and the NASB and Holman render kopiōntes as "weary."

## The story

For nearly five hundred years, English-speaking Christians have heard this verse right before coming to the Lord's Table. In Archbishop Thomas Cranmer's Book of Common Prayer (1549, revised 1552), the minister turns to the people and says: "Hear what comfortable words our Saviour Christ saith unto all that truly turn to him: Come unto me, all that travail and are heavy laden, and I will refresh you." These "Comfortable Words" (comfortable then meant strengthening, from the Latin confortare, to make strong) are still spoken in Anglican churches today. Two centuries later, George Frideric Handel set the verse to music in Messiah (1742). In the gentle aria "He shall feed his flock like a shepherd," the soprano takes up Jesus' invitation: "Come unto Him, all ye that labour, come unto Him that are heavy laden, and He will give you rest." Millions have heard it every Christmas season since.

## Dig Deeper: Roots in Hebrew & Jewish Sources

Jesus spoke these words to Jewish listeners steeped in the Scriptures, and the very next verse lets us hear the echo: "ye shall find rest unto your souls" (Matthew 11:29) is drawn from Jeremiah 6:16, where the LORD tells Israel to "ask for the old paths, where is the good way, and walk therein, and ye shall find rest for your souls" (Hebrew u-mitz'u margoa le-nafshechem). Sadly, Israel answered, "We will not walk therein." Jesus offers that same rest again, now in himself. The word "rest" has deep Hebrew roots too. When Moses begged God not to leave his people, the LORD promised, "My presence shall go with thee, and I will give thee rest" (Exodus 33:14, va-hanichoti lakh, from the root nuach, "to rest"). The Aramaic Peshitta of Matthew 11:28 uses that same family of words, ʾanīḥkūn, "I will give you rest." And when Jesus goes on to say, "Take my yoke upon you" (11:29), his hearers would know the image well. Later rabbinic teaching, preserved in the Mishnah (compiled around AD 200, more than a century after Jesus), speaks of taking on "the yoke of the kingdom of Heaven" when reciting the Shema, and of "the yoke of the Torah" lifting other burdens. These sayings were written down after the Gospel, so they are not sources Matthew borrowed from; they are windows into the Jewish language of yoke and burden that Jesus' audience shared. Against that background, his claim is breathtaking: the rest God gave through his presence, and the yoke that once meant the Law, he now offers as his own. Dig Deeper is background study, not new Scripture, and not an endorsement of any later theology.
