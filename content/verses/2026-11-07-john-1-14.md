## Setting the scene

John's Gospel opens not with a manger or a genealogy, but with eternity: "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God" (John 1:1). Verses 1–13 set the stage—creation through the Word, light shining in darkness, John the Baptist as witness, and the gift that those who receive him become children of God. Then comes this verse, the hinge of the prologue and one of the most treasured lines in all of Scripture. The eternal Word does not remain distant. He becomes flesh. He pitches his tent among us. The same disciple who leaned on Jesus' breast will later write that they had seen, heard, and handled the Word of life (1 John 1:1-2). Here, in a single sentence, John tells the heart of the incarnation: God with us, glory seen, grace and truth poured out.

## Key words

- **Logos → Verbum → Word.** The same Logos of John 1:1: the eternal, personal Word who was with God and was God. Jerome's Verbum keeps that weight. English "Word" is not a mere sound or idea; it is the Son who speaks the Father and through whom all things were made.
- **sarx → caro → flesh.** Not a ghost or a temporary appearance, but real human nature—body, soul, the whole of our fragile condition—taken on without ceasing to be God. The early Church clung to this word against every teaching that Christ only seemed human.
- **eskēnōsen → habitavit → dwelt (tabernacled).** From skēnoō, "to pitch a tent / tabernacle." The Word did not visit and leave; he set up camp among us. Hebrew readers hear the wilderness tabernacle, where the glory of the LORD filled the tent (Exodus 40:34-35). Jerome's habitavit, "he dwelt," is quieter in Latin but still the same gift: God living with his people.
- **monogenous → unigeniti → only-begotten / only Son.** Unique, one-of-a-kind Son. Nestle and the Vulgate keep the traditional "only-begotten" sense; the ESV renders "only Son." Either way the point is the same: no other shares this glory from the Father.
- **charitos → gratiæ → grace.** Unmerited favor, gift, kindness. John will soon say that from his fullness we have all received "grace upon grace" (John 1:16).
- **alētheias → veritatis → truth.** Reality as God knows it, and faithfulness. Paired with grace here as God's "abounding in steadfast love and faithfulness" is paired in Exodus 34:6. In Jesus, grace and truth are not rivals; they meet in one Person.

## Alignment

Very close across Greek, Latin and English. Nestle 1904, Westcott–Hort, the Byzantine Majority, the Greek Orthodox text, Tischendorf and both Textus Receptus editions all read essentially the same Greek; punctuation and capitalization of Logos / Patros vary slightly, but the wording does not. Jerome's Vulgate tracks the Greek tightly: Logos / Verbum / Word, sarx / caro / flesh, eskēnōsen / habitavit / dwelt, monogenous / unigeniti / only-begotten, charitos / gratiæ / grace, alētheias / veritatis / truth. The KJV's "was made flesh" and the ESV's "became flesh" both render egeneto; "beheld" (KJV) and "have seen" (ESV) both answer etheasametha. The richest translational note is eskēnōsen: "dwelt" is accurate and traditional; "tabernacled" or "pitched his tent" (Young, Rotherham, and several older commentators) brings out the Exodus echo that the Greek word carries.

## The story

The Church has sung and prayed this verse for nearly two thousand years because it names the miracle at the center of the faith: the eternal Son truly became man and lived among us. Christmas, the creeds, and every Communion table rest on it. John does not say the Word became an idea, a feeling, or a vision. He became flesh—and then he tabernacled among ordinary people who could see his glory with their own eyes. That glory is not only the blaze of the Transfiguration; it is also the glory of compassion, obedience, the cross, and the empty tomb. Full of grace and truth, he is the greater tabernacle and the true temple: God dwelling with us, not behind a veil, but face to face in Jesus Christ.

## Dig Deeper: Roots in Hebrew & Jewish Sources

John 1:14 stands on Hebrew ground in a special way. When the Word "became flesh" and "tabernacled" among us (eskēnōsen), the Gospel is using the language of God's dwelling with Israel. In Exodus the LORD says, "Let them make me a sanctuary, that I may dwell among them" (Exodus 25:8), and when the tabernacle is finished "the glory of the LORD filled the tabernacle" so that Moses could not enter (Exodus 40:34-35). John's "we beheld his glory" answers that wilderness glory—now seen not in cloud and fire alone, but in the face of Jesus. The Greek skēnoō (to pitch a tent) and later Jewish talk of the Shekinah (the dwelling Presence) share the same root idea of God making his home with his people; that link is background study, not a claim that John is quoting a later rabbinic term. "Grace and truth" also echo the Hebrew pairing of hesed and emet—"steadfast love and faithfulness"—in Exodus 34:6, when the LORD passes before Moses and proclaims his name. Monogenēs ("only-begotten / unique Son") sits beside Hebrew yachid, the "only" beloved son language of Genesis 22. These Tanakh texts are pre-Christian Scripture; John's Gospel is 1st-century Christian witness; later Mishnah and Talmud (compiled from about AD 200 onward) continue Jewish reflection on God's presence, but they are later than the Gospel scene. Dig Deeper here is background study, not new Scripture, and not an endorsement of any later theology.
