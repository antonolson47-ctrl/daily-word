## Setting the scene

It is the evening of the eighth day after the Resurrection, in a room in Jerusalem behind shut doors. A week earlier, on Easter night, the risen Jesus had stood among his disciples and said, "Peace be unto you," and shown them his hands and his side (John 20:19-20). But Thomas, "one of the twelve, called Didymus" (the Twin), was not with them. When the others told him, "We have seen the Lord," he answered, "Except I shall see in his hands the print of the nails, and put my finger into the print of the nails, and thrust my hand into his side, I will not believe" (20:24-25). Now "after eight days again his disciples were within, and Thomas with them" (20:26). Jesus comes, the doors still shut, and speaks first to Thomas: "Reach hither thy finger, and behold my hands; and reach hither thy hand, and thrust it into my side: and be not faithless, but believing" (20:27). Thomas answers.

## Key words

- **apekrithē → respondit → answered.** Thomas does not merely speak; he answers. Jesus has met his own words almost exactly ("finger," "hands," "side"), and this is his reply. The Gospel never says he actually touched the wounds. Seeing and hearing the Lord was enough.
- **Kyrios → Dominus → Lord.** Kyrios can mean "sir" or "master," and Mary Magdalene had called Jesus her Lord at the tomb (20:13). But in the Greek Old Testament Kyrios is also the word that stands in for the holy Name of God. On Thomas's lips, after the Resurrection, it carries its full weight.
- **Theos → Deus → God.** This is the high point of John's Gospel. It opened with "the Word was God" (1:1), and here, near its close, a disciple says the same thing to the risen Jesus face to face. John adds right after that these things "are written, that ye might believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God" (20:31).
- **ho … mou, ho … mou → meus … meus → my … my.** Each title has its own article and its own "my." Grammatically it is the nominative with the article used as a direct address, a common Greek way of calling out to someone, so it is spoken to Jesus, not about him: "my Lord and my God." It is a personal confession, not a general statement.

## Alignment

Very close across Greek, Latin and English. Nestle 1904, Westcott–Hort (with no NA27/UBS4 variants) and Tischendorf agree on the wording of the confession; the Byzantine Majority Text, the Greek Orthodox text and both Textus Receptus editions open with "And" (kai), and the Textus Receptus adds the article before Thomas (ho Thōmas), which the KJV reflects as "And Thomas answered." Every Greek text reads the confession itself the same way: Ho Kyrios mou kai ho Theos mou. Jerome's Latin is word for word, Dominus meus et Deus meus, and the Douay-Rheims follows it: "My Lord, and my God." The KJV and ESV agree; the ESV simply shortens "answered and said unto him" to "answered him" and adds an exclamation mark.

## The story

Thomas is remembered in the Church as "doubting Thomas," but tradition honors him far more as believing Thomas. Pope St Gregory the Great preached that the unbelief of Thomas did more for our faith than the faith of the disciples who believed at once, because in touching the wounds of his Master he healed the wounds of our unbelief (Homily 26 on the Gospels). Ancient tradition carries Thomas east as far as India, where the St Thomas Christians of Kerala trace their faith to his preaching and where he is honored as a martyr at Mylapore, near Chennai, at the Basilica of San Thome. His words have also become a prayer of the faithful: many Catholics quietly say "My Lord and my God" as the Host is lifted up at Mass, a devotion Pope St Pius X encouraged in 1907. Caravaggio painted the scene around 1601-02 in "The Incredulity of Saint Thomas," with Thomas leaning close as Jesus himself guides his hand.

## Dig Deeper: Roots in Hebrew & Jewish Sources

Thomas was a Jew of Galilee, raised on the psalms and the prayers of Israel, and his confession is worded in the language of those prayers. In Psalm 35:23 David cries out, "Stir up thyself, and awake to my judgment, even unto my cause, my God and my Lord." The Hebrew reads Elohai va'Adonai (אֱלֹהַי וַאדֹנָי), "my God and my Lord," and the Greek Septuagint, where it is Psalm 34:23, renders it ho theos mou kai ho kyrios mou, the very words Thomas uses, only in reverse order. Behind the two words stand two of the great names of God in the Hebrew Scriptures. Elohim is the first name for God in the Bible ("In the beginning God," Genesis 1:1). Adonai, "my Lord," is the word Jewish readers say aloud in place of the holy four-letter Name, which is why the Septuagint so often renders that Name as Kyrios and the KJV as "the LORD." So when a devout Jew calls the risen Jesus "my Lord and my God," he is reaching for the words Israel reserved for the God of Abraham. The Aramaic Peshitta, the Bible of the Syriac-speaking churches, renders it Mari w'Alahi, from the same mar ("Lord") heard in the early Christian prayer Maranatha, "Our Lord, come." No Dead Sea Scroll passage bears directly on this verse, so none is listed here. Dig Deeper is background study, not new Scripture, and not an endorsement of any later theology.

**A quick timeline, so the sources don't blur together**

| Source | Date | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| Genesis and Psalm 35 (Tanakh / Old Testament) | Hebrew Scriptures, written centuries before Christ | Elohim and Adonai; "my God and my Lord" |
| Septuagint (Greek Old Testament) | translated about 250-100 BC | The Greek Bible the evangelists often quote, with ho theos mou kai ho kyrios mou in Psalm 34:23 |
| Gospel of John | 1st century AD | "My Lord and my God" |
| Mishnah and Talmud (rabbinic writings) | Mishnah about AD 200; Talmud about AD 500 | Later Jewish sources; the Jewish Virtual Library notes that by the time of the Talmud saying a substitute such as Adonai for the Name was the custom |
| Peshitta (Syriac/Aramaic New Testament) | standard by about the 5th century AD | Renders the confession Mari w'Alahi |
