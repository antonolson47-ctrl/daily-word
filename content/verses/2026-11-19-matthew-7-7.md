## Setting the scene

Jesus is still on the mountain in Galilee, near the close of the Sermon on the Mount. He has taught his disciples the Lord's Prayer, told them not to be anxious about tomorrow, and warned them not to judge. Now he turns to the Father's heart. "Ask, seek, knock" opens a short teaching on prayer that runs to 7:11, and he grounds it in something every family knows: "Or what man is there of you, whom if his son ask bread, will he give him a stone?… If ye then, being evil, know how to give good gifts unto your children, how much more shall your Father which is in heaven give good things to them that ask him?" (7:9-11). The very next verse is the Golden Rule (7:12). Luke records the same words just after the Lord's Prayer and the parable of the friend who knocks at midnight (Luke 11:5-13).

## Key words

- **aiteite → petite → ask.** All three commands are present imperatives, which in Greek usually means ongoing action: "keep on asking." The Holman Bible brings this out: "Keep asking… Keep searching… Keep knocking." Aiteō is the plain word for asking for something you need, the way a child asks a father. Latin peto gives us "petition."
- **dothēsetai → dabitur → it shall be given.** A future passive with no giver named. Scholars call this the "divine passive": the unspoken giver is God himself, and verse 11 says so outright: "your Father which is in heaven."
- **zēteite, heurēsete → quærite, invenietis → seek, and ye shall find.** Zēteō means to search with effort, as the shepherd searches for the lost sheep and the woman for her lost coin. Latin quæro is the root of "quest" and "query"; invenio (literally "come upon") gives us "invent," first meaning "to find."
- **krouete, anoigēsetai → pulsate, aperietur → knock, it shall be opened.** Krouō is to knock at a door with the knuckles. The same word comes back in the risen Christ's promise, "Behold, I stand at the door, and knock" (Revelation 3:20). We knock at heaven's door, and he knocks at ours. Latin pulso, to beat or strike, gives us "pulse."

## Alignment

Exact, word for word. Every Greek text on Bible Hub (Nestle 1904, Westcott–Hort with NA27/UBS4, the Byzantine Majority Text, the Greek Orthodox text, Tischendorf and both Textus Receptus editions) has the same words here, with differences only in punctuation. The Vulgate follows the Greek clause for clause, three commands and three promises, and the KJV, Douay-Rheims and ESV all match it closely. The one choice translators make is how to show the present tense: most keep the simple "Ask… seek… knock," while the Holman and Rotherham render it "keep asking" or "be asking." Luke 11:9 has the same words.

## The story

St Augustine loved this verse. He closes his Confessions, the story of his own long search for God, with these words to the Lord: "Let it be asked of You, sought in You, knocked for at You; so, even so shall it be received, so shall it be found, so shall it be opened. Amen." The man who once wrote "our heart is restless, until it rests in You" ended his book by trusting this promise. Many Christians have also noticed the little gift hidden in the English: the first letters of Ask, Seek, Knock spell ASK. It works only in English, not in the Greek or Latin, but it is a handy way to remember that the whole verse is an invitation to pray and to keep praying.

## Dig Deeper: Roots in Hebrew & Jewish Sources

"Seek, and ye shall find" echoes a promise God made to Israel long before. Moses told the people that even in exile, "if from thence thou shalt seek the LORD thy God, thou shalt find him, if thou seek him with all thy heart and with all thy soul" (Deuteronomy 4:29), and through Jeremiah the Lord said to the exiles in Babylon, "ye shall seek me, and find me, when ye shall search for me with all your heart" (Jeremiah 29:13). Isaiah calls, "Seek ye the LORD while he may be found" (55:6). The Hebrew verbs there are biqqesh and darash (דָּרַשׁ), "to seek, inquire." Darash is the word Franz Delitzsch chose for "seek" when he translated Matthew into Hebrew (שאלו וינתן לכם דרשו ותמצאו דפקו ויפתח לכם), and his word for "knock," dafaq, is the same verb as in the Song of Songs: "the voice of my beloved that knocketh" (kol dodi dofek, 5:2). Later Jewish teachers drew a lovely picture from that verse. In the midrash Song of Songs Rabbah, God says to Israel: "open for Me one opening of repentance like the eye of the needle, and I will open for you openings that wagons and carriages enter through." That midrash was compiled centuries after Christ, so it is a parallel, not a source Jesus drew on; and we know of no strong Dead Sea Scroll parallel for this verse. Dig Deeper is background study, not new Scripture, and not an endorsement of any later theology.

**A quick timeline, so the sources don't blur together**

| Source | Date | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| Deuteronomy, Isaiah, Jeremiah (Tanakh / Old Testament) | Hebrew Scriptures, written and gathered over many centuries before Christ | Seek the Lord with all your heart, and you will find him |
| Gospel of Matthew | 1st century AD | "Ask, and it shall be given you; seek, and ye shall find; knock, and it shall be opened unto you" |
| Song of Songs Rabbah (midrash) | compiled centuries after Christ, commonly dated to about the 6th century AD | A later rabbinic teaching: open to God a needle's eye of repentance, and he opens a door wide enough for wagons |
| Delitzsch Hebrew New Testament | 1877 | A modern Hebrew translation of the Gospel, using darash ("seek") and dafaq ("knock") |
