## Setting the scene

The hill country outside Bethlehem, about six miles south of Jerusalem, around 6–4 BC. Caesar Augustus has ordered a census of the whole Roman world, and Joseph has brought Mary, heavy with child, to his family town of Bethlehem, the city of David. There is no room for them in the guest room, so Mary lays her newborn son in a manger, a feeding trough for animals (Luke 2:7). Out in the fields that night, shepherds are keeping watch over their flocks. Shepherds were near the bottom of the social ladder, rough working men whose word was not always trusted. Yet they are the first to hear. An angel of the Lord appears, the glory of the Lord shines around them, and they are terrified. "Fear not," the angel says, "for, behold, I bring you good tidings of great joy" (2:10): a Savior, Christ the Lord, has been born in the city of David. Then, suddenly, the angel is joined by "a multitude of the heavenly host," literally a heavenly army, and the army does not fight. It sings.

## Key words

- **Doxa → Gloria → Glory.** Doxa in the Greek Bible carries the weight of the Hebrew kāvôd, the shining, heavy presence of God that filled the tabernacle and the temple. The shepherds have just seen it: "the glory of the Lord shone round about them" (2:9).
- **en hypsistois → in altissimis → in the highest.** Literally "in the highest places," heaven itself. The phrase echoes Psalm 148:1, "Praise him in the heights," which the Greek Psalter renders with the same words, en tois hypsistois.
- **eirēnē → pax → peace.** The Greek word stands for the Hebrew shālôm: not just the absence of war but wholeness and right relationship with God. Augustus was hailed across the empire as the bringer of the Pax Romana; Luke sets a different peace, announced by angels over a manger, beside it.
- **en anthrōpois eudokias → hominibus bonæ voluntatis → good will toward men / among those with whom he is pleased.** Eudokia means good pleasure, favor, delight. The question is whose. The ESV reads it as God's favor resting on people; Jerome's "men of good will" can be read as people who have good will themselves. Either way, the peace is a gift that comes down from heaven.
- **One letter, two readings.** The oldest manuscripts end with eudokias (genitive, "of good pleasure"). Later Byzantine manuscripts, and the Textus Receptus behind the KJV, have eudokia (nominative). That single sigma turns two lines into three: glory to God, peace on earth, good will to men.

## Alignment

Alignment Close in meaning, with a real textual difference in the last word. Greek (Nestle), Latin and ESV follow the older reading, eudokias, "peace among people of his good pleasure." The KJV follows the later eudokia, "good will toward men," which makes a three-part song. Glory, God, the highest, earth and peace line up word for word in every version. A tidbit Jerome's Vulgate says "Gloria in altissimis Deo," but the famous hymn of the Mass says "Gloria in excelsis Deo." The liturgical line comes from the older Latin translations used before Jerome, and the church kept singing it even after his revision. That is the phrase in the refrain of "Angels We Have Heard on High." The Gloria hymn itself grew from an early Greek morning hymn that starts with this angel song and is already found in a fourth-century collection of church rules, the Apostolic Constitutions. So every time a congregation sings "Gloria in excelsis," it is picking up the song the shepherds heard.

## Dig Deeper: Roots in Hebrew & Jewish Sources

Luke writes in Greek, but the angels' song is full of Hebrew ideas. "Glory" is the Greek stand-in for kāvôd, God's weighty, shining presence. "Peace" is shālôm. "In the highest" echoes the Psalms, where all heaven is called to praise God "in the heights" (Psalm 148:1, Hebrew bammᵉrômîm). And the hardest word, eudokia, "good pleasure," is the word the Greek Psalter uses for the Hebrew rāṣôn, God's favor (Psalm 51:18, Hebrew 51:20). Then came a discovery that shed light on the older reading. Among the Dead Sea Scrolls, the Thanksgiving Hymns (Hodayot) from Qumran speak of "the sons of his good pleasure" (bᵉnê rᵉṣônô), meaning the people God has chosen to favor. That is very close to Luke's "people of his good pleasure," and it tells us the phrase was a natural Jewish way of speaking in Jesus' day: the peace is for those on whom God's favor rests. These sources are background study, not Scripture, and including them is not an endorsement of any later theology. Keep the dates straight: the Psalms are Hebrew Bible (Tanakh); the Greek Psalter (Septuagint) is a Jewish translation made before Christ; the Thanksgiving Hymns were composed in the 2nd–1st century BC, and the surviving copies were made before Luke wrote; the Mishnah (about AD 200) and Talmud (about AD 400–600) come later and are not sources Luke used.

**A quick timeline, so the sources don't blur together**

| Source | Date | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| Psalms (Tanakh / Old Testament) | about 1000–400 BC | Hebrew praise "in the heights" and God's rāṣôn, favor |
| Septuagint (LXX) | 3rd–2nd century BC | Jewish Greek translation; renders rāṣôn as eudokia |
| Thanksgiving Hymns (Dead Sea Scrolls) | composed 2nd–1st century BC | "Sons of his good pleasure," a pre-Christian parallel |
| Gospel of Luke | 1st century AD | The angels' song over Bethlehem |
| Mishnah / Talmud | about AD 200 / AD 400–600 | Later rabbinic teaching, not a source Luke used |
