## Setting the scene

Jerusalem, an upper room, the night before the crucifixion, around AD 30. Jesus has just washed his disciples' feet, Judas has slipped out into the dark (John 13:30), and Jesus has told Peter he will deny him three times before the rooster crows. The disciples are shaken. Into that fear Jesus speaks the words that open John 14: "Let not your hearts be troubled... In my Father's house are many rooms... I go to prepare a place for you." He adds, "You know the way to where I am going." Thomas, honest as always, objects: "Lord, we do not know where you are going. How can we know the way?" (John 14:5). This verse is Jesus' answer. He does not hand Thomas a map. He points to himself.

## Key words

- **Egō eimi → Ego sum → I am.** The sixth of the seven great "I am" sayings in John (bread, light, door, shepherd, resurrection, way, vine). Egō is emphatic: "I myself am." Readers of the Greek Old Testament heard an echo of God's own self-naming, "I am who I am" (Exodus 3:14, egō eimi ho ōn).
- **hodos → via → the way.** A road, a path, a journey. In the Hebrew Scriptures "the way" (derekh) means a way of living as much as a route. Within a few years the first believers were simply called "the Way" (Acts 9:2; 24:14).
- **alētheia → veritas → the truth.** Literally "un-hiddenness": a (not) plus lēthē (forgetting, concealment). In John, truth is not just a correct statement but reality uncovered, God made known. Pilate will later ask, "What is truth?" (John 18:38) while Truth stands in front of him.
- **zōē → vita → the life.** Not bios (lifespan, livelihood) but zōē, the life of God himself, the word John uses for "eternal life." "In him was life, and the life was the light of men" (John 1:4).
- **oudeis erchetai...** ei mē di' emou → Nemo venit... nisi per me → no one comes... except through me. Di' emou means "through me" or "by way of me": Jesus is not only the guide who points the road, he is the road.

## Alignment

Alignment Exceptionally close. Greek, Latin, KJV and ESV line up word for word. The only differences are English punctuation and a small Greek variant: some editions (Westcott–Hort, NA27/UBS4 in brackets, and the Textus Receptus) add the article, ho Iēsous ("the Jesus"), which does not change the meaning. A tidbit Thomas à Kempis, the 15th-century monk who wrote The Imitation of Christ, built a whole chapter on this verse in Jerome's Latin: "Without the way there is no going; without the truth there is no knowing; without the life there is no living" (Book III, chapter 56). Centuries earlier Augustine put it more simply in his sermons on John: Christ is the destination as God and the road as man, so "walk by him the man, and you come to God." It is also why the earliest Christians were called followers of "the Way" before they were ever called Christians (Acts 11:26). Faith began not as a philosophy but as a road walked behind a Person.

## Dig Deeper: Roots in Hebrew & Jewish Sources

John wrote in Greek, but "way, truth and life" are three of the oldest words in the Hebrew Scriptures. "The way" is derekh, used for how a person walks with God: "Teach me your way, O LORD, that I may walk in your truth" (Psalm 86:11) puts way (derekh) and truth (ʾemet) in a single line, and "You make known to me the path of life" (Psalm 16:11) joins path and life. Later Jewish teachers called the whole body of practical law halakhah, from halakh, "to walk." Among the Dead Sea Scrolls, the Qumran community read Isaiah 40:3, "In the wilderness prepare the way of the LORD," as its own calling and spoke of its members as "those who choose the Way" in the Community Rule. That is a striking pre-Christian parallel to the early church calling itself "the Way" (Acts 9:2). And Jesus' "I am" (egō eimi) echoes how the Greek Old Testament renders God's name at the burning bush (Exodus 3:14). These sources are background study, not Scripture, and including them is not an endorsement of any later theology. Keep the dates straight: the Psalms, Exodus and Isaiah are Hebrew Bible (Tanakh); the Community Rule is pre-Christian (about 100 BC); the word halakhah belongs to later rabbinic literature (the Mishnah, about AD 200, and the Talmud, about AD 400–600), which John did not use, though it shows how deeply "walking" shaped Jewish ideas of faithfulness.

**A quick timeline, so the sources don't blur together**

| Source | Date | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| Exodus, Psalms, Isaiah (Tanakh / Old Testament) | about 1000–500 BC (traditional dating varies) | "I am," "teach me your way," "prepare the way of the LORD" |
| Community Rule (Dead Sea Scrolls) | about 100 BC | Qumran's "those who choose the Way," a pre-Christian witness |
| Gospel of John | late 1st century AD | Jesus: "I am the way, and the truth, and the life" |
| Mishnah / Talmud | about AD 200 / 400–600 | Later rabbinic halakhah, "the walking," not sources John used |
