## Setting the scene

A mountain in Galilee, some days after the resurrection. On Easter morning the angel and then Jesus himself told the women to send the brothers north: "go into Galilee; there shall they see me" (Matthew 28:7, 10). So "the eleven disciples went away into Galilee, into a mountain where Jesus had appointed them" (Matthew 28:16). When they saw him "they worshipped him: but some doubted" (Matthew 28:17). Jesus comes near and speaks: "All power is given unto me in heaven and in earth" (Matthew 28:18). This verse follows with "therefore." Earlier he had sent the Twelve only "to the lost sheep of the house of Israel" (Matthew 10:5-6); now the risen King sends them to every people. These are the last words of Matthew's Gospel, ending with "lo, I am with you alway, even unto the end of the world" (Matthew 28:20).

## Key words

- **mathēteusate → docete → teach / make disciples.** The only command in the verse. It is an aorist imperative of mathēteuō, a verb built from mathētēs, "disciple" or "learner": literally "disciple them," make learners and followers. Jerome chose docete, "teach," and the KJV followed him; the ESV's "make disciples" is closer to the Greek. Teaching gets its own word in the next verse: "teaching (didaskontes, docentes) them to observe all things" (Matthew 28:20).
- **poreuthentes → euntes → go / going.** Grammatically a participle, "having gone," that leans on the main command. It is not optional: it carries the force of the imperative, which is why nearly every translation renders it "Go."
- **panta ta ethnē → omnes gentes → all nations.** Ethnos means a people or nation; in Jewish usage "the nations" (Hebrew goyim) usually meant the Gentiles, everyone outside Israel. The same phrase appears in Matthew 24:14 and 25:32. Latin gentes gives us "Gentiles."
- **baptizontes → baptizantes → baptizing.** From baptizō, "to dip, immerse." Jerome simply carried the Greek word over into Latin, and English did the same.
- **eis to onoma → in nomine → in the name.** Greek eis means "into," so the sense is being brought into the name, into belonging to God; the Latin and English "in" is softer. Note that "name" is singular, followed by three: Father, Son and Holy Spirit.

## Alignment

Close. The Greek editions agree except for one small word: oun ("therefore") appears in Nestle 1904, Westcott–Hort and both Textus Receptus editions, but not in the Byzantine Majority Text, the Greek Orthodox text or Tischendorf. Jerome's Latin has ergo. The real difference is one verb: Jerome's docete ("teach") narrows mathēteusate ("make disciples"), and the KJV and Douay-Rheims inherit "teach"; the ESV, NASB and NIV restore "make disciples." The Latin and English "in the name" also softens the Greek "into the name," as the ASV and Young's Literal ("into the name") show.

## The story

Every surviving Greek manuscript that contains this verse has the threefold name, Father, Son and Holy Spirit. Even earlier than our oldest full copies of Matthew, the Didache, a short Christian church manual usually dated to the late 1st or early 2nd century, tells believers to "baptize into the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit" (Didache 7:1). Some scholars have pointed out that Eusebius of Caesarea, writing in the early 300s, sometimes quoted the verse in a shorter form, "make disciples of all nations in my name," but no manuscript or ancient translation reads that way. The Church has baptized with these words ever since. The scene's own detail is worth keeping too: the men given this command included some who "doubted" just one verse earlier (Matthew 28:17). Jesus sent them anyway, with his authority before the command and his presence after it.

## Dig Deeper: Roots in Hebrew & Jewish Sources

The Great Commission was spoken by a Jewish teacher to Jewish disciples, and each part of it has Hebrew roots. "Make disciples" echoes the world of the Jewish teacher and his talmidim (students); the rabbis' oldest recorded advice to teachers was "raise up many disciples." "All nations" recalls God's very first promise to Abraham, that "in thee shall all families of the earth be blessed" (Genesis 12:3), and the servant whom God would make "a light to the Gentiles (goyim), that thou mayest be my salvation unto the end of the earth" (Isaiah 49:6). Baptism, too, was not a strange new idea to a Jew: ritual immersion in water for purity was everyday practice in Second Temple Judaism, as the many stepped immersion pools (mikva'ot) found by archaeologists show, and John the Baptist had already called Israel to the water (Matthew 3:6). Later rabbinic law describes a Gentile who joins Israel being immersed and coming up "like a born Jew in every sense," a striking parallel to being brought "into the name." These links are background study tools, not new Scripture, and including them is not an endorsement of any later theology. Keep the dates straight: Genesis and Isaiah are Hebrew Bible (Tanakh), written centuries before Christ; Matthew wrote in the 1st century AD; the Mishnah, which contains Pirkei Avot, was compiled about AD 200, though it attributes "raise up many disciples" to the Men of the Great Assembly long before; and the Babylonian Talmud's conversion passage was compiled later still, around AD 500, so it shows later Jewish practice, not necessarily what the apostles knew.

**Where to dig**

1. [Genesis 12:3 in Hebrew and English, on Sefaria](https://www.sefaria.org/Genesis.12.3?lang=bi). God's promise to Abraham that "all families of the earth" will be blessed through him, the Old Testament root of "all nations."
2. [Isaiah 49:6 in Hebrew and English, on Sefaria](https://www.sefaria.org/Isaiah.49.6?lang=bi). The servant made "a light to the nations" so that salvation reaches "the end of the earth," a prophecy the apostles later quoted about their mission (Acts 13:47).
3. [Pirkei Avot 1:1, on Sefaria](https://www.sefaria.org/Pirkei_Avot.1.1?lang=bi). The opening of the Mishnah's "Ethics of the Fathers," with the saying "raise up many disciples" (talmidim) and the chain of teachers passing on Torah from Moses onward.
4. [Babylonian Talmud, Yevamot 47b, on Sefaria](https://www.sefaria.org/Yevamot.47b?lang=bi). The later rabbinic description of a convert's teaching and immersion, after which he is "like a born Jew in every sense," useful background on immersion as entry into a people.
