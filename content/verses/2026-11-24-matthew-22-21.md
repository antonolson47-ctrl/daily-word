## Setting the scene

It is the last week before the cross, and the Temple courts have become a battlefield of words. Jesus has just told the parable of the wedding feast, and his enemies decide to trap him in his speech. They form an alliance that would normally be unthinkable: disciples of the Pharisees, who resented Roman rule, and the Herodians, who owed their power to it (22:15-16). They come with flattery, "Master, we know that thou art true," and then spring the trap: "Is it lawful to give tribute unto Caesar, or not?" (22:17). The tax in question was the hated Roman census tax, paid in Roman silver. If Jesus says "Yes," the crowds who long for freedom will turn against him. If he says "No," the Herodians will report him to Pilate as a rebel. Jesus sees straight through it: "Why tempt ye me, ye hypocrites? Shew me the tribute money." Someone produces a denarius, right there in the courts of God's house, and Jesus asks a simple question: "Whose is this image and superscription?" (22:20). Then comes today's verse, an answer so wise that his opponents "marvelled, and left him, and went their way" (22:22). In one sentence, Jesus gives Caesar his coin and reclaims for God everything that bears God's image.

## Key words

- **Kaisaros → Caesaris → Caesar's.** The answer comes out of the questioners' own mouths. The coin bears Caesar's face and name, so by their own admission it belongs to his order of things. In Jesus' day the Caesar was Tiberius, who reigned AD 14-37.
- **apodote → reddite → render, give back.** The questioners asked whether it was lawful "to give" (dounai, 22:17). Jesus answers with a different verb: apodidōmi, "to give back, to pay what is owed, to return." Jerome's reddite (re-dare, "give again") keeps the same sense. Tribute is not a gift but a debt, and the word quietly raises the question of what else we owe, and to whom.
- **oun → ergo → therefore.** The verdict follows from the evidence. Since the coin carries Caesar's image, give it back to him.
- **ta Kaisaros Kaisari → quae sunt Caesaris, Caesari → the things that are Caesar's, to Caesar.** Jesus does recognise a real but limited sphere of earthly authority. St. Paul echoes it: "Render therefore to all their dues: tribute to whom tribute is due" (Romans 13:7).
- **kai ta tou Theou tō Theō → et quae sunt Dei, Deo → and to God the things that are God's.** The second half is the knockout. Jesus had just asked, "Whose is this image (eikōn, imago)?" Every listener knew Genesis: God made man "in his own image" (Genesis 1:27). If the coin belongs to Caesar because it bears his image, then the person holding the coin belongs to God, wholly and completely. Caesar may have the silver; God claims the heart.

## Alignment

Very close. Every Greek text on Bible Hub agrees on the saying itself, Ἀπόδοτε οὖν τὰ Καίσαρος Καίσαρι καὶ τὰ τοῦ θεοῦ τῷ θεῷ. The only difference is in the little introduction: the Byzantine Majority Text, the Greek Orthodox text and both Textus Receptus editions read λέγουσιν αὐτῷ ("they say unto him"), NA27/UBS4 print αὐτῷ in brackets, and Nestle 1904, Westcott–Hort and Tischendorf omit it. The KJV's "They say unto him" follows the Textus Receptus; the ESV's "They said" follows the shorter text. The Vulgate is word for word and includes ei ("to him"): dicunt ei, reddite ergo quae sunt Caesaris Caesari. The KJV's "render" carries the Latin reddite straight into English. The Aramaic Peshitta likewise says simply, "give, therefore, what is Caesar's to Caesar, and what is God's to God."

## The story

Coin collectors still call it "the tribute penny." The KJV translates denarion as "penny," and the coin most scholars believe Jesus held up is the silver denarius of Tiberius, one of the most common silver coins of his reign. On the front is the emperor's laurelled head with the inscription TI CAESAR DIVI AVG F AVGVSTVS, "Tiberius Caesar Augustus, son of the divine Augustus." On the back sits a seated woman, often identified as his mother Livia, with the words PONTIF MAXIM, "high priest." Picture the scene: in the very courts of the Lord's Temple, a coin is lifted up proclaiming a man as the son of a god and high priest. Jesus does not shout or riot. He simply says, give the man back his metal, and give God what is truly His. The true Son of God and the true High Priest was standing right there, and within days he would offer the only sacrifice that could ransom the image of God in every human heart.

## Dig Deeper: Roots in Hebrew & Jewish Sources

Today's verse lands in the middle of a burning question for Jesus' own people: how should God's covenant nation live under a pagan empire? When Rome imposed direct rule and a census on Judea in AD 6, the Jewish historian Josephus reports that Judas the Galilean led a revolt, teaching that paying tribute to Rome meant accepting a mortal master besides God (Antiquities 18.1.1; Jewish War 2.8.1). That memory made the Pharisees' trap question explosive. But the Hebrew Scriptures held another voice too: when Israel was exiled to Babylon, the prophet Jeremiah told the people to "seek the peace of the city ... and pray unto the LORD for it" (Jeremiah 29:7), living faithfully under foreign rule without giving their worship to it. Jesus' answer goes deeper than both. His question, "Whose image?", reaches back to the first chapter of the Torah, where the Hebrew word tzelem, "image," marks every human being as God's own. Later Jewish tradition also weighed civic duty: the Mishnah, compiled around AD 200, preserves the saying of Rabbi Hanina, the deputy high priest from the last days of the Temple, "Pray for the welfare of the government." That saying is later than the Gospels in its written form and is background, not a source Matthew used. No Dead Sea Scrolls text speaks directly to this verse, so none is listed here. Dig Deeper is background study, not new Scripture, and not an endorsement of any later theology.
