## Setting the scene

It is Thursday night in Jerusalem, the night Jesus will be betrayed. Supper is over in the upper room. Jesus has washed the disciples' feet, Judas has gone out into the dark (John 13:30), and Jesus has given the eleven a new commandment: "love one another; as I have loved you" (13:34). Now, perhaps on the walk out toward the Kidron valley and Gethsemane (14:31), he speaks of the vine and the branches: "Abide in me." Then he returns to love. "This is my commandment, That ye love one another, as I have loved you" (15:12). The next verse tells them how much he has loved them. Within a day these words will be fulfilled on a Roman cross outside the city wall.

## Key words

- **agapē → dilectio → love.** The self-giving love John uses for God's love for the world (3:16) and for the new commandment (13:34). Jerome chose dilectio, from diligo, "to choose, to prize," rather than amor; the love here is a decision and a gift, not only a feeling.
- **meizona … oudeis echei → majorem … nemo habet → greater … hath no man.** A comparison with no rival: "no one has a love greater than this." Jesus is not ranking kinds of love so much as naming the summit.
- **psychē → anima → life.** The same word as "soul," translating the Hebrew nefesh: the whole living self. Jesus used the same phrase of himself as the Good Shepherd: "the good shepherd giveth his life [psychēn] for the sheep" (10:11).
- **thē → ponat → lay down.** From tithēmi, "to place, set down." John uses it for Jesus laying aside his garments to wash feet (13:4) and for the shepherd laying down his life (10:11, 17-18). It is a deliberate act: "No man taketh it from me, but I lay it down of myself" (10:18).
- **hyper → pro → for.** "On behalf of, in place of." Paul uses the same word: "Christ died for [hyper] us" (Romans 5:8).
- **philōn → amicis → friends.** Philos is from phileō, the love of warm affection. The next verses explain: "Ye are my friends, if ye do whatsoever I command you. Henceforth I call you not servants … but I have called you friends" (15:14-15).

## Alignment

Very close across Greek, Latin and English. Nestle 1904, Westcott–Hort, the Byzantine Majority Text, the Greek Orthodox text and both Textus Receptus editions read the same words; Tischendorf alone omits the small word tis ("someone"), which does not change the sense. Jerome follows the Greek closely, with the indefinite quis ("anyone") for tis; some online printings of the Vulgate show qui, a slip for quis. The KJV's "no man … a man" and the ESV's "no one … someone" both render the same Greek, the ESV simply choosing gender-neutral wording. Tyndale's 1526 phrasing, "Greater love then this hath no man," was kept almost unchanged by the KJV translators.

## The story

This verse is carved on countless war memorials across the English-speaking world. After the First World War, towns and villages in Britain, Canada, Australia and New Zealand chose "Greater love hath no man than this" for the stone crosses and plaques bearing their dead's names, and it is still read on Remembrance Day and Veterans Day, which fell yesterday, November 11. The verse is also tied to Maximilian Kolbe, the Polish Franciscan priest who in August 1941 at Auschwitz volunteered to take the place of Franciszek Gajowniczek, a stranger and a husband and father, in a starvation bunker. Gajowniczek survived the war, lived to 1995, and was present when Pope John Paul II canonized Kolbe in 1982. Yet the verse's first fulfillment came the day after Jesus spoke it, when he laid down his life not only for friends but, as Paul says, "while we were yet sinners" (Romans 5:8).

## Dig Deeper: Roots in Hebrew & Jewish Sources

Jesus speaks these words in the upper room on the night before he dies, and two threads from the Hebrew Scriptures run underneath them. The first is friendship with God. Abraham is called God's "friend" (Isaiah 41:8; 2 Chronicles 20:7), and "the LORD spake unto Moses face to face, as a man speaketh unto his friend" (Exodus 33:11). Two verses after this one Jesus tells the disciples, "I have called you friends" (John 15:15), placing them in that line. The second thread is the Hebrew word nefesh, "soul" or "life," which stands behind the Greek psychē here. The Greek Old Testament regularly translates nefesh with psychē, and the Scriptures use the phrase "to put one's life in one's hand" for risking everything (Judges 12:3; 1 Samuel 19:5). The great picture of friendship-love is Jonathan, who "loved him [David] as his own soul" (1 Samuel 18:1) and stripped off his own robe and sword to give them to David. Much later, the Babylonian Talmud (compiled about AD 500) records a famous rabbinic debate about two travelers with one jug of water: Ben Petora said both should drink and die together, while Rabbi Akiva ruled that "your life comes first" (Bava Metzia 62a). Set beside that honest question, the Gospel's claim is striking: Jesus does not only teach that laying down one's life is the greatest love; the next day he does it. There is no strong Dead Sea Scrolls parallel for this verse, so none is listed. Dig Deeper here is background study, not new Scripture, and not an endorsement of any later theology.

**Where to dig**

1. [1 Samuel 18 in Hebrew and English, on Sefaria](https://www.sefaria.org/I_Samuel.18?lang=bi). The covenant friendship of Jonathan and David, where Jonathan "loved him as his own soul" (nefesh) and gives David his robe and weapons.
2. [Isaiah 41 in Hebrew and English, on Sefaria](https://www.sefaria.org/Isaiah.41?lang=bi). God names Abraham "my friend" (verse 8), the Old Testament root of Jesus calling his disciples friends.
3. [Bava Metzia 62a in the Babylonian Talmud, on Sefaria](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Metzia.62a?lang=bi). The later rabbinic debate between Ben Petora and Rabbi Akiva about two travelers who share one jug of water.
4. [Blue Letter Bible lexicon for φίλος (philos)](https://www.blueletterbible.org/lexicon/g5384/kjv/tr/0-1/). Every New Testament use of the word "friend," from the "friend of sinners" to "Abraham … the Friend of God" (James 2:23).
